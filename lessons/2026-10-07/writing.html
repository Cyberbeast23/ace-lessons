✍️ Writing Week 10: A Scientific Lab Report: The Pulse Experiment

Yesterday you persuaded the Roman Senate. Today, Ace, you'll write like a scientist. Scientists don't just say what they think; they test it and then write a lab report so anyone in the world can repeat the experiment and check the results. You'll learn the six parts of a lab report, how to write a testable hypothesis ("If... then... because..."), and how to write a procedure with clear, numbered steps. Then you'll run a real experiment on your own heart!

Part 1: The Lesson

A lab report has six parts

William Harvey wrote about his experiments so carefully that other doctors could repeat them. Scientists still follow the same plan:

1. Question: what are you trying to find out?
2. Hypothesis: your prediction, written as If... then... because...
3. Materials: everything you need
4. Procedure: numbered steps that start with command verbs
5. Results: what happened, with numbers (often in a table)
6. Conclusion: was your hypothesis supported? Why?

Five scientist writing moves

  • A testable hypothesis: it must be something you can measure. "If I do jumping jacks for one minute, then my heart rate will increase, because my muscles will need more oxygen."
  • Command verbs in the procedure: start each step with an action word, like a recipe. "Sit quietly for two minutes. Count your pulse for 15 seconds. Multiply by 4."
  • Exact numbers and metric units: not "a while" but "for 60 seconds"; not "a lot faster" but "from 76 bpm to 132 bpm."
  • Fair test: change only one thing (the variable) and keep everything else the same.
  • An honest conclusion: say whether the hypothesis was supported or not supported, use your data as evidence, and explain why. A hypothesis that turns out wrong is still good science!

Useful science linking words: first, next, then, after, finally, as a result, this shows that, therefore, however.

Model Lab Report (short version)

Question + hypothesisMaterials + procedureResultsConclusion

Question: How does one minute of jumping jacks change my heart rate? Hypothesis: If I do jumping jacks for one minute, then my heart rate will increase by at least 30 bpm, because my muscles will need more oxygen-rich blood.
Materials: a stopwatch, a pencil, and a data table. Procedure: 1. Sit quietly for two minutes. 2. Find your pulse on your wrist and count the beats for 15 seconds. 3. Multiply by 4 and record your resting heart rate. 4. Do jumping jacks for 60 seconds. 5. Count your pulse again right away and record it.
Results: My resting heart rate was 76 bpm. Right after the jumping jacks, it was 132 bpm, an increase of 56 bpm (about 74%).
Conclusion: My hypothesis was supported, because my heart rate rose by 56 bpm, which is more than 30 bpm. This shows that exercise makes the heart pump faster to deliver more oxygen to working muscles. Next time, I could repeat the test three times and find the mean to make my results more reliable.

Notice: The hypothesis can be measured, every procedure step starts with a command verb, the results use exact numbers, and the conclusion uses the data as evidence.
