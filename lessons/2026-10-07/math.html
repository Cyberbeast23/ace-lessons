❤️ Math Week 10: Heart & Blood Circulation Lab

Put on your lab coat, Ace! Today you'll think like a cardiologist (a heart doctor). You'll level up with unit rates from pulse counts, cardiac output (litres per minute), proportions, percent increase and decrease, working backwards from a percent, reading a heart-rate line graph (rate of change and estimating between points), algebraic formulas, and an equation with brackets — the same maths doctors, nurses, and sports scientists use every day.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your doctor's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right unit?

Worked Example 1: From a pulse count to a unit rate

A unit rate tells you how much happens in one unit of something, like beats per one minute (bpm).

Nurses often count a pulse for 15 seconds. There are four 15-second blocks in a minute, so they multiply by 4.

Example: 22 beats in 15 seconds → 22 × 4 = 88 bpm.

Counted for 20 seconds instead? 60 ÷ 20 = 3, so multiply by 3. Counted for 12 seconds? 60 ÷ 12 = 5, so multiply by 5.

Worked Example 2: Cardiac output (how much blood the heart pumps)

cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. Stroke volume is the amount of blood pushed out in one beat.

Heart rate 80 bpm, stroke volume 65 mL: 80 × 65 = 5200 mL per minute.

Change to litres: 1 L = 1000 mL, so 5200 mL = 5.2 L per minute.

Worked Example 3: Percent increase, percent decrease, and working backwards

Percent change = change ÷ starting amount × 100. From 60 bpm to 90 bpm: change 30, so 30 ÷ 60 × 100 = 50% increase. From 90 down to 72: change 18, so 18 ÷ 90 × 100 = 20% decrease.

Working backwards: "After a 25% increase, the heart rate was 100 bpm. What was it before?" The new amount is 100% + 25% = 125% of the old one, so old = 100 ÷ 1.25 = 80 bpm. (Not 100 − 25!)

Worked Example 4: Reading a line graph

Rate of change between two points = change in the y-value ÷ change in the x-value. If heart rate goes from 90 bpm at 3 min to 120 bpm at 5 min, it rose 30 bpm in 2 min, so 30 ÷ 2 = 15 bpm per minute. The steepest part of the line is where the rate of change is biggest.

Estimating between points: if the line is straight from 90 (at 3 min) to 120 (at 5 min), then halfway, at 4 min, the value is halfway between: (90 + 120) ÷ 2 = 105 bpm.

Worked Example 5: Formulas and equations with brackets

Sports coaches estimate an adult's maximum heart rate with the formula 220 − a, where a is age in years. For a 30-year-old: 220 − 30 = 190 bpm. (It's only an estimate, and it isn't used for kids.)

Equation with brackets: 5(12 − x) + 9x = 76. Expand: 60 − 5x + 9x = 76 → 60 + 4x = 76 → 4x = 16 → x = 4. Check: 5 × 8 + 9 × 4 = 40 + 36 = 76 ✔
