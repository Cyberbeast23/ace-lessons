The Doctor Who Followed the Blood

Every science textbook today explains that blood travels in a loop: out of the heart, around the body, and back again. It seems obvious now. Yet for most of human history, even the smartest doctors believed something completely different. The story of how people discovered the truth is really a story about how science works: someone notices that an old idea does not fit the evidence, tests it carefully, and is brave enough to say so.

About 1800 years ago, a Greek doctor named Galen worked in the Roman Empire, first treating injured gladiators and later caring for emperors. Galen was a brilliant observer, and his books were copied and studied for about 1400 years. He taught that the liver turned food into fresh blood and that the body used blood up the way a fire uses up wood. He also believed that blood seeped from the right side of the heart to the left side through tiny invisible holes in the wall between them, called the septum. Because Galen was so respected, few doctors dared to question him.

A few did. In the 1200s, a physician named Ibn al-Nafis, working in Damascus and Cairo, wrote that the septum was thick and solid, with no holes at all. Blood, he argued, must travel from the right side of the heart to the lungs, mix with air there, and only then return to the left side. He was right, but his work was written in Arabic and remained almost unknown in Europe for centuries.

The person who finally put the whole puzzle together was William Harvey, an English doctor born in 1578. Harvey had studied in Padua, Italy, where his teacher had discovered small flaps, called valves, inside veins. Harvey wondered why veins would need valves. Instead of simply trusting old books, he dissected animals, watched their hearts beat, and designed experiments. In one ingenious test, he tied a band tightly around a person's upper arm so that the veins below it swelled up. When he pressed on a vein and tried to push the blood away from the heart, toward the hand, it would not budge, because the valves blocked it. When he pushed the blood toward the heart, it flowed easily. Blood in the veins, he reasoned, travels in only one direction: back to the heart.

Harvey also used mathematics. He estimated how much blood the heart pushes out with each beat and multiplied by the number of beats in an hour. The answer was staggering: in a single hour, the heart pumped more blood than the entire mass of a grown man. Nobody could possibly eat enough food to make that much new blood. These numbers seemed to contradict everything Galen had taught. Harvey reached a bold conclusion: the body does not use up its blood. Instead, the same blood must circulate, pumped out through the arteries and returned through the veins, over and over again. In 1628, he published his ideas in a short book whose Latin title means "On the Motion of the Heart and Blood."

Not everyone cheered. Some doctors chose to ridicule Harvey, calling his ideas foolish, and according to one writer who knew him, many of his patients left him. Harvey also could not answer one important question: how did blood get from the smallest arteries into the smallest veins? He predicted that connections must exist, even though no one could see them. In 1661, four years after Harvey's death, an Italian scientist named Marcello Malpighi looked at a frog's lung through an early microscope and finally saw them: microscopic tubes, thinner than a hair, linking arteries to veins. Today we call them capillaries, from the Latin capillus, meaning "hair."

We now know that blood follows two loops, both powered by the heart. The short pulmonary loop (from the Latin pulmo, "lung") carries blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen. The long systemic loop carries that oxygen-rich blood to every other part of the body. Doctors who specialize in the heart are called cardiologists, from the Greek kardia, "heart," and -logist, "one who studies." Every time a cardiologist checks a pulse or listens through a stethoscope, they rely on Harvey's discovery. Perhaps his greatest gift, though, was not a fact about blood but a way of thinking: test ideas with careful observation and measurement, and follow the evidence, even when it disagrees with famous experts.
