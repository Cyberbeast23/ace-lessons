Yesterday you wrote like an entrepreneur. Today, Ace, you'll write like a poet who is also a scientist. Scientists describe the world with exact facts; poets describe it with vivid pictures and the music of words. A great science poem does both: every image is beautiful and true. Today you'll write a free-verse poem (a poem that doesn't need to rhyme) about a tree, a leaf, a seed, or a nurse log, packed with real photosynthesis and rainforest science.

Part 1: The Lesson

Six tools in a poet's toolbox 🧰

Poems are short, so every word must work hard. Poets use these tools to help a reader see, hear and feel an idea.

1. Simile: compares two different things using like or as ("Roots spread like a hidden city's streets.")
2. Metaphor: says one thing is another, with no like or as ("The canopy is a green umbrella.")
3. Personification: gives a non-human thing human actions or feelings ("The old cedar whispers to its neighbours.")
4. Alliteration: repeats the same first sound in nearby words ("Silent, sun-soaked, sugar-making leaves")
5. Onomatopoeia: a word that sounds like what it means (drip, crack, swish, thud)
6. Science fact: a true, exact detail woven into the poem ("I drink carbon dioxide and breathe out oxygen.")

Five poet's moves

  • Break lines on purpose: end a line on a strong word so the reader pauses there.
  • Group lines into stanzas: a stanza is a poem's "paragraph." Start a new one when the idea, time or place changes.
  • Choose precise words: not "a big tree" but "a 70-metre Sitka spruce"; not "goes" but "creeps," "surges," or "trickles."
  • Pick a point of view: try writing as the tree itself ("I"), which makes personification easy.
  • End with a twist or an echo: repeat your first line in a new way, or end with a surprising thought.

Science-poem rule: beautiful and accurate. If a line says something false (like "plants eat soil"), fix the science, then make it beautiful again.

Model Science Poem (short version): "I Am the Cedar"

Personification + science factSimile + alliterationMetaphor + onomatopoeiaEnding with an echo

I am the cedar. I have stood here for five hundred years,
sipping rain through my roots,
drinking the carbon dioxide you breathe out.
My needles catch sunlight like a million tiny solar panels,
silent, sun-soaked, sugar-making.
My canopy is a green roof over the forest.
Drip. Drip. The rain slides down my bark
to the fungi that trade me water for sugar.
One day I will fall with a thunderous crack,
and seedlings will grow from my body.
I am the cedar. I am still giving.

Notice: the poem is told by the tree ("I"); every image is also a true science fact; the stanzas change as the idea changes (roots, leaves, forest, death and new life); and the last stanza echoes the first line with a twist.
