Green Machines: How the Rainforest Makes Its Own Food

You cannot make lunch out of sunshine and air. A plant can. Every green leaf is a tiny factory that runs on light, and the process it uses, called photosynthesis, feeds almost every living thing on Earth. The cereal in your bowl, the salmon in a BC river, and even the oxygen in your next breath all trace back to a leaf somewhere catching the sun.

Here is how the factory works. Tiny holes on the underside of a leaf, called stomata, let in carbon dioxide gas from the air. At the same time, roots absorb water from the soil, and thin tubes carry it all the way up the stem or trunk to the leaves. Inside each leaf cell are small green parts called chloroplasts. They are packed with chlorophyll, a green chemical that captures the energy in sunlight.

Using that energy, the chloroplasts pull apart the water and carbon dioxide and put their atoms back together in a new way. The result is glucose, a sugar the plant uses for energy and to build new wood, leaves and roots. The leftover oxygen escapes through the stomata into the air. Scientists sum it up in one line: carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen. In other words, a plant breathes in what we breathe out, and breathes out what we need.

Nowhere in Canada does this factory work harder than in the coastal temperate rainforest of British Columbia. Mild winters and as much as 4 metres of rain a year allow giants such as western redcedar, Sitka spruce and Douglas fir to grow for centuries. Some are taller than a 20-storey building. High above the ground, their branches form a thick canopy that soaks up most of the sunlight. Down on the dim forest floor, ferns, mosses and young trees must make do with the light that slips through the gaps.

A forest is not just a crowd of trees standing alone. Under the soil, the roots of many trees are wrapped in threads of fungi. The fungi help the roots collect water and nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, that plants need to stay healthy. In return, the trees feed the fungi some of their sugar. This partnership is symbiotic: both living things benefit. Forest scientists, including researchers at the University of British Columbia, have found evidence that these fungal threads can connect neighbouring trees, and that some sugar and chemical signals may travel through them from one tree to another.

Even when a giant tree falls, its work is not finished. Insects, fungi and bacteria slowly decompose the trunk, breaking the wood down into rich soil. As it rots, the log becomes a "nurse log." Seeds that land on its soft, damp surface sprout and grow, and years later you may see a row of young hemlocks standing on stilt-like roots where the old log used to be.

These forests do more than grow wood. By pulling carbon dioxide out of the air and storing the carbon in their trunks, roots and soil, old forests help slow climate change. They also sustain bears, eagles, salmon and countless smaller creatures, and they have supported First Nations communities for thousands of years. That is why many people work to protect old-growth forests and to replant areas after logging and wildfires.

So the next time you walk under a tall tree, look up. Each of its thousands of needles or leaves is quietly turning light, water and air into food, and giving you a fresh breath of oxygen in return.
