🌲 Math Week 36: Green Machines: Rainforest Botanist's Math Lab

Ace, every leaf is a tiny solar-powered factory. Using sunlight, a plant turns carbon dioxide and water into sugar and gives off the oxygen we breathe. British Columbia's coastal rainforest holds some of the tallest and oldest trees on Earth, and botanists and foresters use math every day to study them. Today you'll work like a rainforest botanist with counting atoms in the photosynthesis equation, growth rates, measuring a tree's height with shadows (similar triangles), tree-ring ages, circumference and π, percent water in a leaf, carbon dioxide and rounding up, litres to cubic metres, the mean from a bar graph, germination rates, ratios, hectares and tree planting, an equation with the variable on both sides, doubling patterns, and unit rates.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your botanist's field checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (cm, m, mm, L, ha, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? If one length is in cm and another in m, convert first.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A tree can be 70 m tall, but not 700 m!

Worked Example 1: Counting atoms in a chemical equation

Photosynthesis: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ (carbon dioxide + water → glucose sugar + oxygen). The big number in front multiplies the whole molecule; the small number counts atoms of the element just before it.

Carbon atoms on the left: 6 CO₂ has 6 × 1 = 6. Hydrogen on the left: 6 H₂O has 6 × 2 = 12. Glucose on the right, C₆H₁₂O₆, also has 6 carbons and 12 hydrogens. Atoms are never created or destroyed, so both sides must match!

Worked Example 2: Growth at a steady rate

Final height = start + rate × time. Convert first if the units differ.

A sapling starts at 0.5 m and grows 30 cm (0.3 m) per year for 5 years: 0.5 + 0.3 × 5 = 2 m.

Worked Example 3: Measuring a tall tree with shadows

At the same moment, the sun makes every object's height and shadow length have the same ratio (they form similar triangles).

A 2 m pole casts a 1 m shadow, so height is 2 × the shadow. A tree with a 15 m shadow is 15 × 2 = 30 m tall.

Worked Example 4: Tree rings

Most trees in BC add one growth ring each year. Age ≈ radius ÷ average ring width (same units!).

Radius 30 cm = 300 mm, rings about 3 mm wide: 300 ÷ 3 = about 100 years old.

Worked Example 5: Circumference and π

Circumference = π × diameter, so diameter = circumference ÷ π. Use π ≈ 3.14.

A trunk measures 6.28 m around with a tape: diameter = 6.28 ÷ 3.14 = 2 m.

Worked Example 6: When to round UP

If you need enough of something, a decimal answer must be rounded up to the next whole number, even if the decimal is small.

You need 100 L of water and each bucket holds 30 L: 100 ÷ 30 = 3.33..., so you need 4 buckets (3 buckets would only hold 90 L).

Worked Example 7: Litres, cubic metres and hectares

1000 L = 1 m³ (a cube 1 m on each side holds 1000 L). Land area: 1 hectare (ha) = 10 000 m², about the size of two soccer fields.

2500 L = 2500 ÷ 1000 = 2.5 m³. 3 ha = 3 × 10 000 = 30 000 m².

Worked Example 8: Germination rate (a percent)

Germination rate = seeds that sprouted ÷ seeds planted × 100. To compare trays with different numbers of seeds, always compare the percents, not the counts.

18 of 20 seeds sprouted: 18 ÷ 20 = 0.9 = 90%.

Worked Example 9: Doubling patterns

If something doubles every day, then one day before it was half as big. Work backward instead of forward!

Mould covers a whole slice of bread on day 10 and doubles daily, so it covered half on day 9 and a quarter on day 8.

Worked Example 10: Variables on both sides

Two plants: one is 10 cm and grows 5 cm a week, the other is 30 cm and grows 1 cm a week. When are they equal? 10 + 5x = 30 + x → 4x = 20 → x = 5 weeks (both are 35 cm).
