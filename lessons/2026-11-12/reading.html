Food's Fantastic Voyage: The Nine-Metre Journey Through Your Digestive System

Picture a crunchy apple slice. In a day or two, the useful parts of that slice will have become part of you, helping build your muscles, power your brain, and keep your heart beating. To get there, the apple must travel through a twisting tube about nine metres long in an adult, longer than a city bus. This tube, together with helpers like the liver and pancreas, is your digestive system. Its job is to break food into pieces so tiny that they can slip into your blood.

The journey begins before the food even reaches your mouth. Just smelling a fresh pizza can make your mouth water. That water is saliva, and it is more than spit. As soon as you take a bite, your front teeth slice the food and your molars grind it, while saliva wets it and starts to break it down chemically. Saliva contains enzymes, special proteins that speed up chemical reactions. One enzyme starts turning starch into sugar, which is why a cracker begins to taste slightly sweet if you chew it long enough. Finally, your tongue shapes the mushy food into a soft ball and pushes it to the back of your throat.

Next, you swallow. A small flap called the epiglottis closes over your windpipe so the food does not go down the wrong way. The food then enters the esophagus, a muscular tube about 25 centimetres long. The esophagus does not simply let food fall. Instead, rings of muscle squeeze one after another, like hands pushing toothpaste along a tube. This wave of squeezing is called peristalsis, and it is so strong that astronauts can swallow in space and you could even swallow while standing on your head (though it is not recommended).

After about eight seconds, the food arrives in the stomach, a stretchy, J-shaped bag. The stomach's muscular walls churn the food, mixing and mashing it for two to four hours. Meanwhile, the stomach lining releases acid strong enough to dissolve some metals and kill most germs. Why doesn't the stomach digest itself? It coats its walls with thick mucus and replaces its lining cells every few days. By the time the stomach is done, the food has become a thick, soupy liquid called chyme. A ring of muscle at the bottom of the stomach opens and lets a few spoonfuls of chyme through at a time.

Much of the real work happens in the next stop, the small intestine. It is called "small" because it is narrow, but it is about six metres long, coiled tightly like a garden hose. Here, bile from the liver breaks fat into tiny droplets, much as dish soap breaks up grease, and juices from the pancreas finish breaking down proteins, fats and carbohydrates. The inside wall is covered with millions of tiny, finger-like bumps called villi. Because of these folds and bumps, the inner surface of the small intestine is roughly the size of a small studio apartment. The villi absorb sugars, amino acids, fats, vitamins and minerals, passing them into the blood, which carries them to every cell in your body.

Whatever the body cannot digest, such as much of the fibre from fruit, vegetables and whole grains, moves on to the large intestine. This wider tube, about one and a half metres long, soaks up most of the remaining water. It is also home to trillions of microbes, mostly bacteria, that live in your gut. Far from being harmful, most of these tiny partners help you. They feast on fibre, make some vitamins, such as vitamin K, and help train your immune system. Eventually, what is left, including water, fibre, worn-out cells and bacteria, leaves your body as waste. The whole trip usually takes between one and three days.

For a long time, nobody knew exactly what happened inside a working stomach. Then, in 1822, a young French-Canadian voyageur named Alexis St. Martin was accidentally shot at a fur-trading post on Mackinac Island. He survived, but the wound healed with a small opening into his stomach. An army doctor, William Beaumont, spent years dropping bits of food on strings through the opening and pulling them out to see how quickly they changed. His experiments showed that digestion in the stomach is a chemical process, not just mashing.

Today, scientists know that what we eat and how we eat both matter. Chewing slowly gives your saliva time to work. Drinking water keeps food moving smoothly, and fibre feeds the helpful microbes in your large intestine. Your digestive system works every day without you thinking about it. The least you can do is give it good food to work with!
