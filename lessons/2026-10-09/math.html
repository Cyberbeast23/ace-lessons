Agent Ace, your mission today is to think like a cryptographer (someone who makes codes) and a codebreaker (someone who cracks them). Secret codes are built out of maths, from Julius Caesar's letter shifts to the prime numbers that protect every online bank account today. You'll level up with clock arithmetic and remainders, negative numbers on a wrap-around wheel, prime factorization, LCM and GCF, binary numbers and powers of 2, counting the number of possible passwords, arrangements, ratios and percents, a frequency bar graph, and equations with brackets.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your codebreaker's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right unit?

Worked Example 1: Caesar shifts and clock arithmetic

Number the letters A = 0, B = 1, C = 2, … Z = 25. A Caesar cipher adds the same shift to every letter.

Shift 3: C (2) becomes 2 + 3 = 5, which is F.

The alphabet wraps around like a clock. X (23) + 3 = 26, but there is no letter 26, so subtract 26: 26 − 26 = 0, which is A. Mathematicians call this modular arithmetic: you keep only the remainder after dividing by 26.

To decode, subtract the shift. If the answer is negative, add 26: coded B (1) with shift 3 is 1 − 3 = −2, and −2 + 26 = 24, which is Y.

Worked Example 2: Primes and prime factorization

A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, …).

Every whole number greater than 1 can be broken into primes. Use a factor tree: 84 = 2 × 42 = 2 × 2 × 21 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7. Multiplying primes is easy, but going backwards with huge numbers is so hard that computers use it to protect secrets!

Worked Example 3: LCM and GCF

LCM (lowest common multiple): two code wheels turn once every 4 s and every 6 s. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12… Multiples of 6: 6, 12… They line up again after 12 s.

GCF (greatest common factor): to split 24 red cards and 36 blue cards into identical packs, find the biggest number that divides both: 12 packs (each with 2 red and 3 blue).

Worked Example 4: Binary numbers and powers of 2

Computers store every message using only 0 and 1. In binary, the place values are powers of 2: … 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.

1011 in binary = 1 × 8 + 0 × 4 + 1 × 2 + 1 × 1 = 11. To go the other way, take away the biggest power of 2 you can: 13 = 8 + 4 + 1 = 1101.

With 3 binary digits (bits) you can make 2 × 2 × 2 = 23 = 8 different patterns.

Worked Example 5: Counting passwords, arrangements, and equations

Counting principle: multiply the number of choices for each spot. A 3-digit lock (0–9 on each dial) has 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 combinations.

Arrangements: the letters of SPY can be ordered in 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 ways (SPY, SYP, PSY, PYS, YSP, YPS).

Equation with brackets: 3(2x + 1) = 45. Divide both sides by 3: 2x + 1 = 15 → 2x = 14 → x = 7. Check: 3 × (14 + 1) = 45 ✔
