✍️ Writing Week 12: How-To Guide: Make and Crack a Secret Code

Yesterday you wrote like a news reporter. Today, Ace, you'll write like an instruction writer. A how-to guide (also called procedural writing) teaches someone to do something they've never done before, using only your words. If one step is missing or out of order, the reader gets stuck, or their secret code doesn't work! You'll learn how to write clear command steps, a precise materials list, and helpful tips and warnings. Then you'll write your own guide to a secret code.

Part 1: The Lesson

A how-to guide has five parts 🛠️

Readers follow a how-to guide from top to bottom, doing each step as they read it:

1. Title: tells exactly what readers will make or do, often starting with "How to"
2. Introduction: one or two sentences on what it is, why it's useful, and how long it takes
3. Materials: everything the reader needs, gathered before they start
4. Steps: numbered, in the exact order, each beginning with a command verb
5. Tips, warnings + conclusion: help readers avoid mistakes and check that it worked

Five instruction-writer moves

  • Start each step with a command verb (the imperative): Cut, Write, Turn, Line up, Count. Not "You should maybe cut..."
  • One action per step, in the exact order: if the reader must do two things, make two steps.
  • Be precise: give exact amounts and sizes in metric units. "Cut a circle 10 cm across" is better than "cut a smallish circle."
  • Use sequence words with a comma after them: First, Next, After that, When the ink is dry, Finally,
  • Talk to the reader and warn them early: use "you," and put a warning before the step where the mistake could happen. "Be careful: write the letters the same size, or the slices won't line up."

Grown-up word: lists of steps should be parallel: every item has the same grammar shape (count the letters, find the most common one, guess the shift).

Model How-To Guide (short version)

Title + introductionMaterialsStepsTips + conclusion

How to Make a Caesar Cipher Wheel
A cipher wheel lets you code and decode secret messages in seconds. With some cardboard and a pencil, you can make one in about 20 minutes.
You will need these materials: two circles of thin cardboard (one 14 cm across and one 10 cm across), a protractor, a pencil, and a paper fastener.
1. Divide each circle into 26 equal slices, each about 13.8° wide (because 360° ÷ 26 ≈ 13.8°).
2. Write the alphabet around the edge of both circles, one letter in each slice.
3. Push the paper fastener through the centres of both circles, with the small circle on top.
4. Turn the small wheel until its A lines up with the big wheel's D. You now have a shift of 3!
Be careful to write every letter the same size, or the slices won't line up. To test your wheel, code the word ACE using the big wheel's letters. If you get DFH, your wheel works!

Notice: The title says exactly what you'll make, the materials list has exact sizes, every step starts with a command verb (Divide, Write, Push, Turn) and does only one job, and the guide ends by telling the reader how to check their work.
