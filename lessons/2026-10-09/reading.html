The Secret War Between Codemakers and Codebreakers

For as long as people have had secrets, other people have wanted to read them. Generals, kings, spies, and even schoolchildren passing notes have all faced the same problem: how do you send a message that only the right person can understand? The answer is cryptography, the art of hidden writing. But every time someone invents a clever new code, someone else works just as hard to break it. This contest between codemakers and codebreakers has shaped history for more than 2000 years.

One of the earliest famous codes belonged to Julius Caesar. According to the Roman writer Suetonius, Caesar protected his private letters by replacing each letter with the one three places further along in the alphabet, so A became D and B became E. To anyone who did not know the trick, the message looked like nonsense. For a while, that was enough. Many of Caesar's enemies could not read at all, and the idea of a secret alphabet was new. The Caesar cipher had a fatal weakness, however: there are only 25 possible shifts, so a patient enemy could simply try them all.

Stronger codes used a scrambled alphabet, in which any letter could stand for any other. There are so many possible scrambled alphabets that no one could ever test them one by one. For centuries, these codes seemed unbreakable. Then, in the 800s, a scholar named Al-Kindi, working in Baghdad, described an ingenious shortcut. He noticed that in any language, some letters appear far more often than others. In English, for example, about one letter in eight is an E. A codebreaker who counts the letters in a secret message can guess that the most common symbol probably stands for the most common letter, then fill in the rest like a crossword puzzle. This method, called frequency analysis, meant that any code that simply swapped one letter for another was now vulnerable.

Codebreaking could even decide the fate of a queen. In 1586, Mary, Queen of Scots, was being held prisoner in England. Her supporters wrote to her in a secret cipher, hidden inside the stoppers of beer barrels, about a plot to free her and take the English throne. What Mary did not know was that her letters were being secretly intercepted. A skilled codebreaker named Thomas Phelippes used frequency analysis to read them. The letters became evidence against her, and Mary was executed in 1587. Her trust in a weak code cost her everything.

The most famous battle between codemakers and codebreakers happened during the Second World War. The German military used a typewriter-like machine called Enigma, which changed its secret alphabet after every single letter. With its rotors and plugs, it could be set up in more ways than there are grains of sand on all the world's beaches. Yet mathematicians in Poland, led by Marian Rejewski, worked out how Enigma was wired years before the war began, and they shared their discoveries with Britain and France in 1939. At Bletchley Park in England, thousands of people, including the mathematician Alan Turing, built on that work in a systematic way, designing machines that could test thousands of settings each day. Canada had its own codebreaking team in Ottawa, too. Some codes were never broken at all. Navajo Code Talkers in the United States Marine Corps, and Cree speakers from Canada such as Charles Tomkins, sent messages in their own languages, which the enemy had no way of learning. Historians believe that breaking enemy codes shortened the war and saved many lives.

Today, the war goes on inside your computer. Every time someone types a password or buys something online, their information is encrypted using prime numbers so large that the fastest computers would need thousands of years to factor them. Codemakers are already preparing for powerful new quantum computers that might one day crack today's codes. The tools have changed from Caesar's letters to computer chips, but the lesson has not: a code is only as strong as its weakest point, and it is crucial never to assume that a secret will stay hidden forever. Sometimes the weakest point is not the maths at all, but a person who conceals a password under the keyboard.

Model answer: It means that even a very complicated code can fail if just one part of it is easy to attack. Caesar's cipher had only 25 possible shifts, so an enemy could try every one. Mary, Queen of Scots, trusted a cipher that only swapped letters, so Thomas Phelippes could crack it with frequency analysis and use her own letters against her. Even today, a strong computer code is useless if someone hides their password under the keyboard. To keep my passwords safe, I would make them long, never share them, and never write them where others can find them.
