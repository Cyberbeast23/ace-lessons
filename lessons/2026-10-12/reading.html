How Your Brain Talks to Itself (and Why Sleep Matters)

Press your fingertip to this screen. Before you even finish reading this sentence, a rush of information has already raced from your skin to your spinal cord and up into your brain. That journey happens because of neurons, the specialised cells that form the nervous system. A typical neuron has bushy branches called dendrites that collect incoming messages, a long cable called an axon that carries the signal onward, and a fatty coating called myelin that works like insulation on a wire. Myelinated axons can send impulses at astonishing speeds—sometimes around 100 metres per second, fast enough to cross a soccer pitch in the blink of an eye.

Neurons do not quite touch. Between one axon tip and the next cell sits a tiny gap called a synapse. When an electrical impulse reaches the end of an axon, the neuron releases chemical messengers that float across the gap and spark a new electrical signal in the neighbour. Your thoughts, memories, and feelings are patterns of these hand-offs cascading through networks of billions of cells. Scientists estimate that a human brain holds roughly 86 billion neurons, linked by trillions of synapses—more connections than there are stars visible in the night sky.

Not every message needs a long debate in the thinking brain. Touch a hot pan, and your hand jerks back before you consciously shout "Ow!" That reflex is a shortcut: sensory neurons alert the spinal cord, which instantly orders motor neurons to pull the muscle. Only afterward does the signal reach the cerebrum—the large, wrinkled top of the brain—so you can notice the heat and learn not to touch the pan again. Reflexes are life-saving because they skip the slower route through careful decision-making.

Different brain regions specialise. The cerebrum handles language, planning, imagination, and voluntary movement. Tucked under the back of the cerebrum, the cerebellum helps with balance and the smooth coordination that lets you catch a ball or ride a bike. Deep inside, smaller structures help store memories and manage emotions. Damage from a hard knock to the head—a concussion—can scramble these networks temporarily, which is why helmets matter for cycling, skiing, and contact sports. A helmet cannot make you invincible, but it spreads out the force of a blow and greatly lowers the chance of serious injury.

Here is a surprise many future doctors love: the brain keeps changing. When Ace practises a spelling word or a Cantonese phrase, synapses that fire together grow stronger—a process scientists summarise as "cells that fire together, wire together." Sleep is not wasted time. During deep sleep and dreaming, the brain replays the day's patterns, strengthens useful connections, and clears chemical waste. Children and teens who regularly lose sleep often find memory and focus harder the next day, not because they are lazy, but because their brains missed a night of essential maintenance.

So the nervous system is both a high-speed messaging network and a learning machine. Sensory nerves bring the world in; the spinal cord and brain decide what matters; motor nerves send commands out. Protect that system with helmets and seat belts, feed it with curiosity and practice, and give it the sleep it needs. The more carefully you look after your brain, the more brilliantly it can look after you—a truth every doctor, athlete, and student eventually discovers.
