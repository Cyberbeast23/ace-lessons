✍️ Writing Week 15: Problem-Solution Essay: Protect Your Brain 🧠🛡️

Yesterday you wrote a historical letter. Today, Ace, you'll write like a problem-solver. A problem-solution essay names a real problem, explains why it matters, and proposes a clear solution with reasons. Future doctors write this way all the time: they spot a health risk, explain the harm, and recommend a plan. Your topic today: protecting the brain from concussion with helmets and smart habits.

Part 1: The Lesson

A problem-solution essay has six parts 🧩

Readers need to understand the trouble before they will accept your fix:

1. Hook + topic: grab attention and name the issue in one clear sentence
2. Problem: what is going wrong? Who is affected?
3. Why it matters: effects / consequences (use facts or logical reasons)
4. Solution: your clear plan (be specific, not vague)
5. How / why it works: evidence, steps, or answers to "but what if…?"
6. Call to action: tell the reader what to do next

Five problem-solver moves

  • Be precise: "Kids should wear a properly fitted bike helmet every ride" beats "people should be safer."
  • Show the harm: a concussion can scramble thinking, memory and balance for days or weeks.
  • Offer a realistic solution: helmets, seat belts, breaking falls safely in sports, and telling an adult after a hard hit to the head.
  • Answer an objection: "Some kids say helmets look silly, but looking briefly silly is better than a brain injury."
  • End with action: tell the reader one thing to do this week.

Grown-up words: a problem is the unwanted situation; a solution is the plan that reduces or removes it. Evidence is a fact or reason that supports your claim. A call to action tells the reader what to do.

Model Problem-Solution Essay (short version)

Hook + problemWhy it matters + solutionHow it works / objectionCall to action

Every year, kids tumble off bikes, crash on ski hills, or collide in sports. The real danger is not a scraped knee—it is a concussion, a brain injury caused by a sudden jolt to the head.
A concussion can leave someone dizzy, foggy, and unable to concentrate at school for days. The best everyday solution is simple: wear a certified helmet that fits snugly, every single ride or downhill run, and buckle the chin strap.
A helmet spreads out the force of a bump so the brain is less likely to slam hard inside the skull. Some kids worry helmets look uncool, but protecting 86 billion neurons is cooler than any hair style.
This week, check your helmet's fit with a grown-up, and promise yourself: no wheels or hills without it.

Notice: the model names a specific problem (concussion), explains harm (foggy thinking), offers a concrete solution (fitted helmet + strap), answers an objection (looks uncool), and ends with a clear action.
