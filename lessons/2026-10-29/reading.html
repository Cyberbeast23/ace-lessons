Olive Wreaths and Big Ideas: The Gifts of Ancient Greece

Imagine a summer day in 480 BCE. Forty thousand people are crowded onto grassy slopes at Olympia, sweating in the heat and roaring as a pack of runners sprints down a sandy track. The winner will not receive money or a gold medal. He will get a crown made from the leaves of a wild olive tree. Yet that simple wreath was one of the greatest honours a person in ancient Greece could earn, and his home city might celebrate him for the rest of his life.

Ancient Greece was not one country the way Canada is. It was a patchwork of hundreds of independent city-states, each with its own government, army and laws. Athens and Sparta were the most famous, and they were fierce rivals. Athens was proud of its art, theatre and ideas, while Sparta trained almost every boy from the age of seven to become a tough soldier. The city-states often fought each other, but they shared the same language, the same gods and the same stories.

The Olympic Games brought them together. The Games were held every four years at Olympia, a sacred place honouring Zeus, the king of the gods. Before each festival, messengers travelled across Greece to announce a truce, so that athletes and fans could journey safely even through lands at war. The events included footraces, wrestling, boxing, chariot racing and the pentathlon, a five-part contest that tested speed, strength and skill.

Meanwhile, in Athens, something even more surprising was taking shape. Around 508 BCE, the Athenians began to govern themselves through democracy, from the Greek words for "people" and "power." Instead of obeying a king, citizens gathered on a hillside called the Pnyx for the assembly, where they debated and voted on laws by raising their hands. If they feared that one politician was becoming too powerful, they could vote to send him away for ten years by scratching his name on a piece of broken pottery. However, Athenian democracy had serious limits. Women, enslaved people and foreigners were excluded, so only a small fraction of the people living in Athens actually had a say.

The Greeks also asked enormous questions. Philosophers such as Socrates, Plato and Aristotle wondered what makes a person good, what a fair government looks like, and how we can know whether something is true. Socrates was famous for never simply giving answers. Instead, he asked his students question after question until they discovered the holes in their own reasoning. Other Greek thinkers studied the natural world. Hippocrates taught that illnesses have natural causes, not curses from the gods, and doctors today still speak of the Hippocratic Oath, a promise to help patients and do no harm.

Greek mathematicians were just as bold. Pythagoras and his followers believed that numbers explained everything from music to the stars. Around 240 BCE, a scholar named Eratosthenes measured the shadows cast by the Sun in two Egyptian cities and used geometry to calculate the distance around the entire Earth. His answer was remarkably close to the true value, more than 1700 years before anyone sailed around the globe.

Ancient Greece faded long ago, conquered first by Macedonia and later by Rome, but its legacy is everywhere. When you vote in a class election, walk past a building with tall columns, use a word like "telephone" or "biology," or watch the Olympic Games on TV, you are borrowing a gift from the Greeks. The modern Olympics, first held in Athens in 1896, were directly inspired by the ancient festival. Perhaps the greatest gift of all, though, is the habit Socrates tried to teach: never stop asking "Why?"
