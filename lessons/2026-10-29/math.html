🏛️ Math Week 32: Ancient Greece Math Lab

Ace, the ancient Greeks loved numbers so much that some of them thought the whole universe was built out of math. They timed runners at the first Olympic Games, designed temples with perfect proportions, counted votes in the world's first democracy, and even measured the size of the Earth with a stick and a shadow. Today you'll think like a Greek mathematician with BCE/CE timelines, unit rates and speed in km/h, area, counting patterns, Greek (Attic) numerals, the Pythagorean theorem, proportional reasoning with angles, percentages and fractions, finding a missing value from a mean, and an equation with the variable on both sides.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your philosopher's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (m with m, minutes with minutes)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Timelines across BCE and CE

BCE years count down toward the year 1; CE years count up. There is no year 0: 1 BCE is followed directly by 1 CE.

From 500 BCE to 200 CE: 500 + 200 = 700, then subtract 1 because there is no year 0: 699 years. (Check with small numbers: 1 BCE to 1 CE is just 1 year, and 1 + 1 − 1 = 1 ✔)

Worked Example 2: From m/s to km/h

1 m/s means 1 metre every second. In an hour (3600 s) that's 3600 m = 3.6 km. So multiply m/s by 3.6 to get km/h.

A runner covers 200 m in 25 s: 200 ÷ 25 = 8 m/s, and 8 × 3.6 = 28.8 km/h.

Worked Example 3: Greek (Attic) numerals

Athenians wrote numbers with letters: I = 1, Γ = 5 (from pente), Δ = 10 (from deka), H = 100 (from hekaton), X = 1000 (from khilioi). Like Roman numerals, you add the symbols up.

XHHΔΓIII = 1000 + 200 + 10 + 5 + 3 = 1218.

Worked Example 4: The Pythagorean theorem

In a right triangle, the longest side (across from the right angle) is the hypotenuse, c. Pythagoras's rule: a² + b² = c².

Legs 3 m and 4 m: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25, and √25 = 5 m. (√ means "what number times itself gives this?")

Worked Example 5: Angles and proportions

A full circle is 360°. If an arc is 30° of a circle, it is 30/360 = 1/12 of the whole way around. So if that arc is 100 km long, the whole circle is 100 × 12 = 1200 km.

Rule: whole circle = arc length × (360 ÷ angle).

Worked Example 6: The missing value in a mean

Mean = total ÷ number of values, so total = mean × number of values.

Three scores 7, 9, 8 and a fourth unknown score give a mean of 8.5. Total needed = 8.5 × 4 = 34. Known total = 24. Missing score = 34 − 24 = 10.

Worked Example 7: Variables on both sides

2(x + 3) = x + 10. Expand the brackets: 2x + 6 = x + 10. Subtract x from both sides: x + 6 = 10. Subtract 6: x = 4. Check: 2 × 7 = 14 and 4 + 10 = 14 ✔
