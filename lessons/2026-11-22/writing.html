Yesterday you wrote a picture book for five-year-olds. Today, Ace, you'll write for one of the toughest audiences there is: a busy adult who is deciding whether to hire you. When people apply for a job, a scholarship, a summer camp leadership spot or a volunteer position, they usually send a cover letter: a one-page formal letter that says which job they want, proves they're a good fit by matching their skills to what the job needs, and asks politely for the next step. A great cover letter is short, specific and confident, without bragging. Today's genre is the cover letter (formal letter of application).

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong cover letter 📨

Most cover letters fit on one page: about 200 to 350 words in three or four short paragraphs.

1. Heading and greeting: the date, then a formal greeting using the person's name if you know it (Dear Ms. Chen,) or Dear Hiring Team, if you don't
2. Opening paragraph: name the exact job you're applying for, where you saw it, and one sentence that makes the reader want to keep reading
3. Matching skills with evidence: pick two or three things the job asks for and prove you have them with a real example ("I built…", "I led…", "I solved…")
4. Why this job (show you did research): a fact about the company or the work that shows you care about this job, not just any job
5. Closing paragraph: thank the reader, and ask politely for the next step ("I would welcome the chance to meet with you.")
6. Sign-off: a formal closing with a comma (Sincerely, or Yours truly,), then your name

Five habits of great cover-letter writers

  • Read the job posting like a detective: underline each thing the employer wants. Your letter should answer those exact needs.
  • Show, don't just tell: ❌ "I am a hard worker." ✅ "I rebuilt my bridge model three times until it held 2 kg." Evidence is more convincing than adjectives.
  • Use a formal, polite tone: no slang (super, awesome, stuff, gonna), no emojis, and complete sentences. Formal doesn't mean stiff: you can still sound friendly and excited.
  • Make it about them, too: explain how your skills will help the company, not only why you want the job.
  • Keep it short and proofread it: an employer may read 100 letters. One spelling mistake in a letter that says "I pay attention to detail" can cost you the job!

Cover-letter writer's rule: for every claim you make about yourself, ask, "What is my proof?" If you have no example, cut the claim or find one.

Mini-lesson: grammar for formal letters 📏

Commas in dates: put a comma between the day and the year, and after the year if the sentence keeps going: On November 22, 2026, I visited a construction site.

Greeting and closing punctuation: Dear Ms. Chen, (comma after the greeting; Ms. and Mr. take a period in Canadian style) and Sincerely, (only the first word of the closing is capitalized: Yours truly,).

"My teacher and I" or "my teacher and me"? Cover the other person and test it. I built a model ✅ so My teacher and I built a model. She helped me ✅ so She helped my teacher and me.

Capitals for proper nouns: names of companies, schools, cities and buildings: Skyline Builders, Vancouver, Brock Commons.

Canadian spelling: metre, centre, colour, neighbour, labour. An engineer's letter should measure in metres!

The job posting

JUNIOR ENGINEER (Youth Summer Program), Skyline Builders, Vancouver
Skyline Builders designs and builds safe, earthquake-ready towers, including mass-timber buildings. We are looking for a curious young person to join our design team for a summer program. You should: (1) enjoy solving problems and testing ideas, (2) be careful and accurate with measurements and math, and (3) work well on a team. Send a cover letter to Ms. Ana Chen, Program Leader.

Model cover letter (about 250 words)

Heading, greeting + openingMatching skills with evidenceWhy this job (research)Closing + sign-off

November 22, 2026

Dear Ms. Chen,

I am writing to apply for the Junior Engineer position in the Youth Summer Program at Skyline Builders, which I saw on your website. Ever since I watched a crane lift a steel beam forty storeys into the air, I have wanted to know how tall buildings stay standing.

Your posting asks for someone who enjoys solving problems and testing ideas. Last spring, my classmates and I built a tower out of straws and tape for our science fair. It collapsed during the first shake test, so we added triangle braces and a heavier base and tested it four more times. Our final tower was 1.2 metres tall and survived every test. You also want someone accurate with math. I measure carefully, and I use math every day to check my work. Finally, I work well on a team: I helped organize our group so that everyone had a job, and we finished early.

I am especially excited about your mass-timber buildings. I learned that wood stores carbon and that prefabricated panels can let a crew build about two floors a week. I would love to learn how engineers make wooden towers safe in earthquakes.

Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the chance to meet with you and show you my tower designs.

Sincerely,
Jordan Lee

Notice: the opening names the exact job and hooks the reader with a real moment. The middle paragraph answers the posting's needs in the same order (problem-solving, math, teamwork), and the problem-solving claim is backed by real proof: a tower that failed, was improved, and passed. The research paragraph shows Jordan knows what this company does. Look for the comma in the date, Dear Ms. Chen,, my classmates and I, the Canadian spelling metres and the formal closing Sincerely,. One thing Jordan could still improve: "I use math every day" has no specific proof yet!
