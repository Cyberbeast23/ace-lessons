Today's skill is one that researchers, reporters and university students use every single day: synthesizing information from two sources. To synthesize means to put ideas from different texts together to build a bigger understanding than either text gives you alone. As you read, ask:
  • 1. Where do the texts agree? When two sources say the same thing, you can be more confident it's true.
  • 2. What does each text add? One text might explain the general idea; the other might give a specific example or a person's experience.
  • 3. Where do they differ? Do they have different purposes, points of view, or even details that seem to disagree? Can both be right?
  • 4. What can I conclude using both? Some questions can only be answered by combining a fact from Text 1 with a fact from Text 2.

Text 1: Reaching for the Sky: How Skyscrapers Stand Tall

Stand at the bottom of a skyscraper and look straight up. The walls seem to rush toward the clouds. It's easy to forget that every one of those floors is pressing down on the floors below it, and that the wind is pushing on the whole tower at once. A skyscraper is not just a tall building. It is a carefully balanced machine for fighting two invisible forces: gravity, which pulls everything down, and wind (and, in places like British Columbia, earthquakes), which push it sideways.

Two inventions made skyscrapers possible. For thousands of years, tall buildings had thick stone or brick walls that carried all the weight. The higher you built, the thicker the bottom walls had to be, until there was hardly room left inside. In the 1880s, engineers in Chicago began using a skeleton of iron and steel beams and columns to hold up the building instead. The outside walls became a thin "skin" that only had to keep out the weather. The Home Insurance Building, finished in 1885, is often called the first skyscraper, even though it had only ten storeys. The second invention was the safety elevator. In 1854, Elisha Otis stood on a raised platform at a fair in New York and ordered the rope cut. A spring-loaded brake caught the platform, and the crowd gasped. Without safe elevators, nobody would want to climb to the 40th floor.

Gravity: from the top to the bedrock. The weight of a skyscraper travels down a path. Floors rest on beams, beams rest on columns, and columns carry the load to the foundation. Columns are squeezed, or put in compression. Concrete is excellent at handling compression, and steel is excellent at handling being stretched (tension). That's why engineers reinforce concrete with steel bars called rebar: together, the two materials handle both kinds of force. At the bottom, deep piles or a thick concrete mat spread the load into the ground, and in many cities piles are driven all the way down to solid bedrock.

Wind and earthquakes: bending without breaking. A skyscraper is supposed to move a little. A stiff tower that cannot bend at all could crack, so engineers design towers to be strong but slightly flexible, like a tall tree. Many towers have a strong concrete core in the middle, around the elevators and stairs, that acts like a spine. Some super-tall towers add a tuned mass damper: a huge weight near the top that swings in the opposite direction to the sway and helps dampen it. Taipei 101 in Taiwan has a steel ball weighing about 660 tonnes hanging inside its upper floors. In earthquake country like coastal British Columbia, the building code requires towers to be designed to survive strong shaking.

The next big idea: building with wood. Steel and concrete are strong, but making them releases a lot of carbon dioxide. Some engineers now build tall with mass timber: thick panels and beams made by gluing layers of wood together. Wood is a renewable material, because forests can be replanted, and it stores carbon that trees pulled out of the air. Wooden skyscrapers are still much shorter than steel giants, but they are growing taller every few years.

Text 2: "We Built It Like a Giant Puzzle": An Interview with an Engineer

This interview is imagined, but the engineer's story is based on real facts about Brock Commons Tallwood House, an 18-storey student residence at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver.

Q: People hear "wooden skyscraper" and think, "Isn't that a fire hazard?" What do you say?
That's the first question everyone asks! Mass timber isn't like a pile of kindling. A thick wood panel chars on the outside in a fire, and that charred layer actually protects the wood inside, a bit like a crust. In our building, we also covered the wood with layers of fire-resistant drywall and put sprinklers everywhere. Safety inspectors checked every detail before anyone moved in.

Q: Is the whole building made of wood?
No, and that surprises people. The ground floor is concrete, and there are two concrete cores that hold the stairs and elevators. They're the building's backbone against wind and earthquakes. The 17 floors above the ground floor are wood: glued-laminated columns, which we call glulam, and cross-laminated timber floor panels. So it's really a team of materials, each doing the job it's best at.

Q: How long did it take to build?
The wooden structure went up in about 70 days, roughly two floors a week. That was possible because the panels were prefabricated in a factory, cut by computer to within millimetres, and numbered. When the trucks arrived, the crane lifted each piece into place and the crew bolted it down. It was like building a giant puzzle, where every piece fits only one spot. Building that fast also meant fewer noisy, dusty months for the neighbours.

Q: What was the hardest part?
Rain! This is Vancouver, after all. Wood can swell when it gets wet, so we had to protect the panels and work quickly between storms. And because the building was one of the tallest wooden buildings in the world when it opened, there wasn't a rule book for everything. We tested a lot of ideas first.

Q: What would you tell a kid who wants to be an engineer?
Ask "Why?" about everything. Why doesn't that bridge sag? Why is that tower shaped like that? Every structure you see is the answer to a problem someone solved. And be good at math. I use it every day.
