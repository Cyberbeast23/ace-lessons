🏙️ Math Week 56: Reaching for the Sky! Skyscraper Engineer's Math Lab

Ace, every skyscraper begins as a page of numbers. Before a single crane arrives, structural engineers work out how tall each storey will be, how heavy each concrete floor is, how fast the elevators must travel, how much the top of the tower may sway in the wind, and how many weeks the construction crew will need. Today you're joining the design team as a skyscraper engineer. You'll use multi-step expressions, comparing with division and rounding, rates of speed, scale drawings, volume and mass, percent decrease, rounding up for whole weeks, surface area of walls, integers on a number line, converting km/h to m/s, combined work rates, the mean, two-step equations, volume of a hollow prism and multiplying by a rate.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your engineer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (cm, m, km, m², m³, t, s, h, km/h, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change centimetres to metres or hours to seconds before you calculate.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A storey is about 3 m tall, not 30 m, and an elevator can't reach the top of a tower in half a second.

Worked Example 1: Multi-step expressions

A building has 12 storeys that are each 3 m tall, plus a 4 m roof. Height = 12 × 3 + 4 = 36 + 4 = 40 m. (Multiply before you add.)

Worked Example 2: Comparing with division

A 90 m tower is how many times as tall as a 40 m one? 90 ÷ 40 = 2.25, which rounds to 2.3 times (nearest tenth).

Worked Example 3: Speed, distance and time

Time = distance ÷ speed. An elevator moving at 4 m/s takes 100 ÷ 4 = 25 s to rise 100 m.

Worked Example 4: Scale drawings and models

On a 1:200 model, every 1 cm on the model stands for 200 cm in real life. A 15 cm model is 15 × 200 = 3 000 cm = 30 m tall. (100 cm = 1 m.)

Worked Example 5: Volume, then mass

A concrete slab 10 m × 5 m × 0.2 m has a volume of 10 × 5 × 0.2 = 10 m³. If 1 m³ of concrete has a mass of about 2.4 t, the slab is 10 × 2.4 = 24 t.

Worked Example 6: Percent decrease

If something drops from 50 to 40, the decrease is 10. Percent decrease = 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = 20%. Always divide by the starting amount.

Worked Example 7: Rounding up for whole weeks

If a crew needs 6.5 weeks of work, it can't stop halfway through a week and call it done, so the job takes 7 weeks. When you need enough time (or buses, or boxes), round up.

Worked Example 8: Area of the outside walls

A building with a square base 10 m on each side has a perimeter of 4 × 10 = 40 m. If it is 20 m tall, its four walls cover 40 × 20 = 800 m².

Worked Example 9: Integers on a number line

From level −2 (two floors underground) up to level 5 is 5 − (−2) = 5 + 2 = 7 floors.

Worked Example 10: Converting km/h to m/s

1 km = 1 000 m and 1 h = 3 600 s. So 36 km/h = 36 000 m ÷ 3 600 s = 10 m/s. Shortcut: divide km/h by 3.6.

Worked Example 11: Combined work rates

One crew paints 20 windows an hour. Two crews paint 2 × 20 = 40 an hour, so 300 windows take 300 ÷ 40 = 7.5 hours.

Worked Example 12: The mean

Mean = total ÷ how many. For 120, 100 and 140: 120 + 100 + 140 = 360, and 360 ÷ 3 = 120.

Worked Example 13: Two-step equations

"A 5 m lobby plus n storeys of 3 m each makes 65 m" means 5 + 3n = 65. Undo in reverse: 65 − 5 = 60, then 60 ÷ 3 = 20 storeys.

Worked Example 14: Volume of a hollow prism

A hollow box is a big prism minus the empty space inside. Outside 4 m × 4 m, hollow 2 m × 2 m, height 10 m: (16 − 4) × 10 = 120 m³.

Worked Example 15: Multiplying by a rate

If every cubic metre of something stores 0.5 t, then 300 m³ stores 300 × 0.5 = 150 t.
