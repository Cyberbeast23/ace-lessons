📖 Reading Week 62: From Bean to Bar: The Long, Surprising Journey of Chocolate

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you compared perspectives. Today's skill is one that strong readers use without even noticing, until a text gets hard: monitoring your comprehension and using fix-up strategies. Good readers keep checking, "Does this make sense?" When everything makes sense, it's a click 👍. When a word, sentence or idea stops your understanding, it's a clunk 🔧. A clunk isn't a failure. It's a signal to slow down and use a tool from the fix-up toolbox:
  • 🔁 Reread: go back and read the sentence (or the sentence before it) again, more slowly.
  • ⏩ Read on: sometimes the next sentence explains the hard one. Keep going a little, then come back.
  • 🔍 Use context clues: look for a definition, an example, a comparison or a contrast near the hard word ("…the nibs, or broken pieces of bean…").
  • ✂️ Break the word apart: look for a prefix, root or suffix you know (de- + forest + -ation).
  • 🖼️ Picture it: make a movie in your mind, or sketch a quick diagram of a process.
  • ❓ Ask and paraphrase: ask, "What is this paragraph mostly about?" and say it in your own words. If you can't, that's a clunk!
  • Clunk checks: today's passage has three brown 🔧 boxes. When you reach one, stop and check: click or clunk? Which fix-up tool would help?
  • Word detective: Latin cultus = "tilled, cared for," so to cultivate a plant is to grow it with care · Latin cupere = "to desire," so something coveted is strongly desired · Latin metus = "fear," so a meticulous person is so careful it's almost as if they fear making a mistake · Latin sustinere = "to hold up," so a sustainable way of farming can keep going for a long time
  • Words in yellow are vocabulary words. Use the sentence around them (the context) to figure out what they mean.

From Bean to Bar: The Long, Surprising Journey of Chocolate

Break a piece off a chocolate bar and listen. That sharp snap is the sound of thousands of years of history, a long chain of farmers and traders, and some surprisingly tricky chemistry. Before chocolate was a bar, it was a drink. Before it was a drink, it was a bitter seed hidden inside a strange, football-shaped fruit. And before anyone tasted it, it grew on a small tree in the rainforests of South America.

A drink fit for the gods. Scientists studying ancient pottery in Ecuador have found traces of cacao that are more than 5 000 years old, so people there were using it very long ago. Later, the Olmec, the Maya and other peoples of Mesoamerica (today's Mexico and Central America) cultivated cacao trees in their gardens and forests. The Maya roasted and ground the seeds, mixed them with water, maize and chili peppers, and poured the drink from one cup to another, held high, to build a thick foam. It was bitter, spicy and nothing like a modern candy bar. The Aztecs, who ruled a great empire in the highlands of Mexico, could not grow cacao in their cool mountain valleys, so they collected the beans as tribute, a kind of tax paid by the peoples they had conquered. Cacao beans were so valuable that the Aztecs used them as money. Centuries later, a Swedish scientist gave the tree its official name: Theobroma cacao, from Greek words meaning "food of the gods."

Sugar crosses the ocean. In the 1500s, Spanish invaders brought cacao back to Spain. Europeans found the drink too bitter, so they added sugar, cinnamon and vanilla and served it hot. Over the next two hundred years, hot chocolate became a coveted treat in the palaces of Spain, France and England. Only the rich could afford it. To grow enough cacao for Europe's appetite, colonists set up plantations in the Caribbean and South America and forced enslaved Africans to work on them. The sweet drink had a bitter history.

🔧 Clunk check #1: Did the word tribute clunk for you? Reread the sentence. The author gave a context clue right after the comma. What does it mean? Then paraphrase this section in one sentence: how did chocolate change when it reached Europe?

A tree that grows its fruit on its trunk. Cacao trees are fussy. They grow only in a hot, rainy belt within about 20° of the equator, and they prefer the shade of taller rainforest trees. Their tiny white flowers sprout straight out of the trunk and thick branches, not from the tips of twigs, so the ripe pods look as if someone stuck rugby balls onto the bark. The flowers are pollinated mainly by midges, flies so small you could barely see one on your fingernail. Each pod holds about 30 to 50 seeds, the "beans," packed in a sweet, white, slimy pulp. Farmers cut the pods open with machetes, scoop out the beans and pile them under banana leaves. For about a week, yeasts and bacteria feed on the pulp, and the pile grows hot. This fermentation changes chemicals inside the beans and creates the first hints of chocolate flavour. Without it, no amount of sugar could make a good bar. Then the beans are spread out to dry in the sun before they are shipped to factories around the world.

The factory: roast, crack, grind. At the factory, the beans are roasted, which deepens their flavour, the way toasting does to bread. Then machines crack them and blow away the papery shells, a step called winnowing, leaving broken pieces of bean called nibs. When nibs are ground, the heat of grinding melts their fat, and they turn into a thick, runny brown paste called cocoa liquor. (Despite its name, it contains no alcohol at all.) Cocoa liquor is made of two parts: dark, flavourful cocoa solids and a pale yellow fat called cocoa butter. In 1828, a Dutch chemist named Coenraad van Houten invented a press that could squeeze most of the cocoa butter out, leaving a cake that could be crushed into cocoa powder. By 1847, a British company called J. S. Fry & Sons had worked out that if you mixed cocoa butter back in with sugar and cocoa, you could pour the mixture into a mould and make a solid bar you could eat. In 1875, the Swiss chocolatier Daniel Peter added milk, and milk chocolate was born. Four years later, another Swiss maker, Rodolphe Lindt, invented the conche, a machine that stirred warm chocolate for days and turned a gritty bar into a smooth one. Each invention revolutionized the way people ate chocolate.

Why chocolate snaps. Here is where chocolate becomes a science puzzle. When melted cocoa butter cools and hardens, its fat molecules stack up into crystals, much as bricks can be stacked into many different walls. Cocoa butter can form six different crystal types, and only one of them, which scientists call Form V, gives chocolate a glossy shine, a crisp snap and a smooth melt on your tongue. The other forms make chocolate dull, soft or crumbly. To get Form V, chocolatiers temper the chocolate: they heat it until every crystal melts, cool it while stirring so a few crystals begin to form, and then warm it very slightly so that only the good Form V crystals survive. These "seed" crystals then guide the rest of the chocolate to stack the same way. The process is meticulous: a difference of just one or two degrees can ruin a batch. If a bar gets too warm in a hot car and then cools again, the crystals rearrange, and grey-white streaks called bloom appear. The bar is still safe to eat, but it has lost its snap.

The bitter side of sweet. Today, the world grows about five million tonnes of cacao a year, and roughly 60 percent of it comes from just two countries in West Africa: Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. Most of it is grown on small family farms. Yet many cacao farmers earn only a tiny share of what a chocolate bar costs in a Canadian store, often a few cents per bar. Because farm families are so poor, many children work on cacao farms instead of going to school, sometimes doing dangerous jobs with machetes and chemical sprays. This exploitation has been reported for more than twenty years, and big chocolate companies have promised again and again to end it. The environment pays too: in some regions, rainforests have been cut down to plant more cacao, a problem called deforestation. In 2024, heavy rains, drought and a plant disease that spreads among cacao trees shrank the harvest, and cacao prices around the world shot to record highs.

🔧 Clunk check #3: Did deforestation clunk? Break it apart: de- (remove) + forest + -ation (the process of). Now check your understanding: name two problems this section describes, one for people and one for the environment.

A better bar? Some farmers and companies are trying a different way. Fair-trade programs promise farmers a minimum price and extra money for schools and clean water. In agroforestry, cacao is planted in the shade of taller trees, the way it grows in the wild, which protects the soil, gives birds and midges a home and can keep a farm healthy for generations. Scientists are breeding cacao trees that resist disease, and some small "bean-to-bar" chocolate makers buy directly from farmers and print the farm's name on the wrapper. None of these ideas is perfect yet, but together they point toward a more sustainable future, one in which the people who grow chocolate can enjoy a good life too.

So the next time you hear that snap, think about the journey: a midge visiting a flower on a tree trunk, a week of warm fermentation in a pile of pulp, a factory full of grinders and conches, crystals stacking like perfect bricks, and a farmer thousands of kilometres away who deserves a fair share of the treat.
