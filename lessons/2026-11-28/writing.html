Yesterday you wrote a poem for two voices. Today, Ace, you'll write in a genre that turns the reader into a detective: the mystery story. A good mystery hands the reader the same clues the detective finds, hides the answer in plain sight, tries to trick the reader with a false lead, and then, in the big reveal, explains how every clue fits. It's the perfect genre for today's reading skill, too: a mystery reader keeps checking, "Does this make sense? What did I miss?" Today's genre is the mystery story, and its setting is a chocolate shop!

Part 1: The Lesson

The six building blocks of a mystery 🕵️

Think of a mystery as a fair puzzle: the reader should be able to solve it, but only if they pay close attention.

Hook: the puzzle→Meet the suspects→Find the clues→Follow a red herring→The key clue clicks→The reveal
1. The detective: a curious, observant main character who notices small details and asks good questions. Detectives don't have to be adults!
2. The puzzle (the crime): something strange, missing or ruined, introduced early with a strong hook so the reader needs to know what happened.
3. Suspects: usually two to four characters, each with a reason (a motive) or a chance (an opportunity) to be guilty.
4. Clues: real details that point to the answer. Plant them early and casually so the reader can find them, but might not notice them.
5. A red herring: a false clue that points the wrong way, so the reader suspects the wrong person.
6. The reveal: the detective explains the solution step by step, showing how each clue fits. No surprise answers that weren't set up!

Five habits of great mystery writers

  • Play fair: every clue the detective uses in the reveal must appear earlier in the story.
  • Hide clues in a crowd: slip the important detail into a list of ordinary ones (a mop bucket, a sweater on a hook, a cocoa-dusted switch).
  • Give every suspect a reason: if only one character could have done it, there's no mystery.
  • Use dialogue to question suspects: what people say (and what they avoid saying) is a clue too.
  • Use real science or facts: a clue that depends on a true fact (like how chocolate blooms) makes the reveal feel clever and believable.

Mystery rule: when the reader reaches the reveal, they should think, "Of course! The clue was right there!"

Mini-lesson: grammar for detectives 🖋️

Punctuating dialogue: the spoken words and their punctuation go inside the quotation marks. Use a comma before the speaker tag, unless it's a question or exclamation. ✅ "Look at these streaks," said Priya. ✅ "Who was here last night?" asked Uncle Raj. Start a new paragraph each time a new person speaks.

Commas after introductory words and clauses: ✅ When the alarm rang, Priya ran downstairs. ✅ Suddenly, the lights flickered.

its vs. it's: its = belonging to it (The bunny lost its shine.). it's = it is (It's a clue!). Test it: if "it is" works, use it's.

Canadian spelling: flavour, colour, favourite, centre, grey, neighbour. The grey streaks ruined the colour of my favourite bar.

Model mystery: "The Case of the Cloudy Chocolates" (about 420 words)

ClueRed herringThe reveal

On the morning of the Winter Festival, Priya Sandhu pushed open the cooling-room door at her uncle's chocolate shop and gasped. Two hundred chocolate bunnies, glossy and perfect the night before, were covered in grey-white streaks.

"They're ruined!" cried Uncle Raj. "Somebody sabotaged my bunnies!"

Priya, who wanted to be a scientist, took out her notebook. The cooling room was cold, as usual. She looked around: a mop bucket by the door, a thick wool sweater on a hook, a broom and a light switch smudged with brown cocoa powder. Then she noticed something strange. Only the bunnies on the top shelf, right under the heating vent, had streaks. The bunnies on the bottom shelf still shone.

There were three suspects. Mr. Baptiste, the night cleaner, had mopped the cooling room at midnight. Sam, the new apprentice, had stayed late to wrap boxes. And Jolene Fairweather, who owned the rival chocolate shop across the street, had visited at closing time. "Your bunnies look lovely," she'd said, smiling. Now, in the garbage can, Priya found a crumpled gold wrapper from Jolene's shop.

"It's her!" said Uncle Raj. "She wants to win the festival prize."

"Maybe," said Priya. "But a wrapper doesn't ruin chocolate. Heat does."

She remembered what she'd read about bloom. When tempered chocolate gets warm and then cools again, its cocoa butter crystals rearrange and grey streaks appear. The bunnies hadn't been touched. They had been warmed.

She looked up. The thermostat beside the vent read 30 °C, but the cooling room had been set to 16 °C.

"Somebody turned up the heat last night, then turned it back down this morning," Priya explained. "The warm air blew out of the vent onto the top shelf only. That's why the bottom bunnies are fine. The thermostat has a brown fingerprint, and the only person who was dusting truffles with cocoa powder last night was…"

Everyone turned to Sam. His face went red. "I was freezing while I wrapped boxes," he admitted. "I turned the heat up, and this morning I turned it back down. I didn't know it would hurt the chocolate! That's why I brought my sweater today."

Uncle Raj sighed, then laughed. "Well, Detective Priya, it's a good thing chocolate can be melted and tempered again."

By noon, two hundred shiny new bunnies sat on the shelves, and Sam had a new job: guarding the thermostat.

Notice: the story opens with the puzzle in the first paragraph, introduces three suspects who each had a chance, and hides the key clues (top shelf only, the cocoa-dusted switch, the sweater) among ordinary details. Jolene's wrapper is the red herring. In the reveal, Priya uses a real science fact from today's reading (heat + cooling = bloom) to explain how every clue fits. Every clue she uses appears before the reveal, so the mystery plays fair.
