Renaissance Genius: The Curious Notebooks of Leonardo da Vinci

Imagine opening a notebook and finding a sketch of a flying machine, a recipe for paint, a drawing of a human heart and a shopping list, all on the same page. Stranger still, the handwriting runs backward, from right to left, so you need a mirror to read it. Pages like this belonged to Leonardo da Vinci, one of the most famous thinkers who ever lived.

Leonardo was born in 1452 near the small town of Vinci, in what is now Italy. He lived during the Renaissance, a word that means "rebirth." For about two hundred years, artists and scholars in Europe rediscovered the books and buildings of ancient Greece and Rome and began asking bold new questions about the world. Cities such as Florence buzzed with painters, sculptors, bankers and builders.

At about fourteen, Leonardo became an apprentice in the Florence workshop of a master artist named Andrea del Verrocchio. An apprentice was a young worker who learned a craft by helping a master every day. Leonardo ground pigments, prepared wooden panels and practised drawing drapery, hands and faces again and again. Legend says that when Verrocchio saw an angel the young Leonardo had painted, he realized his student had already surpassed him.

What made Leonardo different was not just talent but curiosity. He did not want to paint a bird; he wanted to know how a bird flies. He watched water swirling around rocks, studied how light falls on a curved surface and sketched the muscles of horses. To understand the human body, he dissected human bodies, cutting them apart carefully to see what lay inside. He made some of the first accurate drawings of the curved human spine, and he studied how the valves of the heart open and close. Some of his ideas about blood flowing through the heart were only confirmed by scientists in modern times.

Leonardo's notebooks also overflow with inventions: a flying machine with flapping wings, a parachute shaped like a pyramid, a diving suit and an armoured vehicle. Most were never built in his lifetime, because the materials and engines he needed did not exist yet. In recent years, engineers have built prototypes from his drawings to test them. A few worked surprisingly well; others showed why he never finished them. Leonardo was not afraid of failure. He would rather sketch a hundred possible answers than pretend to know the right one.

Of course, Leonardo is most famous for his paintings, even though he finished fewer than twenty that survive today. In the Mona Lisa, which hangs in the Louvre museum in Paris, he used a technique called sfumato, softly blending colours with no sharp outlines so that the face seems to glow through a light haze. For The Last Supper, a huge wall painting in Milan, he tried an experimental method of painting on dry plaster so that he could work slowly. Sadly, the paint did not bond well with the wall and began to deteriorate within his own lifetime. Restorers have spent years trying to save what is left.

Leonardo died in France in 1519. After his death, his pages were scattered among collectors, and many of his discoveries stayed hidden for centuries, so other scientists had to make them again. About seven thousand pages survive. They show a meticulous observer who checked every detail, yet also a dreamer who asked "why?" about everything. Leonardo's real lesson may be this: art and science are not opposites. Both begin with looking closely and wondering.

Model answer: The author means that the same habits, looking closely and asking questions, helped Leonardo in both art and science. To paint people well, he dissected bodies and learned how muscles, the spine and the heart really work, and those drawings were so accurate that some of his ideas were confirmed only in modern times. His painting technique, sfumato, came from studying how light falls on curved surfaces. Even his inventions began as careful sketches. So for Leonardo, drawing was a way of doing science, and science made his art better.
