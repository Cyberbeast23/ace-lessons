✍️ Writing Week 44: Biographical Sketch — A Life That Changed the World 🎨📜

Yesterday you wrote a public service announcement. Today, Ace, you'll write a biographical sketch: a short, true piece of writing about one real person's life. A biography is more than a list of dates. A great biographer chooses the moments that matter, backs them up with facts, and helps the reader understand why this person is still worth remembering. You just read about Leonardo da Vinci; now it's your turn to be the biographer.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong biographical sketch 📜

A biographical sketch tells a life mostly in time order, but it is built around one big idea about the person (your controlling idea), like "Leonardo's curiosity connected art and science."

1. Lead: a vivid moment, image or surprising fact that pulls the reader in, plus your controlling idea
2. Early life: when and where the person was born, and what shaped them as a young person
3. Turning point: the moment or choice that set their life in a new direction
4. Achievement with evidence: what they did, with exact facts (dates, numbers, names of works)
5. Challenge or setback: a problem, failure or obstacle and how they responded
6. Legacy: why the person still matters today, tied back to your controlling idea

Four tricks biographers use

  • Choose, don't list: pick 3–4 key moments instead of every event.
  • Time transitions: At fourteen…, A few years later…, By 1503…, After his death… keep the reader on the timeline.
  • Show character through actions: instead of "he was curious," show him watching water swirl around rocks.
  • Stay fair and accurate: separate facts from legends ("Legend says…") and never invent quotations.

Biographer's rule: every paragraph should help prove your controlling idea about the person.

Mini-lesson: appositives and verb tense 🔧

An appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun beside it. When it adds extra information, set it off with commas: Leonardo, a left-handed artist, wrote from right to left. / He trained with Verrocchio, a master painter in Florence.

Verb tense: tell finished events in the past tense (He painted the Mona Lisa.). Use the present tense only for things that are still true today (The painting hangs in the Louvre. His notebooks show his curiosity.). Don't switch tenses in the middle of an event.

❌ In 1466 Leonardo moves to Florence and joined a workshop.
✅ In 1466 Leonardo moved to Florence and joined a workshop.

Dates bonus: put a comma between the day and the year, and after the year in the middle of a sentence: On April 15, 1452, Leonardo was born.

Model biographical sketch (about 190 words)

Lead + controlling ideaEarly life + turning pointAchievement + challengeLegacy

Imagine a notebook where a flying machine, a human heart and a shopping list share one page, all written backward. Its owner, Leonardo da Vinci, proved that curiosity can connect art and science. Leonardo was born in 1452 near Vinci, a small town in Italy. At about fourteen, he became an apprentice to Andrea del Verrocchio, a master artist in Florence. In that busy workshop, he learned to grind pigments and draw faces, but he also began asking how the world worked. Leonardo painted the Mona Lisa, which now hangs in the Louvre, using soft blending called sfumato. To draw bodies accurately, he dissected them and sketched the spine and heart. Not everything worked: The Last Supper began to flake because his experimental paint did not bond with the wall. Today, about seven thousand pages of his notes survive. They remind us that looking closely and asking "why?" is the first step for both an artist and a scientist.

Notice: the lead starts with a picture and ends with the controlling idea; appositives (a small town in Italy, a master artist in Florence) add facts smoothly; finished events are in the past tense, but "hangs" and "survive" are present because they are still true; the ending connects back to curiosity.
