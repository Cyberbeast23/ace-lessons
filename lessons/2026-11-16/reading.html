Real science is not just a pile of facts; it is a chain of thinking: a question → a hypothesis (a possible answer that can be tested) → a prediction ("If my hypothesis is right, then I should find…") → a test → evidence → a conclusion (which new evidence can still change!). From Fins to Feet: The Prediction That Found Tiktaalik

Hold out your arm and wiggle your fingers. Inside your arm is a pattern of bones: one bone in your upper arm, two bones in your forearm, a cluster of little bones in your wrist, and then your fingers. Here is the surprising part. A whale's flipper, a bat's wing, a frog's front leg and a dog's paw all have the very same pattern. Scientists think this is because all of these animals inherited it from a shared ancestor, a creature that lived long, long ago. So where did the pattern come from in the first place? The answer, it turns out, was waiting inside a fish fossil in the frozen rocks of Canada's Arctic.

In the 1990s, a palaeontologist named Neil Shubin and his colleagues Ted Daeschler and Farish Jenkins were puzzling over a gap in the fossil record. They knew about fish with thick, fleshy, "lobed" fins that lived about 380 million years ago. They also knew about some of the earliest tetrapods, animals with four limbs and toes, which lived about 365 million years ago. But what came in between? The team's hypothesis was that there must once have been a transitional creature: an animal partway between a fish and a four-legged animal, with some features of each.

A hypothesis is only useful if you can test it, so the team made a bold prediction. If that in-between animal had existed, its fossils should be found in rocks of the right age, the right type and the right place. The age: about 375 million years old, between the fish and the tetrapods. The type: sedimentary rock that formed from the mud and sand of shallow streams, because that is where a fish might start to explore the water's edge. The place: somewhere those rocks were exposed at the surface, not buried under soil, forests or cities.

Searching through geology maps, the scientists found rocks that matched all three clues on Ellesmere Island, in what is now Nunavut. Canadian geologists had already mapped the area, and the maps showed thick layers of Devonian stream rocks lying bare in the Arctic hills. It was one of the most remote places on Earth, about 1 500 km from the North Pole. If the team's thinking was right, the fossil should be there. If they searched carefully and found nothing like it, they would have to rethink their idea.

Testing the prediction was not easy. The team could only work during the short Arctic summer, when the Sun never sets and the temperature can still drop below freezing. A small plane dropped them off with their tents, food and tools, and they had to watch for polar bears while they worked. Their first expedition, in 1999, turned up plenty of fish scales and bones, but not the animal they were looking for. They came back in 2000, and again in 2002. Each summer they learned more about which rock layers to search, and each summer they flew home without the creature they hoped to find.

Then, in July 2004, on their fourth trip, a member of the team spotted a flat, triangular snout poking out of the rock. Over the following weeks and months, the team uncovered several skeletons. When the fossils were carefully cleaned in the laboratory, the scientists could hardly believe what they saw. The animal had scales and fins like a fish. But it also had a flat head with eyes on top, like a crocodile, and something no fish before it had: a neck, so it could turn its head without moving its whole body. Its ribs were sturdy enough to help support its body, and it seems to have had lungs as well as gills, so it could probably gulp air.

The most exciting discovery was inside its front fins. Under the fin rays were bones that matched the pattern in your arm: one bone, then two bones, then a set of small wrist-like bones. Tiktaalik had a shoulder, an elbow and a simple wrist inside a fish fin! It probably could prop itself up on the bottom of a shallow stream, almost like doing a push-up, and peek above the water. Its body was a mosaic, a mix of old fish features and new ones that would later help animals live on land. Each new feature was an adaptation that helped it survive in warm, shallow, swampy water.

The scientists asked Inuit Elders in Nunavut to help name the creature. They chose Tiktaalik (tik-TAH-lik), the Inuktitut word for a large freshwater fish. The discovery was announced to the world in 2006, and it quickly became one of the most famous fossils ever found.

Does this mean Tiktaalik was our great-great-great-grandparent? Not exactly. Scientists think of it more like a cousin of our ancestors: a member of the family that shows us what those ancestors were probably like. And science keeps moving. In 2010, researchers in Poland reported tetrapod footprints in rock that is about 395 million years old, older than Tiktaalik. If those tracks really were made by four-legged animals, then tetrapods were already walking around before Tiktaalik lived. This new evidence does not erase what Tiktaalik teaches; it shows that the story of fins becoming feet may have started even earlier and was more complicated than anyone thought.

Still, the hunt for Tiktaalik remains a perfect example of how science works. The team did not find their fossil by luck. They asked a question, made a hypothesis, used it to predict exactly where to look, and then tested that prediction in one of the harshest places on the planet. So the next time you wiggle your fingers, remember: the bones inside them have a story that stretches back 375 million years, to a fish in a stream that is now a frozen hillside in Nunavut.
