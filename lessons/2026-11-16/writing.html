✍️ Writing Week 50: Dichotomous Classification Key — Who's Who in the Devonian Stream? 🐟🔑

Yesterday you wrote a reflective essay and thought on the page. Today, Ace, you'll write a brand-new genre that scientists, park rangers and museum curators use every day: a dichotomous key (dy-KOT-uh-mus). Dichotomous comes from Greek words meaning "cut in two." A dichotomous key helps someone identify a living thing (or a fossil!) by answering a series of either/or choices: Does it have a backbone, or not? Does it have a neck, or not? Each choice splits the group in two, until only one name is left. Writing a good key is harder than it looks: every word must be precise, every choice must be a true opposite, and a stranger must be able to follow it without you there to explain. Then you'll add a short rationale that explains your thinking, just like a real scientist.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong classification key 🔑

A key works like a path through a forest that keeps forking: at every fork, you choose one of two trails, and each trail leads either to the next fork or to a name. Scientists call each pair of choices a couplet (1a and 1b).

1. Title + purpose statement: who will use the key, and what it helps them identify
2. Specimen list: every animal (or object) the key can identify
3. Couplets: numbered pairs of opposite choices (1a / 1b) that end in a number to go to, or a name
4. Trait glossary: a sentence that explains any tricky science word used in the key
5. Test run: walk one specimen through the key, step by step, to prove it works
6. Rationale: explain why you chose these traits and put them in this order

Five rules key-writers follow

  • True opposites: 1a and 1b must be about the same trait. ✅ Has a neck / Has no neck. ❌ Has a neck / Has sharp teeth (an animal could have both!).
  • Observable traits only: choose things you can see in the specimen or fossil. ❌ "Is friendly," "looks cool," "was fast." ✅ "Has bony plates covering its head."
  • Precise words and metric measurements: ❌ "Is big." ✅ "Body longer than 1 m."
  • Start broad, then narrow: the first couplet should split the group using the biggest, easiest difference. Save the trickiest differences for last.
  • Count your couplets: a key for n specimens always needs n − 1 couplets. Five animals → four couplets. (Math to the rescue!)

Key-writer's rule: a stranger holding your key, and nothing else, should end up at the right name every single time.

Mini-lesson: grammar for precise science writing 🔬

Parallel structure: items that go together should be built the same way. ✅ Tiktaalik could swim, breathe air and prop itself up. ❌ Tiktaalik could swim, breathing air, and to prop itself up. In a key, start both halves of a couplet the same way: Has… / Has no…

Hyphens in compound adjectives: when two or more words work together to describe a noun that comes after them, join them with hyphens: a lobe-finned fish, a four-legged animal, a 375-million-year-old fossil. After the noun, no hyphens: The fossil is 375 million years old.

Scientific names: the first word (the genus) gets a capital letter; the second word (the species) does not: Tiktaalik roseae. In print they go in italics. Names of places stay capitalized: Ellesmere Island, Nunavut, the Arctic.

Fewer vs. less: use fewer for things you can count (fewer bones, fewer couplets) and less for things you measure (less mud, less water). Canadian spelling: armour, colour, grey, metre, centre.

Who's Who in the Devonian Stream: A Key to Five Animals
This key helps a museum visitor name five animals from a 375-million-year-old stream by looking only at their bodies. The five animals are a sea scorpion, a placoderm, Eusthenopteron, Tiktaalik and Acanthostega.
5 animals→Couplet 1→Couplet 2→Couplet 3→Couplet 4→1 name
1a. Has a backbone ………… go to 2
1b. Has no backbone; body covered by a jointed outer shell ………… sea scorpion
2a. Has thick, bony armour plates covering its head and chest ………… placoderm
2b. Has no armour plates on its head and chest ………… go to 3
3a. Has limbs with toes ………… Acanthostega
3b. Has fins ………… go to 4
4a. Has a neck and a flat head with eyes on top ………… Tiktaalik
4b. Has no neck; eyes are on the sides of its head ………… Eusthenopteron A lobe fin is a fleshy fin with bones inside it, almost like a tiny arm. Let's test the key with Tiktaalik: it has a backbone (1a), no armour plates (2b), fins instead of toes (3b), and a neck with eyes on top of its flat head (4a). The key leads straight to its name. I started with the backbone because it is the biggest and easiest difference to see, and it splits the animals the same way scientists do. I saved the neck for last because only a careful look tells Eusthenopteron and Tiktaalik apart, and that tiny difference is exactly what shows fins beginning to change into feet.

Notice: the title and purpose say who the key is for; five animals need exactly four couplets; every couplet is a true either/or about something you can see; both halves start the same way (Has… / Has no…), which is parallel structure; every path ends at exactly one name; the glossary explains a tricky term; the test run proves the key works; and the rationale explains the writer's choices. Look for the hyphens in 375-million-year-old and the italic scientific names.
