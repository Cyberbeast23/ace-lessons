🐟 Math Week 50: From Fins to Feet: Fossil Timekeeper's Math Lab

Ace, about 375 million years ago, a strange fish with a flat head, a neck and sturdy, bony fins was poking around the shallow streams of a warm, swampy land. Today its bones lie in the frozen rocks of Ellesmere Island, Nunavut, in Canada's High Arctic. Its name is Tiktaalik (tik-TAH-lik), a word suggested by Inuit elders, and it shows how fish slowly evolved into the first four-legged animals. Today you are a fossil timekeeper on the expedition that found it. You'll use timeline scale models, negative numbers on a timeline, half-lives (repeated halving), rates of rock build-up, Venn diagrams for sorting animals, a fraction of a fraction, scale drawings that shrink and enlarge, surface area of a rectangular prism, coordinates in all four quadrants, order of operations, percents of Earth's history, and counting pairs.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your fossil timekeeper's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (mm, cm, m, m², cm², kg, mg, years, million years, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change metres to centimetres or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A fish fossil is about a metre long, not a kilometre!

Worked Example 1: A timeline scale model

Earth is about 4.6 billion years old (4 600 million years). Squeeze that onto a 46 m rope, and every metre stands for 4 600 ÷ 46 = 100 million years.

Something that happened 250 million years ago sits 250 ÷ 100 = 2.5 m from the "today" end of the rope.

Worked Example 2: "Years ago" as negative numbers

On a timeline, today is 0, so 300 million years ago is −300 (million). The time between −300 and −260 is −260 − (−300) = 40 million years.

What fraction of the way from −300 to −260 is −290? It is 10 of the 40 steps along, so 10/40 = 1/4 of the way.

Worked Example 3: Half-life (repeated halving)

Some atoms in rocks slowly change into other atoms, like a clock ticking. A half-life is the time it takes for half of them to change. Scientists use these "rock clocks" to find the age of ancient rocks.

Start with 40 mg. After 1 half-life: 20 mg. After 2: 10 mg. After 3: 5 mg. Each half-life, divide by 2.

Worked Example 4: Rates (how fast rock layers build up)

If mud piles up 3 cm every 1 000 years, then a layer 12 cm thick took 12 ÷ 3 = 4 groups of 1 000 years = 4 000 years. Always make the units match first (change metres to centimetres!).

Worked Example 5: Venn diagrams for sorting (classification)

20 animals: 12 can swim, 9 can fly, and 4 can do both. If you add 12 + 9, you count the 4 "both" animals twice! So swim or fly = 12 + 9 − 4 = 17, and neither = 20 − 17 = 3.

Worked Example 6: A fraction of a fraction

"Of" means multiply. 2/3 of 3/4 = (2 × 3)/(3 × 4) = 6/12 = 1/2. Multiply tops, multiply bottoms, then simplify.

Worked Example 7: Scale drawings (shrink and enlarge)

Scientific illustrators draw at a scale. At 1:10, the drawing is 10 times smaller: a 150 cm skeleton is drawn 150 ÷ 10 = 15 cm long.

At 4:1, the drawing is 4 times bigger: a tooth drawn 2 cm long is really 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5 cm (5 mm).

Worked Example 8: Surface area of a rectangular prism

A box has 6 faces in 3 matching pairs. Surface area = 2 × (l × w + l × h + w × h).

Box 10 cm × 5 cm × 2 cm: 2 × (50 + 20 + 10) = 2 × 80 = 160 cm².

Worked Example 9: Coordinates in all four quadrants

A point (x, y) goes x left or right, then y up or down. Negative x = left of the centre, negative y = below it.

From (−3, 1) to (4, 1) the y-values match, so the distance is 4 − (−3) = 7 units.

Worked Example 10: Order of operations

Brackets first, then × and ÷, then + and −. 100 − (3 × 20 + 15) = 100 − (60 + 15) = 100 − 75 = 25.

Worked Example 11: Counting pairs

To compare every animal with every other animal once: each of n animals pairs with (n − 1) others, but that counts every pair twice. Pairs = n × (n − 1) ÷ 2. For 4 animals: 4 × 3 ÷ 2 = 6 pairs.
