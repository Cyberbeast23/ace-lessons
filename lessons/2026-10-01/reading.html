Bones in the Rock

Sixty-six million years ago, a world of giants walked the Earth. Towering predators such as Tyrannosaurus rex hunted across plains, while plant-eating giants like Triceratops chewed tough ferns. Then something enormous changed the planet. Scientists think a huge asteroid slammed into what is now Mexico, darkening the skies and chilling the climate. Many dinosaurs — though not birds, which are living dinosaur relatives — became extinct. Their bodies, eggs, footprints, and even droppings sometimes became fossils: remains or traces locked into rock for ages.

Fossils do not form every day. Most dead animals rot or are eaten. For a bone to fossilize, it usually needs to be buried quickly under mud, sand, or ash so scavengers and weather cannot destroy it. Over millions of years, minerals from groundwater seep into the bone and harden it, turning it into a kind of rock copy. That is why a fossil can feel heavy and cold, more like stone than living tissue. Soft parts such as skin and organs almost never survive, which is why palaeontologists must be careful detectives.

The age of the dinosaurs spans three periods of the Mesozoic Era: Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous. During the Jurassic, famous plant-eaters such as Stegosaurus lumbered through forests, while long-necked sauropods reached for high leaves. Later, in the Cretaceous, T. rex and Triceratops shared landscapes in western North America. Canada has its own dinosaur treasure: Alberta's badlands, especially near Drumheller, hold some of the richest Cretaceous fossil beds on Earth. Visitors can see skeletons in museums and walk rocky valleys where bones still weather out of the cliffs.

Not every dinosaur was a meat-eating predator. Many were herbivores that ate plants, and some were omnivores. Teeth tell the story. Sharp, serrated teeth suggest a carnivore. Flat grinding teeth suggest a plant-eater. Horns, frills, and armour plates were not just decoration; they could scare rivals, attract mates, or protect against bites. Scientists also study trackways — lines of fossil footprints — to learn how dinosaurs walked, whether they travelled in herds, and how fast they might have moved.

Modern palaeontology is teamwork. Dig crews carefully excavate sites with brushes, chisels, and plaster jackets that protect fragile bones for the trip to the lab. In the lab, preparators chip away rock grain by grain. Then researchers compare bones, measure joints, and build digital models. Sometimes a new fossil overturns an old idea — for example, when feathered dinosaurs from China showed that many meat-eaters were more birdlike than anyone once imagined.

Why does any of this matter to a future doctor like you, Ace? Palaeontology trains the same habits medicine needs: patient observation, careful measurement, and the courage to change your mind when better evidence appears. A fossil is a clue, not a full video of the past. Scientists ask questions, test ideas, and admit what they do not yet know. That scientific mindset — curious, careful, and honest — is exactly what good doctors practise every day.

The next time you stand under a towering museum skeleton, look past the drama of giant teeth. Think of the quiet work: a bone buried in mud, minerals seeping in, a dig team brushing dust for hours, a scientist redrawing a family tree. Dinosaurs are gone, but their story is still being written, one fossil at a time. And every careful reader of rocks — just like every careful reader of books — helps the truth come into clearer focus.
