🧪 Math Week 26: Kitchen Chemistry Lab

Ace, your kitchen is secretly a chemistry lab! Every time someone bakes a cake, squeezes a lemon, or drops baking soda into vinegar, chemistry is happening. Real chemists use math all the time: How much of each ingredient? How strong is this solution? How much gas did the reaction make? Today you'll work like a lab chemist with ratios and recipe scaling, millilitres and litres, percent concentration, the pH scale (where every step is 10 times stronger), reading a line graph, rates, the mean, dilution, fractions, conservation of mass, volume, and solving an equation.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your lab checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (mL with mL, g with g)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Scaling a recipe (ratios)

A ratio compares two amounts. If a recipe uses 2 mL of food colouring for every 50 mL of water, then for 200 mL of water (4 times as much), you need 2 × 4 = 8 mL of colouring. Find the multiplier, then use it on both parts.

Worked Example 2: Millilitres and litres

1 L = 1000 mL. To change litres to millilitres, multiply by 1000: 2.5 L = 2500 mL. To change millilitres to litres, divide by 1000: 750 mL = 0.75 L.

Also useful: 1 cm³ holds exactly 1 mL. A box 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm holds 1000 cm³ = 1000 mL = 1 L.

Worked Example 3: Percent concentration

A solution is one substance dissolved in another. Its concentration tells you how much of it is the dissolved stuff. A drink that is 10% juice has 10 mL of juice in every 100 mL.

In 300 mL of that drink: 10% of 300 = 0.10 × 300 = 30 mL of juice.

Going the other way: 8 g of salt dissolved in 32 g of water makes 8 + 32 = 40 g of solution, and 8 ÷ 40 × 100 = 20% salt. (Divide by the whole solution, not just the water!)

Worked Example 4: The pH scale (powers of 10)

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14. 7 is neutral (pure water). Below 7 is acidic; above 7 is basic (also called alkaline).

Here's the amazing part: each step is 10 times stronger. pH 5 is 10 times more acidic than pH 6. pH 4 is 10 × 10 = 100 times more acidic than pH 6. Count the steps, then multiply that many 10s together: 3 steps = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 times.

Worked Example 5: Reading a line graph and finding a rate

To find how much something changed between two times, read both values from the graph and subtract: if a balloon held 20 mL at 5 s and 50 mL at 15 s, it gained 50 − 20 = 30 mL.

A rate is a change divided by the time it took: 30 mL in 10 s is 30 ÷ 10 = 3 mL per second.

Worked Example 6: Dilution in parts

"Mix 1 part juice concentrate with 4 parts water" means the whole drink has 1 + 4 = 5 parts. To make 1000 mL, one part = 1000 ÷ 5 = 200 mL, so use 200 mL concentrate and 800 mL water.

Worked Example 7: Conservation of mass and equations

In a chemical reaction, atoms are never created or destroyed, so the total mass stays the same. If a reaction makes a gas that floats away, the leftover mass is smaller by exactly the mass of the gas. Start with 50 g, end with 48.5 g: 1.5 g of gas escaped.

Solving an equation: "A number times 2, plus 15, is 75" becomes 2n + 15 = 75. Undo each step in reverse: subtract 15 (2n = 60), then divide by 2 (n = 30).
