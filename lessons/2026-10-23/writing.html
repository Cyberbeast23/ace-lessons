✍️ Writing Week 26: Science Lab Report: My Kitchen Chemistry Experiment 🧪📋

Yesterday you wrote a diary as someone living through history. Today, Ace, you'll write like a real scientist: a lab report. Earlier this week you wrote instructions that tell someone how to do something. A lab report goes further: it records what you wanted to find out, what you predicted, what you did, what actually happened, and what it means. Scientists all over the world share their discoveries this way, so another scientist can read the report, repeat the experiment, and check the results.

Part 1: The Lesson

A lab report has six parts 🧩

Think of it as the story of an experiment, told honestly and clearly, in order.

1. Question: what you want to find out, like Which kitchen liquids are acids, and which are bases?
2. Hypothesis: your prediction and your reason: I predict... because...
3. Materials + method: what you used (with amounts) and the steps you followed, clearly enough for someone to repeat
4. Observations: what you noticed with your senses (colours, bubbles, sounds, smells), but never taste!
5. Results: the measurements and data, often in a table (mL, cm, seconds, pH)
6. Conclusion: the answer to your question, whether your hypothesis was supported, and what you'd change next time

Five scientist's moves

  • Be exact: instead of "some vinegar," write "50 mL of vinegar." Write metric units the proper way: 50 mL, 5 g, 3 cm (a space, no period).
  • Use the past tense for what you did and saw: "I poured... the mixture fizzed... the juice turned pink."
  • Keep it a fair test: change only one thing (the variable) and keep everything else the same, like the same amount of liquid in the same size cup.
  • Separate what you saw from what it means: "The juice turned pink" is an observation. "So lemon juice is an acid" belongs in the conclusion.
  • Be honest: if your hypothesis was wrong, say so! A surprise result is still a discovery. Real scientists are wrong all the time, and that's how science moves forward.

Grown-up words: the thing you change on purpose is the independent variable; what you measure is the dependent variable; everything you keep the same are the controlled variables. A cup with no added substance, to compare against, is called a control.

Model Lab Report (short version)

Question + hypothesisMaterials, method + observationsResultsConclusion

Question: Which kitchen liquids are acids, and which are bases? Hypothesis: I predict that lemon juice and vinegar will be acids, because they taste sour, and that baking soda water will be a base.
Method: With Dad's help, I soaked chopped red cabbage in hot water for 15 minutes and strained out the purple juice. I poured 50 mL of each liquid into four identical clear cups and added 10 mL of cabbage juice to each one. I wore safety goggles. Observations: The lemon juice turned bright pink right away, and the vinegar turned reddish pink. The plain water stayed purple. The baking soda water turned blue-green, and tiny bubbles clung to the sides of the cup.
Results: Lemon juice: pink (acid). Vinegar: red-pink (acid). Water: purple (neutral). Baking soda water: blue-green (base).
Conclusion: My hypothesis was supported. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids, water is neutral, and baking soda water is a base. Next time, I will test milk and soap to see where they fit on the colour scale.

Notice: the question is clear and testable; the hypothesis gives a reason ("because they taste sour"); the method has exact amounts (50 mL, 10 mL, 15 minutes) and a safety step; the observations describe colours and bubbles in the past tense; the results are short and organized; and the conclusion answers the question, says whether the hypothesis was supported, and looks ahead.
