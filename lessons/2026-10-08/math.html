🌋 Math Week 11: Volcanoes & Earthquakes Lab

Grab your hard hat, Ace! Today you'll think like a volcanologist and a seismologist (scientists who study volcanoes and earthquakes). British Columbia sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, so this maths matters close to home. You'll level up with unit rates and unit conversions, integers and temperature differences, powers of 10 (the magnitude scale), distance = speed × time with seismic waves, volume and mass, ratios and percents, reading a line graph of earthquake counts, and equations with brackets.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your scientist's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right unit?

Worked Example 1: Unit rates and converting units

A unit rate tells you how much happens in one unit, like metres per one minute.

A lava flow moves 240 m in 20 minutes. 240 ÷ 20 = 12 m per minute.

In one hour: 12 × 60 = 720 m. Change to kilometres: 1 km = 1000 m, so 720 m = 0.72 km per hour.

Worked Example 2: Integers and temperature differences

The difference between two temperatures is higher − lower. If lava is 1100 °C and the snowy summit air is −12 °C, the difference is 1100 − (−12) = 1100 + 12 = 1112 °C.

Underground, rock gets hotter as you go deeper (about 25 °C warmer for every kilometre in many places). Starting at 10 °C, 4 km down is about 10 + 4 × 25 = 110 °C.

Worked Example 3: Powers of 10 and the magnitude scale

Each step up the earthquake magnitude scale means the ground shakes about 10 times as much. From magnitude 3 to magnitude 5 is two steps: 10 × 10 = 102 = 100 times the shaking.

The energy released grows even faster: about 32 times for each step. Two steps: 32 × 32 = 1024 times the energy!

Worked Example 4: Distance, speed, and time with seismic waves

time = distance ÷ speed. Earthquakes send out fast P-waves (about 6 km/s) and slower S-waves (about 3 km/s here). For a city 120 km away: P arrives after 120 ÷ 6 = 20 s and S after 120 ÷ 3 = 40 s.

The gap between them (the S − P lag) is 40 − 20 = 20 s. Seismologists use this lag to work out how far away an earthquake was.

Worked Example 5: Percent change, graphs, and equations with brackets

Percent change = change ÷ starting amount × 100. From 40 earthquakes a day to 50: change 10, so 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = 25% increase. On a line graph, the steepest part shows the fastest change.

Equation with brackets: 3x + 2(x + 5) = 40. Expand: 3x + 2x + 10 = 40 → 5x + 10 = 40 → 5x = 30 → x = 6. Check: 18 + 2 × 11 = 18 + 22 = 40 ✔
