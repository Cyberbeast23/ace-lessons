Fire and Shaking on the Ring of Fire

Look at a map of the Pacific Ocean and trace its edges with your finger: up the coast of South America, past California, along British Columbia and Alaska, across to Japan, and down toward New Zealand. You have just followed the Ring of Fire, a giant horseshoe where most of the world's earthquakes happen and where hundreds of volcanoes rise. British Columbia sits right on this ring. The calm, snowy mountains you see from the highway are not as quiet as they look.

To understand why, you need to know that Earth's outer shell is not one solid piece. It is cracked into enormous slabs called tectonic plates, which drift a few centimetres each year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Just off the coast of Vancouver Island, a small plate called the Juan de Fuca plate is pushing against the much larger North American plate. Because the ocean plate is heavier, it slides underneath, sinking slowly into the hot interior of the Earth. This process is called subduction, and it causes two kinds of trouble. First, the sinking plate carries water down with it, which helps nearby rock melt into magma. The magma is lighter than the solid rock around it, so it rises, and some of it eventually bursts out through volcanoes. Second, the plates do not slide smoothly. They lock together, bend, and store up energy for hundreds of years, until they suddenly slip and release it as an earthquake.

The most famous eruption in this region happened at Mount St. Helens in Washington State, about 300 km south of the BC border. In the spring of 1980, small earthquakes shook the mountain, and a huge bulge began to swell on its north side as magma pushed up from below. Scientists set up instruments to monitor the volcano day and night, and officials ordered people to evacuate the area around it. On the morning of May 18, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake shook loose the bulging north side, causing the largest landslide ever recorded. With the weight suddenly removed, the trapped gas and magma exploded sideways. The devastating blast flattened forests for hundreds of square kilometres, and ash fell across eleven states. Fifty-seven people died, including a young volcanologist named David Johnston, whose last radio message to his team was "Vancouver! Vancouver! This is it!" (His team's base was in Vancouver, Washington.) Without the warnings and closures, many more people would have been in danger.

Canada has volcanoes too. Mount Garibaldi, near Squamish, is dormant: it has not erupted for thousands of years, but it could wake up one day. Mount Meager, north of Pemberton, exploded about 2400 years ago and spread ash as far as Alberta. In northern BC, a volcano called the Tseax Cone erupted only about 300 years ago. Its lava flows buried Nisga'a villages, and the Nisga'a people still tell the story of that disaster today.

The biggest danger, though, may be an earthquake. Along the Cascadia Subduction Zone, where the two plates meet, scientists have found drowned "ghost forests" whose trees died when the coast suddenly dropped and salt water flooded in. Several First Nations on Vancouver Island have long-told oral histories of a terrible shaking and a great flood of the sea. Meanwhile, careful records kept in Japan describe a mysterious tsunami that struck in January 1700 with no earthquake felt there at all. These separate clues corroborate each other. Together, they show that a giant earthquake, about magnitude 9, struck Cascadia on the night of January 26, 1700, and sent a tsunami all the way across the Pacific.

Geologists say another great Cascadia earthquake is inevitable. No one can say whether it will happen next week or hundreds of years from now, but we can be ready. Today, seismographs, GPS stations, and gas sensors watch the plates and volcanoes of BC around the clock, and new early-warning systems can send an alert seconds before strong shaking arrives. Engineers build schools and bridges to sway instead of crumble. And every October, students across the province practise the most important earthquake skill of all: Drop, Cover, and Hold On. The ground beneath us is restless, but people who understand it are far safer living on top of it.

Model answer: One kind of evidence came from the coast itself: scientists found ghost forests whose trees died when the land suddenly dropped and salt water flooded in. Another kind came from people: First Nations on Vancouver Island tell oral histories of a great shaking and flood, and written records in Japan describe a tsunami in January 1700 that arrived with no earthquake felt there. Having several kinds of evidence is more convincing because each clue could have another explanation on its own, but when the trees, the stories, and the Japanese records all point to the same event, it is very unlikely they are all wrong.
