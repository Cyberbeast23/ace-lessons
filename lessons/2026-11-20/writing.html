✍️ Writing Week 54: FAQ Page — Avalanche Safety Questions & Answers ❓🏔️

Yesterday you wrote a ballad, a story meant to be sung. Today, Ace, you'll write one of the most useful kinds of writing on the internet: an FAQ page. FAQ stands for Frequently Asked Questions. Parks, hospitals, airlines, games and schools all have one. An FAQ writer has to do something tricky: guess what readers will wonder, then answer each question quickly, clearly and accurately, so a busy reader can find what they need in seconds. Avalanche Canada, ski resorts and Parks Canada all publish safety FAQs, and on a mountain, a clear answer can save a life. Today's genre is the FAQ page: questions in the reader's own words, with direct, precise answers.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong FAQ ❓

An FAQ is built from a title and a set of question-and-answer blocks. Each answer has its own small structure.

1. Title and audience line: what the FAQ is about and who it's for ("…for families planning a first winter hike")
2. Reader's question: written the way a real reader would ask it, ending with a question mark
3. Direct answer first: the very first sentence answers the question (Yes. / No. / Usually not, if…)
4. Explanation with a reason or fact: one or two sentences that tell why, using a true fact or number
5. Example or comparison: a picture in the reader's mind that makes the answer stick
6. Where to learn more: a trustworthy place to get more help (a website, a ranger, a course)

Five habits of great FAQ writers

  • Think like the reader: list the questions a beginner would really ask ("Do I need special gear?"), not the ones an expert finds interesting.
  • Put the answer first: ❌ "Snow is a complicated material made of crystals that…" ✅ "No. Most groomed ski runs are protected by ski patrol." Busy readers may only read your first sentence.
  • Be precise, not scary or careless: use the qualifiers you practised in reading. ❌ "Gentle slopes are always safe." ✅ "Gentle slopes rarely slide, unless a steeper slope rises above them."
  • Order the questions: start with the most common or most basic question, and group related questions together.
  • Keep answers short: about 2 to 4 sentences each. If an answer needs a whole page, it's probably two questions.

FAQ writer's rule: read only the first sentence of each answer. If those sentences alone answer the questions, your FAQ is working.

Mini-lesson: grammar for questions and answers ❓

Question word order: in most questions, a helping verb comes before the subject: Why do avalanches happen? (not Why avalanches happen?). Can I ski here? Is the trail open? Should we turn back? Every direct question ends with a question mark.

Direct vs. indirect questions: an indirect question is a statement about a question, so it ends with a period and uses normal word order: I wonder whether the trail is safe. Dad asked why we turned back.

Parallel lists: items in a list should match in form. ❌ Get training, the forecast, and having gear. ✅ Get the training, get the forecast and get the gear.

No comma splices: a comma alone cannot join two complete sentences. ❌ Snow fell all night, the danger rose. ✅ Snow fell all night, so the danger rose. or Snow fell all night; the danger rose.

Canadian spelling: centre, metre, colour, travelled, and watch this pair: practice is the noun (avalanche practice) and practise is the verb (we practise searches), just like advice / advise.

Model FAQ: "Winter Mountain Safety FAQ for Kids" (about 200 words)

Winter Mountain Safety FAQ
Answers for kids who love snow and want to stay safe

Can an avalanche happen on a ski hill?
It's very unlikely on open, marked runs. Ski patrollers check the snow every morning and sometimes set off small avalanches on purpose before the lifts open. It's like a crossing guard who checks the road before you cross.

Why are steep slopes dangerous?
Most avalanches start on slopes between about 30° and 45°. That's about as steep as a playground slide, so if it looks like a slide, think twice.

What should I carry in the backcountry?
Carry a transceiver, a probe and a shovel, and practise using them. Friends can dig a buried person out much faster than rescuers can arrive.

Is it safe to go alone?
No. A partner is your best rescuer. Even experts travel in groups.

Want to learn more? Check the daily avalanche forecast before every trip, and ask about a youth avalanche safety course.

Notice: every answer starts with a direct answer (It's very unlikely… / Most avalanches… / Carry… / No.). Each one adds a reason and many add a comparison (like a crossing guard, as steep as a playground slide). The questions use correct question word order (Can an avalanche happen…? Why are…? What should I…? Is it…?). Look for the precise qualifiers very unlikely, most, about and sometimes, the parallel list a transceiver, a probe and a shovel, and the verb practise.
