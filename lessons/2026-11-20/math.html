🏔️ Math Week 54: White Thunder: Avalanche Forecaster's Math Lab

Ace, every winter morning, before most people are awake, avalanche forecasters in British Columbia are already at work. They dig pits in the snow, measure temperatures layer by layer, weigh samples of new snow, check weather stations high in the mountains, and then decide how dangerous the slopes will be that day. Skiers, snowmobilers, highway crews and railway workers all depend on their numbers. Today you're on the forecasting team. You'll use complementary angles, snow-to-water ratios, density (mass per cubic metre), volume in cubic metres and tonnes, converting km/h to m/s, temperature gradients, how air cools as you climb, percent of a total, search-pattern distances, the area of a triangle, volume from area × depth, working backward from a percent decrease, probability as a simplified fraction, comparing rates, and the mean of integers.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your forecaster's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (cm, m, km, kg, t, m², m³, km/h, m/s, °C, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change centimetres to metres or km/h to m/s before you calculate.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Snow can't be heavier than water, and a slope can't be steeper than 90°.

Worked Example 1: Complementary angles

Two angles that add up to 90° are complementary. A tree grows straight up (vertical), and the ground is level (horizontal), so they meet at 90°. If a slope is tilted 25° from level, it is 90° − 25° = 65° from the vertical tree trunk.

Worked Example 2: Snow to water

Fluffy new snow is mostly air. If snow is about 10% water, then 20 cm of snow melts down to 20 × 0.1 = 2 cm of water. Forecasters call this the water equivalent.

Worked Example 3: Density

Density tells you how much mass fits in a space. Water is 1 000 kg/m³. New snow might be 100 kg/m³. A layer of snow 0.4 m deep covering 1 m² has a volume of 0.4 m³, so its mass is 0.4 × 100 = 40 kg.

Worked Example 4: Volume and tonnes

A box of snow 10 m × 20 m × 0.5 m has a volume of 10 × 20 × 0.5 = 100 m³. At 300 kg/m³ that's 30 000 kg. Since 1 tonne = 1 000 kg, that's 30 t.

Worked Example 5: km/h to m/s

1 km/h = 1 000 m in 3 600 s. A quick trick: divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s. So 72 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s. At 20 m/s, 300 m takes 300 ÷ 20 = 15 s.

Worked Example 6: A temperature gradient

A gradient is how much something changes over a distance. If the snow surface is −10 °C and the ground under 100 cm of snow is 0 °C, the change is 10 degrees over 100 cm, or 10 ÷ 10 = 1 °C per 10 cm.

Worked Example 7: Air cools as you climb

On a typical day, air cools by about 6.5 °C for every 1 000 m you go up. If it is 5 °C in a town at 500 m, then at 1 500 m (1 000 m higher) it is about 5 − 6.5 = −1.5 °C.

Worked Example 8: Percent of a total

If 36 out of 40 students wore helmets, that's 36 ÷ 40 = 0.9 = 90%.

Worked Example 9: Covering an area in strips

To search a field 50 m wide and 100 m long in strips 10 m wide, you need 50 ÷ 10 = 5 strips. Walking the 100 m length of each strip: 5 × 100 = 500 m. At 1 m/s, that's 500 s, a little over 8 minutes.

Worked Example 10: Area of a triangle, then volume

Area of a triangle = base × height ÷ 2. A triangle with base 40 m and height 30 m has area 40 × 30 ÷ 2 = 600 m². If something covers that area 2 m deep, its volume is 600 × 2 = 1 200 m³.

Worked Example 11: Working backward from a percent decrease

New snow settles (gets shallower) as it packs down. If it settled by 20%, 80% (0.8) is left. If it is now 40 cm deep: original × 0.8 = 40, so original = 40 ÷ 0.8 = 50 cm.

Worked Example 12: Probability as a simplified fraction

If 8 of 20 days were snowy, the probability that a randomly chosen day was snowy is 8/20. Divide the top and bottom by 4: 2/5.

Worked Example 13: Comparing rates ("how many times faster?")

If Team A finishes a job in 15 minutes and Team B takes 90 minutes, Team A is 90 ÷ 15 = 6 times faster.

Worked Example 14: The mean of integers

Mean = total ÷ how many. For −4, −2, 3 and −1 °C: −4 + (−2) + 3 + (−1) = −4, and −4 ÷ 4 = −1 °C. Add negatives carefully!
