📖 Reading Week 54: White Thunder: How Avalanches Happen, and How People Stay Safe

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you combined information from a timeline, a table and the text. Today's skill is one that scientists, doctors, lawyers and safety experts depend on: reading precisely. Careful writers choose small words that change a sentence's meaning a lot. These are called qualifiers and conditions. A careless reader skips them; a precise reader notices them. As you read, ask:
  • 1. How many? All, most, many, some, few and none mean very different things. "Most avalanches" is not "all avalanches."
  • 2. How often? Always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely and never. "Rarely" still means it can happen!
  • 3. How certain? Will is certain; can, may and might mean it's possible. About and roughly mean a number is not exact.
  • 4. Under what condition? Watch for if…then, when, unless and only if. The condition tells you when the statement is true.
  • Word detective: French avalanche comes from an old Alpine word for a slide down a slope · Latin cohaerere = "to stick together" (cohesive, coherent) · Latin stabilis = "firm, steady," so unstable = not firm · Latin conservare = "to keep safe" (conservative, conservation)
  • Words in yellow are vocabulary words. Use the sentence around them (the context) to figure out what they mean.

White Thunder: How Avalanches Happen, and How People Stay Safe

Just before midnight on March 4, 1910, a crew of railway workers was shovelling snow off the Canadian Pacific Railway tracks at Rogers Pass, high in the Selkirk Mountains of British Columbia. An avalanche had buried the tracks earlier that evening, and the men were digging them out by lantern light. Without warning, a second avalanche roared down the opposite side of the valley and swept over them. Fifty-eight men died. It is still the deadliest avalanche in Canadian history. Six years later, the railway opened the Connaught Tunnel, about 8 km long, so that trains could travel under the pass instead of through its most dangerous slopes.

What is an avalanche? An avalanche is a mass of snow that slides quickly down a slope. There are two main kinds. A loose-snow avalanche starts at a single point, often when a small clump of snow tumbles and pulls more snow with it, and it spreads into a fan or teardrop shape. A slab avalanche is different: a whole layer of snow that is cohesive, meaning its grains are stuck together, breaks away all at once, the way a sheet of ice cracks across a frozen pond. Slabs can be hundreds of metres wide. Most avalanches that kill people are slab avalanches.

A book written by the weather. Every snowfall adds a new layer to the snowpack, so a snowpack is a little like a book in which each page records one storm. Some pages are strong. Others are weak. On cold, clear nights, feathery frost crystals called surface hoar can grow on top of the snow; if the next storm buries them, they become a fragile layer that may stay hidden for weeks. When the snow surface is much colder than the ground, sugary crystals called facets can grow inside the snowpack. These grains don't bond well to each other. A strong slab sitting on top of a weak layer is the classic recipe for a slab avalanche.

Three ingredients. For a slab avalanche to happen, three things usually have to come together. First, a slope steep enough to slide. Most big avalanches start on slopes between about 30° and 45°. Slopes gentler than 30° rarely release on their own, but they are not automatically safe: a gentle valley floor can lie in the path of an avalanche that starts high above it. Very steep slopes, above about 50°, often shed snow in small slides before much can build up. Second, an unstable snowpack, with a weak layer that could collapse. Third, a trigger. Natural triggers include heavy snowfall, rain, a sudden warm spell or a falling cornice, an overhanging lip of wind-packed snow along the edge of a ridge. As a rule of thumb, if more than about 30 cm of new snow falls in 24 hours, the danger usually rises. But the most common trigger in avalanche accidents is a person. The weight of a skier, a snowboarder or a snowmobile can collapse a weak layer, and the crack can race across the slope faster than anyone can react. In about nine out of ten avalanche accidents, the avalanche is triggered by the victim or by someone in the victim's group.

The forecasters. Nobody can say exactly when or where a particular avalanche will happen, but forecasters can say how likely avalanches are. Avalanche Canada, based in Revelstoke, publishes forecasts for mountain regions across Western Canada. Its forecasters combine weather data, reports from guides and ski patrollers, and observations from field teams who dig snow pits, measure the temperature of each layer and tap on columns of snow to see whether, and how easily, a weak layer breaks. Each forecast rates the danger on the scale shown in the table, often with different ratings for the alpine, the tree line and the forests below.

Rogers Pass today. More than a century after the 1910 disaster, the Trans-Canada Highway and the railway both cross Rogers Pass, and dozens of avalanche paths line the route. To keep traffic moving, Parks Canada works with the Canadian Armed Forces. When the snowpack becomes dangerous, the highway is closed, and soldiers fire artillery shells at the starting zones to trigger avalanches deliberately, while no one is below. Concrete snowsheds, like roofs over the road, let some slides pass harmlessly overhead. It is one of the largest avalanche control programs in the world.

The race against time. A person buried in an avalanche is in serious danger, mainly because packed snow makes it hard to breathe. Studies of real accidents show that if a buried person is dug out within about 15 minutes, roughly nine in ten survive. After about 35 minutes, the chance of survival drops to about one in three. Professional rescuers usually cannot arrive that fast, so the best rescuers are the buried person's own companions. That's why backcountry travellers carry three tools: an avalanche transceiver, a small radio that sends and receives a signal so friends can find someone under the snow; a long folding probe to pinpoint the person; and a shovel to dig. The gear only helps if people know how to use it, so they practise searches before they need them. Trained avalanche dogs, which find people by scent, help rescue teams too.

Know before you go. Avalanche experts don't tell people to stay out of the mountains. Instead, they teach a simple habit: get the training, get the forecast, get the gear, and never go alone. Snow will always fall on steep slopes, and avalanches will always happen. Whether people are in the way depends mostly on the choices they make.
