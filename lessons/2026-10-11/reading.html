Steel Through the Mountains: Who Built the Canadian Pacific Railway?

In 1871, British Columbia was a small colony on the far side of the Rocky Mountains. Thousands of kilometres of prairie, forest, rock, and ice separated it from the rest of Canada. BC's leaders agreed to join Canada on one condition: a railway had to connect them to the east within ten years. Prime Minister John A. Macdonald accepted the deal, but many people thought the promise was foolish. Laying steel rails across some of the most rugged land on Earth looked like a formidable task, and perhaps an impossible one.

At first, almost nothing happened. A political scandal and a shortage of money delayed the project for years. Then, in 1881, a new company called the Canadian Pacific Railway (CPR) took over, and it soon hired William Van Horne, a tough, energetic manager, to drive the work forward. On the flat prairies, crews laid track at record speed. The mountains were a different story.

In BC, a contractor named Andrew Onderdonk had to carve a route through the Fraser Canyon, where the river roars between cliffs of solid rock. There were not enough workers in the province, so Onderdonk hired thousands of men from China. Most came from Guangdong, a province in southern China where people speak Cantonese. Between 1881 and 1885, about 15 000 Chinese labourers worked on the railway. They blasted tunnels with gunpowder and powerful explosives, chipped ledges into cliff faces, and built stone walls to hold the track in place. The work was perilous. Rockfalls, explosions, drownings, and accidents happened often, and during the long winters many workers became sick with scurvy because they had no fresh vegetables. Historians estimate that at least 600 Chinese workers died, and some believe the true number was much higher. The workers were also treated unfairly. They were usually paid about $1.00 a day, while white workers often earned $1.50 to $2.50, and Chinese workers had to buy their own food and camp supplies out of their smaller wages.

Farther east, engineers faced a different enemy: steepness. A heavy steam locomotive cannot climb or brake safely on a steep slope. In the Rockies, the track down from Kicking Horse Pass was so steep that it was nicknamed the "Big Hill," and runaway trains were a constant fear. In the Selkirk Mountains, a surveyor named Major A. B. Rogers explored a route through a high pass in 1881, which is still called Rogers Pass today. There, the danger came from above. Huge avalanches thundered down the slopes each winter, so the railway later built long wooden snowsheds, like roofs, to shield the track. Solving these problems took remarkable ingenuity, and engineers kept improving the route for decades afterward.

On November 7, 1885, at a place called Craigellachie in BC's Eagle Pass, railway director Donald Smith hammered in the last spike, a plain iron one. A famous photograph captured the moment: a crowd of men in suits and work clothes gathered around him. Not one Chinese worker appears in the picture. The next summer, the first passenger train from Montreal reached the Pacific coast. The railway carried settlers, goods, and mail across the country, and it helped keep BC part of Canada.

For the Chinese workers, the ending was bitter. In 1885, the same year the railway was finished, the government made most Chinese immigrants pay a "head tax" of $50 to enter Canada. By 1903, the tax had risen to $500. Many workers could not afford to bring their wives and children, and families were split apart for years. In 1923, a new law excluded almost all Chinese immigrants until 1947. The railway also crossed the lands of many First Nations, often without their agreement, and the rapid settlement it brought changed their lives forever. In 2006, the Government of Canada formally apologized for the head tax, an apology that acknowledged a great wrong. The railway is a remarkable achievement, but its full story belongs to everyone who paid a price to build it.

Model answer: The author means that the railway was not built only by the famous leaders in the last-spike photograph, but also by workers who were paid less, risked their lives, and were later treated unfairly. To a politician like John A. Macdonald, the last spike meant a promise kept and a country joined from sea to sea. To a Chinese worker, it meant the end of dangerous, poorly paid work, followed by a head tax that kept his family far away. For many First Nations, the railway brought settlers onto their lands without their agreement. I think the full story matters because if we only remember the proud parts, we forget the people who were hurt and we might repeat the same mistakes.
