🚂 Math Week 14: Building the Canadian Pacific Railway Lab

Ace, in 1871 British Columbia agreed to join Canada, but only if Canada promised to build a railway all the way to the Pacific. The Canadian Pacific Railway (CPR) took thousands of workers, including about 15 000 Chinese labourers, years of dangerous work, and a lot of maths. Today you'll think like a railway surveyor and engineer. You'll level up with distance, speed and time over railway distances, changing km/h into m/s, map scales, track gradients as percents, elevation and temperature with integers, wages and percents, percent increase, elapsed years on a timeline, and the mean from a bar graph.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your railway engineer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right unit?

Worked Example 1: Distance, speed and time

The three are linked: distance = speed × time, time = distance ÷ speed, and speed = distance ÷ time. Cover the one you want, and the other two tell you what to do.

A steam train travels at 40 km/h for 2.5 hours: distance = 40 × 2.5 = 100 km.

To cover 180 km at 45 km/h: time = 180 ÷ 45 = 4 hours. To cover 300 km in 6 hours: speed = 300 ÷ 6 = 50 km/h.

Worked Example 2: Changing km/h into m/s

1 km = 1000 m and 1 hour = 60 × 60 = 3600 seconds. So to change km/h into m/s, multiply by 1000 and divide by 3600.

72 km/h = 72 × 1000 ÷ 3600 = 72 000 ÷ 3600 = 20 m/s. (Shortcut: divide by 3.6.)

Worked Example 3: Map scales

Surveyors drew maps before any track was laid. A scale of 1 : 1 000 000 means 1 cm on the map stands for 1 000 000 cm in real life. Since 100 000 cm = 1 km, that is 1 cm = 10 km.

On that map, 6.5 cm stands for 6.5 × 10 = 65 km. Going the other way, a real distance of 85 km is drawn as 85 ÷ 10 = 8.5 cm.

Worked Example 4: Gradient as a percent

A heavy train cannot climb a steep hill, so engineers measure how steep the track is. Gradient (percent) = rise ÷ horizontal distance × 100, with both in the same unit.

A track that climbs 15 m over 1000 m has a gradient of 15 ÷ 1000 × 100 = 1.5%. On a 2% grade, over 3 km (3000 m) the track climbs 3000 × 0.02 = 60 m.

CPR engineers wanted grades of about 2.2% or less. In the Rocky Mountains, the "Big Hill" near Kicking Horse Pass was about 4.5%, more than twice as steep, and it was dangerous for runaway trains.

Worked Example 5: Integers for elevation and temperature

Change = final − start. Going down gives a negative change. From a pass at 1600 m down to a valley at 1100 m: 1100 − 1600 = −500 m.

Temperatures below zero are negative too. From −12 °C, warming by 7 degrees: −12 + 7 = −5 °C.

Worked Example 6: Percents, percent change and timelines

Percent of: 0.30 out of 1.20 is 0.30 ÷ 1.20 × 100 = 25%.

Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. From 20 to 50: (50 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 150% increase.

Elapsed years = later year − earlier year. From 1867 (Confederation) to 1871 (BC joins Canada) is 1871 − 1867 = 4 years.
