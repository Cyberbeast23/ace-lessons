✍️ Writing Week 14: Historical Letter: A Voice from the Railway 🚂

Yesterday you wrote like a critic. Today, Ace, you'll write like a historian and a storyteller at the same time. You'll step into the shoes of someone who lived through the building of the Canadian Pacific Railway in the 1880s and write a historical letter in their voice. This kind of writing is called historical fiction: the character is invented, but the setting, events and facts must be true. A great historical letter makes readers feel what it was like to be there, and helps them understand a point of view different from their own.

Part 1: The Lesson

A friendly letter has six parts ✉️

Letters from the 1880s followed the same basic shape that letters still use today:

1. Heading: where the writer is and the date, e.g. Camp near Yale, British Columbia, August 14, 1883
2. Greeting: Dear Mother and Father, (capital letters for names, comma at the end)
3. Opening: who you are, where you are, and why you are writing
4. Body: what is happening, with true historical details and sensory description
5. Feelings and reflection: your character's thoughts, worries, hopes and opinions
6. Closing + signature: Your loving son, then the name (comma after the closing)

Five historian-writer moves

  • Stay in character: write in the first person (I, we) and only mention things your character could know in the 1880s: no phones, cars or electric lights in camp!
  • Use real facts: weave in at least two accurate historical details from today's reading (places, dates, jobs, wages, dangers, the head tax).
  • Show with your senses: what did the canyon sound like, the snow feel like, the camp food taste like? "The explosion shook the canyon like thunder" beats "it was loud."
  • Reveal a perspective: a Chinese worker, a surveyor and a politician would describe the same railway very differently. Let readers hear your character's feelings, including what seems fair or unfair to them.
  • Keep your tenses steady: tell what already happened in the past tense (yesterday we blasted) and use the present for how you feel now (I miss you). Don't switch back and forth by accident.

Grown-up words: a primary source is something made at the time by someone who was there (a real letter, diary or photograph). A secondary source is written later about the past (like today's reading passage). Your letter imitates a primary source.

Model Historical Letter (short version)

Heading + greeting + openingBody: historical + sensory detailsFeelings and reflectionClosing + signature

Camp near Yale, British Columbia
August 14, 1883

Dear Mother and Father,
I hope this letter finds you well in our village in Guangdong. It has been almost a year since I crossed the Pacific Ocean to work on the Canadian Pacific Railway.
Every day my crew climbs along the cliffs of the Fraser Canyon to drill holes for the blasting powder. When the explosions boom, the whole canyon shakes like thunder, and dust drifts down like grey snow. The river below is the colour of milky tea, and it roars so loudly that we must shout to hear each other. Last week, falling rocks injured two men from our camp.
I am tired, and I miss your cooking more than I can say. It does not seem fair that we earn only one dollar a day while other workers earn more for the same work. Still, I am proud of the tunnels we have built, and I am saving every coin I can to send home.
Please give my love to my little sister.
Your loving son,
Wing

Notice: Wing is an invented character, but the facts are real (Yale, the Fraser Canyon, blasting, $1 a day, workers from Guangdong). The body uses similes and sounds, the feelings section shows his point of view, and commas appear in the date, after the greeting, and after the closing.
