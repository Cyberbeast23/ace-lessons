Masters of the Dark: How Bats and Owls Rule the Night

Stand in a BC backyard on a warm evening just after sunset, and you might see a small, dark shape flicker past the porch light, zig, zag, and vanish. It is a bat, and it has just caught a moth in mid-air. Somewhere in the forest nearby, an owl is sitting perfectly still, listening. For people, the night is a time to slow down because our eyes work poorly in the dark. For these two hunters, the night is the busiest time of all.

Animals that are active at night are called nocturnal. Living in the dark has real advantages. There is less competition from daytime hunters such as hawks, fewer predators are watching, and on summer nights the air is full of insects. But the dark also creates a big problem: how do you find food you cannot see? Over millions of years, bats and owls have evolved two very different solutions.

A bat solves the problem with sound. As it flies, it emits a rapid stream of high-pitched calls through its mouth or nose. Most of these calls are ultrasonic, far too high for human ears. When a call hits a moth, a tiny echo bounces back. The bat's brain measures how long the echo took to return and how loud it is in each ear, and from that it can tell how far away the insect is, which direction it is flying, and even roughly how big it is. This skill is called echolocation. As the bat closes in, its calls speed up into a "feeding buzz" of up to two hundred calls a second, giving it a nearly continuous picture of its target. Some bats can detect a wire as thin as a human hair.

You may have heard the saying "blind as a bat." It is a common misconception. Every kind of bat can see, and some large fruit bats that live in the tropics hardly use echolocation at all because their eyesight is so good. For insect-hunting bats, though, hearing is simply the better tool in the dark.

Owls rely on their ears too, but they listen instead of calling. An owl's flat, round face works like a satellite dish, catching faint sounds and funnelling them toward its ears. In many owls, the ear openings are asymmetrical: one is higher on the head than the other. A rustle from below reaches one ear a tiny fraction of a second before the other and sounds slightly louder in it. By comparing the two, the owl can pinpoint a mouse scurrying under a layer of snow and strike without ever seeing it. Its huge eyes help as well. They are shaped like tubes and cannot roll in their sockets, which is why an owl turns its whole head, up to about 270 degrees, to look around.

An owl has another secret: silence. The front edges of its wing feathers have stiff, comb-like fringes, and the feathers are covered with a soft, velvety layer. Together they break up the rushing air and muffle the sound of each wingbeat, so the mouse hears nothing until it is too late. Engineers have studied owl feathers to design quieter fans, wind turbines and high-speed trains, an idea called biomimicry.

Even these masters of the dark are vulnerable, however. A fungal disease called white-nose syndrome has killed millions of hibernating bats in eastern North America by waking them again and again until their winter fat runs out, and it has now spread to the Pacific Northwest. In Canada, the little brown bat is listed as an endangered species. Owls are harmed when they eat rodents that have swallowed poison bait. People can help by putting up bat boxes, keeping cats indoors at night, leaving caves undisturbed in winter and choosing traps instead of poison. So when you are out trick-or-treating this Halloween, look up. The real creatures of the night deserve our respect, not our fear.
