✍️ Writing Week 33: Scientific Explanation: Which Night Hunter Is Best Built for the Dark? 🦇🦉

Yesterday you wrote like an ancient storyteller. Today, Ace, you'll write like a scientist. Scientists (and doctors!) don't just say what they think; they prove it. They use a structure called Claim, Evidence, Reasoning (CER): make a clear claim, back it with facts and numbers, then explain the science that connects the facts to the claim. A doctor does exactly this when deciding what is making a patient sick. Today you'll write a CER explanation about bats, owls or another night animal.

Part 1: The Lesson

A scientific explanation has six building blocks 🧩

A claim is your answer to a science question. Evidence is the data or observations that support it. Reasoning is the "because" part: the science idea that explains why the evidence proves the claim. Strong scientists also face the other side with a counterclaim.

1. Title + question: a title, plus the science question you are answering, like "Which hunts better in the dark, a bat or an owl?"
2. Claim: one clear sentence that answers the question. No "I think maybe."
3. Evidence: at least two specific facts, with numbers or measurements where you can
4. Reasoning: the science that links the evidence to the claim ("This matters because...")
5. Counterclaim + rebuttal: what someone might argue against you, and why your claim still holds
6. Conclusion: restate the claim in new words and say why it matters

Five scientist's moves

  • Be precise: not "bats call really high" but "bats call at about 50 kHz, more than twice as high as people can hear."
  • Use signal phrases for evidence: "According to...," "Scientists have measured...," "For example,..."
  • Use reasoning words: because, therefore, as a result, this shows that, this means.
  • Stay objective: write in the third person ("the bat," "scientists"), not "I love bats!" Save opinions for the claim.
  • Answer the other side fairly: "Some people might argue that... However,..." makes your claim stronger, not weaker.

Science tone: clear, calm and exact. Short sentences with strong facts beat long sentences with vague words like "stuff" or "a lot."

Built for the Hunt: The Barn Owl
Can an animal catch prey it cannot see at all? The barn owl can, because its hearing is precise enough to replace its eyes in total darkness.
In laboratory tests, barn owls have caught mice in rooms with no light at all. Their ear openings are asymmetrical, with one ear higher than the other, and their flat facial disc collects sound like a satellite dish.
This shows that the owl does not need light, because a sound reaches each ear at a slightly different time and loudness, and its brain uses those tiny differences to calculate exactly where the mouse is. Some people might argue that the owl's huge eyes are its real secret. However, eyes need at least some light, and the owl still succeeds in complete darkness.
Therefore, the barn owl's hearing, not its eyesight, is what makes it a master of the dark. Understanding how it locates sound could even help engineers design better hearing aids.

Notice: the claim answers the question directly; the evidence is specific; the reasoning explains the science with "because"; the counterclaim is answered with "However"; and the conclusion restates the claim in new words.
