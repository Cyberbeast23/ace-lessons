Weird Wonders in the Rock: The Secrets of the Burgess Shale

Imagine an animal with five eyes on stalks and a long, bendy nose ending in a claw. Picture a "worm" that walks on fourteen thin legs and has two rows of sharp spikes on its back. Now picture a hunter as long as your arm, with two curved, grabbing arms in front of its mouth. These are not monsters from a cartoon. They were real animals, and their fossils were found in a layer of dark rock called the Burgess Shale, on a steep ridge in Yoho National Park, British Columbia.

The story of the discovery began in 1909, when an American scientist named Charles Walcott was exploring the Rocky Mountains. According to a famous tale, a horse in his group slipped on the trail and turned over a slab of rock, revealing a strange fossil. Whether or not the horse really helped, Walcott returned again and again. Over many summers, his team split the shale with hammers and chisels and carried tens of thousands of specimens down the mountain. Most of them were packed off to a museum in Washington, D.C., where they were studied for decades.

Why are these fossils so famous? Most fossils are of hard parts, such as bones, teeth, and shells, because soft parts rot away long before they can turn to stone. In the Burgess Shale, though, scientists can see legs, gills, guts, and even the remains of a last meal. About 508 million years ago, these animals lived on the floor of a warm sea, at the foot of a tall underwater cliff. Every so often, an underwater mudslide swept down and buried them in an instant. The fine mud sealed out oxygen, so the bacteria that usually cause rotting could not do their work. Over millions of years, the mud hardened into shale, and the bodies were preserved as thin, shiny films in the rock.

The Burgess Shale gives us a window into the Cambrian Period, a time when life in the oceans changed dramatically. Before this time, most living things were tiny or soft and simple. Then, within a few tens of millions of years, which is quick on Earth's long clock, an incredible diversity of animals appeared: creatures with shells, eyes, legs, and jaws. Scientists call this burst of new life the "Cambrian explosion." Many of the main animal groups alive today, including the group that eventually led to fish and to us, have an ancestor from around this time.

Studying these fossils is not easy. Each animal was squashed almost flat, so scientists must interpret a crumpled, paper-thin shape and figure out what it looked like when it was alive. Sometimes they get it wrong at first. One small animal looked so bizarre that its finders named it Hallucigenia, as if it had come from a dream. At first, researchers drew it upside down, walking on its spikes and waving its legs in the air! Years later, new fossils from China showed that the "legs" were really the spikes and the "spikes" were really legs. Even the giant hunter, Anomalocaris, was first described as three different creatures, because its arms, mouth, and body had been found separately.

Mistakes like these are not failures; they are part of how science works. A good scientist starts with the most plausible explanation for the evidence available and stays ready to change it when better evidence turns up. Since Walcott's time, Canadian teams from the Royal Ontario Museum have found new Burgess Shale sites and thousands more fossils, including species no one had ever seen. With careful study, each one fills in another piece of the puzzle.

Today, the Burgess Shale is protected as part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and visitors can only reach the fossil beds on guided hikes. Taking fossils is against the law, because each specimen is irreplaceable: once it is broken or lost, its information is gone forever. High on that windy ridge, the rocks still hold secrets from a world that existed long before dinosaurs, flowers, or even trees, a world that helped shape every animal on Earth today, including you.
