✍️ Writing Week 28: Field Guide Entry: Describe a Cambrian Creature Like a Scientist 🦐🔬

Yesterday you wrote like a cartoon screenwriter. Today, Ace, you'll write like a palaeontologist. Scientists who discover a new animal must describe it so clearly that someone on the other side of the world could picture it without ever seeing the fossil. You'll write a field guide entry: a short, organized, factual description with headings, precise details and measurements, and a caption for a picture. You can write about a real Burgess Shale animal from today's reading, or invent your own Cambrian creature, as long as you describe it like a scientist.

Part 1: The Lesson

A field guide entry has six building blocks 🧩

Field guides are organized with headings so a reader can find information fast. Each section has its own job.

1. Name + meaning: the scientific name, what it means, and a one-sentence summary of what kind of animal it is
2. Description: its body plan from front to back: shape, parts, how many of each, and exact measurements in metric units
3. Habitat: where and when it lived (sea floor, open water, mud; how many million years ago)
4. Diet + behaviour: what it ate and how it moved, hunted, or hid
5. Adaptations + evidence: a body part that helped it survive, and the fossil evidence that tells us so
6. Caption: one or two sentences under a picture that tell the reader exactly what to notice

Five scientist's moves

  • Be precise, not vague: instead of "it was kind of big," write "it grew to about 50 cm long." Numbers and units make writing scientific.
  • Use comparisons the reader knows: "about the size of a paper clip," "shaped like a canoe."
  • Describe in a logical order: front to back, or top to bottom, so the reader can build the picture step by step.
  • Separate facts from inferences: "Its fossil shows spines" is a fact. "The spines probably protected it" is an inference, so use words like probably, likely, or scientists think.
  • Use the right words: segment, appendage, carapace, predator, prey. Explain a hard word the first time you use it.

Grown-up words: a species is one kind of living thing; its two-part Latin name, like Opabinia regalis, is written in italics with only the first word capitalized. The animals with jointed legs and a hard outer covering are arthropods.

Model Field Guide Entry (short version)

Name + meaningDescription + habitatDiet, adaptations + evidenceCaption

OPABINIA (Opabinia regalis): named after Opabin Pass in the Rocky Mountains. A small sea animal that may be the strangest creature in the Burgess Shale.
Description: Opabinia grew to about 7 cm long, roughly the length of your finger. At the front of its head were five eyes on short stalks. Below them hung a long, flexible nose, called a proboscis, that ended in a spiny claw. Its soft body had 15 segments, each with a flap on both sides and gills on top. Its tail was shaped like a fan. Habitat: the muddy floor of a warm sea about 508 million years ago.
Diet + adaptations: Scientists think Opabinia used its claw to grab small prey from the mud and pass it back to its mouth. Its five eyes probably helped it spot movement in every direction. The side flaps likely let it swim by waving them in a ripple. Evidence: flattened fossils show the eyes, claw, and flaps clearly.
Caption: Opabinia, seen from above. Look for the five eyes and the claw-tipped proboscis at the front.

Notice: the entry uses headings; the measurements are metric and compared to something familiar ("the length of your finger"); the description goes front to back; hard words are explained (proboscis); and inferences are signalled with scientists think, probably, and likely.
