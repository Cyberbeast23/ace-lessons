🦐 Math Week 28: Burgess Shale Fossil Math Lab

Ace, high in the Rocky Mountains of Yoho National Park, right here in British Columbia, there is a layer of rock packed with some of the strangest animals that ever lived: a creature with five eyes, a "worm" walking on spikes, and a giant hunter with grabbing arms. They swam in a warm sea about 508 million years ago. Palaeontologists use math every day to measure, count, and date these fossils. Today you'll work like a fossil scientist with huge numbers and time spans, a timeline scale, counting inclusive years, mean and median, three-part ratios, percent, volume and mass, model scale with unit conversion, negative numbers, rates over enormous time, a bar graph target, expected results, a two-condition puzzle, and an equation.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your field-scientist checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (mm with mm, m with m)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Huge numbers and timeline scales

Scientists often write big ages in millions of years. 66 million years is 66 000 000 years. To find how far apart two events are, subtract: 300 million − 66 million = 234 million years.

To draw a timeline, choose a scale, such as 1 cm = 10 million years. Then 234 million years is 234 ÷ 10 = 23.4 cm of ribbon.

Worked Example 2: Counting years "from ... through ..." (inclusive)

How many summers is it from 2020 through 2024, counting both? 2024 − 2020 = 4, but list them: 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024. That's 5. When you count both ends, subtract, then add 1. (Think of fence posts: 4 gaps need 5 posts.)

Worked Example 3: Mean and median

The mean (average) = add all the values, then divide by how many there are. For 4, 9, 5: (4 + 9 + 5) ÷ 3 = 18 ÷ 3 = 6.

The median is the middle value after you put them in order. For 8, 3, 6, 10, 4: in order 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, so the median is 6. Don't forget to sort first!

Worked Example 4: Three-part ratios and percent

Shells, plants and worms in a 4 : 1 : 3 ratio out of 80 fossils: there are 4 + 1 + 3 = 8 parts, so one part = 80 ÷ 8 = 10. Worms = 3 parts = 30.

Percent: 30% of 2000 = 0.30 × 2000 = 600. (Or find 10% = 200, then times 3.)

Worked Example 5: Volume, then mass

The volume of a rectangular block = length × width × height. A rock 2 m × 1 m × 0.5 m has a volume of 1 m³.

Density tells you the mass of each cubic metre. If 1 m³ of a rock has a mass of 2500 kg, then 3 m³ has a mass of 3 × 2500 = 7500 kg.

Worked Example 6: Negative numbers and rates

Sea level is 0 m. Below sea level is negative: −40 m. The distance from −40 m up to +100 m is 100 − (−40) = 100 + 40 = 140 m. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding!

Rates over time: if a cliff wears away 2 mm per year, how long to lose 1 m? Change units first: 1 m = 1000 mm. Then 1000 ÷ 2 = 500 years.

Worked Example 7: Reaching a target mean, and equations

To have a mean of 10 over 4 days, you need a total of 10 × 4 = 40. If the first 3 days add up to 27, the last day needs 40 − 27 = 13.

Solving an equation: 8b + 16 = 96. Undo each step in reverse: subtract 16 (8b = 80), then divide by 8 (b = 10).
