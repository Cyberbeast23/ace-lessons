🏔️ Math Week 48: Empire in the Clouds: Inca Quipu Keeper's Math Lab

Ace, about 500 years ago the Inca built the largest empire in the Americas, stretching more than 4 000 km along the Andes Mountains of South America. They had no iron tools, no wheels for carts and no written alphabet, yet they ran roads, bridges, farms and storehouses for millions of people. Their secret record-keepers were the quipucamayocs ("knot keepers"), who stored numbers on bundles of knotted strings called quipus, using a base-ten place-value system like ours. Today you are a quipu keeper. You'll use place value on knotted cords, relay rates and speed in km/h, integer temperature changes, the area of a trapezoid, volume and density, percents, ratios, the area of a circle, map scales, the mean (and working backward from it), a two-step equation, powers of ten, probability, and a multi-step bridge problem.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your quipu keeper's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (m, cm, km, m², m³, kg, °C, s, min, h, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change metres to centimetres or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A llama can carry about 30 kg, but not 300 kg!

Worked Example 1: Reading a quipu (place value)

On a quipu cord, knot clusters sit in rows. The cluster nearest the top is the biggest place value; the one at the bottom is the ones. A blank space means zero, just like the 0 in 305.

Top to bottom: 3 knots, (blank), 5 knots → 3 hundreds, 0 tens, 5 ones = 305. A "main cord" often held the total of the cords hanging from it.

Worked Example 2: Speed and relay rates

Speed = distance ÷ time. To get km/h, the time must be in hours: 15 min = 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25 h.

A runner covers 3 km in 15 min: 3 ÷ 0.25 = 12 km/h. A relay team that hands off every 2 km needs 50 runners to cover 100 km (100 ÷ 2).

Worked Example 3: Integer change

Change = final − start. From −6 °C to 11 °C: 11 − (−6) = 11 + 6 = 17 °C warmer. On a number line, count 6 up to zero, then 11 more.

Worked Example 4: Area of a trapezoid

A trapezoid has two parallel sides (a and b). Area = (a + b) ÷ 2 × h, where h is the distance between the parallel sides. You are finding the average length, then multiplying by the height, just like a rectangle!

a = 10 m, b = 6 m, h = 4 m: (10 + 6) ÷ 2 × 4 = 8 × 4 = 32 m².

Worked Example 5: Volume and density

Density tells how much mass is packed into each cubic metre. Mass = volume × density.

A block 2 m × 1 m × 0.5 m = 1 m³. If the stone's density is 2 600 kg/m³, the block's mass is 1 × 2 600 = 2 600 kg (heavier than a car!).

Worked Example 6: Area of a circle

A = π × r × r (use π ≈ 3.14; r = radius = half the diameter).

Diameter 10 m → r = 5 m → 3.14 × 5 × 5 = 78.5 m².

Worked Example 7: Powers of ten

103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1 000. So 3 × 104 = 3 × 10 000 = 30 000. Scientists write huge numbers this way so they are easy to read and compare.

Worked Example 8: Probability

Probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes, written as a fraction in lowest terms. 3 blue cords out of 12 cords → 3/12 = 1/4.

Worked Example 9: Working backward from a mean

Mean × how many = total. If 4 numbers have a mean of 10, their total is 40. To make 5 numbers have a mean of 12, the total must be 5 × 12 = 60, so the new number must be 60 − 40 = 20.
