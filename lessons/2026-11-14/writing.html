✍️ Writing Week 48: Cause-and-Effect Essay — Why Things Happened in the Inca Empire 🏔️🪢

Yesterday you explained how a system works, step by step. Today, Ace, you'll write like a historian in a brand-new genre: a cause-and-effect essay. Historians are never satisfied with what happened; they want to know why it happened (the causes) and what happened because of it (the effects). The best cause-and-effect writers also spot chain reactions, where one effect becomes the cause of the next thing, like dominoes falling. It is not a story and it is not a list of facts: it is an explanation of connections, backed up by evidence.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong cause-and-effect essay 🔗

A cause-and-effect essay answers two big questions: Why did it happen? and What did it lead to? A cause-and-effect chain (like a flow chart) helps you plan; the paragraphs explain each link with evidence.

1. Hook + thesis: grab the reader, then state the cause and effect your essay will explain
2. Cause: a clear sentence that names why something happened
3. Effect: a clear sentence that names what happened as a result
4. Evidence (facts + numbers): dates, distances, amounts and names that prove the connection is real
5. Chain link: show how one effect became the next cause (This led to…)
6. Conclusion + significance: sum up the chain and explain why it matters

Four tricks cause-and-effect writers use

  • Use signal words: for causes, because, since, due to; for effects, as a result, therefore, consequently, so, led to.
  • Prove the link, don't just claim it: ❌ "The roads were good, so the empire was strong." ✅ "Because chasquis could carry news 240 km in a day, the emperor heard about problems quickly and could send help."
  • Look for more than one cause: big events usually have several causes working together (the fall of the Inca had at least three!).
  • Keep history in the past tense: the Inca built, carried, stored, not build, carry, store. (Things that are still true today, like "Quechua families still farm the terraces," can use the present tense.)

Historian's rule: every "because" in your essay should be backed up by a fact from your reading.

Mini-lesson: sentences that connect ideas 🔗

Cause clause first? Add a comma. Because the slopes were steep, farmers built terraces. If the "because" part comes second, you usually don't need one: Farmers built terraces because the slopes were steep.

Semicolon + transition + comma: join two complete sentences with a strong effect word: The stairs were too steep for carts; therefore, llamas carried the loads. (You can also use a period: … for carts. Therefore, llamas …)

Affect vs. effect: affect is usually a verb (Smallpox affected millions.). Effect is usually a noun (It had a terrible effect.). Trick: Affect = Action, Effect = End result.

Capitals for peoples, languages and places: Inca, Quechua, Spanish, Andes, Cusco, Peru. Canadian spelling: neighbouring, honour, favourite, centre, kilometre.

Model cause-and-effect essay (about 230 words)

Hook + thesisCause → effectEvidence + chain linkConclusion + significance

Roads That Held an Empire Together
Imagine running a country 4 000 km long with no phones, horses or cars. The Inca faced this problem every day. Their road network, the Qhapaq Ñan, was a major cause of the empire's strength, and it set off a powerful chain reaction.
Roads built→Fast messages→Quick action→United empire
Because the roads linked Cusco to every region, news travelled quickly. Chasquis, relay runners who each sprinted about 2.4 km, carried messages roughly 240 km in one day. As a result, the emperor learned about trouble in days instead of weeks. This fast news led to a second effect: help could be sent wherever it was needed. Since tambos, rest houses stocked with food, stood along the roads, soldiers and workers did not have to carry everything themselves; consequently, rebellions and bad harvests were handled before they grew worse. The network was about 40 000 km long, so almost no village was out of reach. In conclusion, the roads caused a chain reaction: faster messages led to faster action, which kept the empire united. Without the Qhapaq Ñan, Tawantinsuyu might have split apart long before 1532.

Notice: the thesis names the cause (the roads) and promises effects; the chain chart matches the paragraphs; signal words (because, as a result, led to, since, consequently, so) make every link clear; numbers (2.4 km, 240 km, 40 000 km) prove the links; "This fast news led to…" is a chain link; the essay stays in the past tense; the conclusion explains why it matters.
