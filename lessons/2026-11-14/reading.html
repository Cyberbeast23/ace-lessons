Empire in the Clouds: How the Inca Ran a Mountain Empire Without Wheels, Iron or Writing

Imagine trying to run a country that is more than 4 000 kilometres long, squeezed along some of the tallest, steepest mountains on Earth. Now imagine doing it without iron tools, without wheeled carts, without horses and without a written alphabet. It sounds impossible. Yet about 500 years ago, the Inca did exactly that in the Andes Mountains of South America. At its height in the early 1500s, their empire stretched from what is now Ecuador to central Chile and was home to roughly ten million people.

The Inca called their empire Tawantinsuyu, "the four regions together." Its capital, Cusco, sits about 3 400 metres above sea level, much higher than the top of Whistler Mountain. For a long time the Inca were one small group among many in the region. Then, beginning around 1438, a ruler named Pachacuti and the leaders who came after him expanded the empire with astonishing speed, sometimes through war and sometimes through deals with neighbouring peoples. In less than a century, a small kingdom became the largest empire the Americas had ever seen.

Feeding all those people was the first great challenge. Steep mountainsides are hard to farm because rain washes the soil away. The Inca's solution was to carve the hills into giant staircases called terraces, flat fields held up by stone walls. Each terrace was built in layers: large stones at the bottom, then gravel, then rich soil on top, so that water drained away instead of flooding the crops. The stone walls soaked up the sun's heat during the day and released it at night, protecting plants from frost. Andean farmers grew maize and quinoa, and they developed hundreds of kinds of potatoes. They even invented a way to preserve potatoes. By leaving them out on freezing nights, trampling out the water and drying them in the sun, they made chuño, a light, freeze-dried food that could last for years.

The food was stored in thousands of stone storehouses called qollqas, often built on breezy hillsides where cool air kept the food from spoiling. The Inca had no money. Instead, families paid their "taxes" with work, called mit'a: farming the state's fields, building roads, serving in the army or weaving cloth. In return, the full storehouses fed soldiers and workers and helped communities survive bad harvests.

To hold the empire together, the Inca built one of the greatest road systems of the ancient world, the Qhapaq Ñan, or "Royal Road." Its many branches added up to about 40 000 kilometres, enough to wrap around Earth. The roads climbed mountain passes with stone stairways, crossed deserts and wound through jungles. Because carts would have been useless on such steep stairs, people travelled on foot, and llamas carried the loads. Every day or so along the road stood a tambo, a rest house with food and shelter. In 2014, the Qhapaq Ñan was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Messages flew along these roads thanks to a relay of runners called chasquis. Each chasqui ran a short stretch at top speed, then passed the message to the next runner waiting at a small hut. Working together, chasquis could move news roughly 240 kilometres in a single day. Where the roads met deep river gorges, communities wove suspension bridges out of grass rope. One of them, the Q'eswachaka bridge in Peru, is still rebuilt by hand every June by local Quechua families, who braid kilometres of grass cord and replace the old bridge in a few days of shared work and celebration.

But how do you keep track of millions of people and storehouses without writing? The Inca used the khipu, or quipu: a main cord with dozens or even hundreds of coloured strings hanging from it. Knots tied at different positions showed ones, tens, hundreds and thousands, the same base-ten place-value system you use in math class. Expert record-keepers could "read" a quipu to report a village's population, a census of who lived where, or how much maize sat in each storehouse. Some researchers believe quipus may also have recorded names, events or even stories, but no one has fully cracked that code yet. It is one of history's great unsolved puzzles.

The Inca were also master builders. Their stonemasons shaped huge blocks with harder stones and bronze tools, then fitted them together so precisely that, in some walls, not even a knife blade fits between them. They used no mortar, the cement-like paste that usually holds stones together. Their walls lean slightly inward, and their doors and windows are shaped like trapezoids, wider at the bottom. During earthquakes, the blocks can shift and then settle back into place. That is why many Inca walls in Cusco are still standing, while some later Spanish buildings on top of them have collapsed.

The most famous Inca site is Machu Picchu, built around 1450, probably as a royal estate for Pachacuti, on a ridge about 2 430 metres high. Spanish invaders never found it. In 1911, an American explorer named Hiram Bingham was guided there by local farmers and made it famous around the world. He is sometimes called its "discoverer," but that is not quite fair: Quechua families living nearby had known about Machu Picchu all along.

The empire's end came swiftly. In the 1520s, smallpox, a disease brought to the Americas by Europeans, spread south ahead of the Europeans themselves. It killed huge numbers of people, including the emperor. Two of his sons, Atahualpa and Huáscar, then fought a bitter civil war for the throne. In 1532, just as Atahualpa won, a small Spanish force led by Francisco Pizarro arrived. With steel weapons, horses and the help of Indigenous groups who resented Inca rule, the Spanish captured Atahualpa. Their conquest took decades to complete, but the empire never recovered.

Much of what we read about the Inca was written by Spanish soldiers and priests, who often misunderstood or looked down on Andean ways of life. Other sources give different views. Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, an Andean nobleman, wrote a long letter to the King of Spain around 1615, filled with nearly 400 drawings of Inca daily life and of the unfair treatment his people suffered under Spanish rule. Today, the millions of Quechua-speaking descendants of the Inca still farm the old terraces, weave traditional cloth and rebuild grass bridges, reminding the world that the people of Tawantinsuyu did not vanish. Their story is still being told, and it is still being written by them.
