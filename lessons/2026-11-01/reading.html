From Shells to Smartphones: How Money and Banks Work

Imagine you have a basket of fresh salmon and you want a new pair of shoes. Long ago, before money existed, you would have had to barter: trade your fish directly for the shoes. But what if the shoemaker didn't want fish? You would need to find someone who had what you wanted and wanted what you had. That rarely happens, and while you searched, your salmon would start to spoil.

To solve this problem, people around the world began using special objects that everyone agreed were valuable. On the Pacific coast, many First Nations traded with dentalium, small tusk-shaped shells gathered from deep water off the west coast of Vancouver Island. Because the shells were hard to collect, they were scarce, and things that are hard to get tend to be worth more. Dentalium travelled through trade networks far inland. In other places, people used cocoa beans, salt or even giant stone wheels as money.

Metal coins appeared about 2600 years ago in a kingdom called Lydia, in what is now Türkiye. Coins were small, easy to count and durable, so they did not rot like fish or crumble like salt. A ruler stamped each coin to show how much it was worth, so traders no longer had to weigh every piece of metal. Later, China began using paper money, which was even lighter to carry than a bag of heavy coins.

Carrying lots of coins was risky, though, because thieves could steal them. In Italy about 800 years ago, money lenders sat at benches, called banca, in busy markets. People left their coins with them for safekeeping, and the lenders loaned some of that money to others who needed it, such as merchants buying ships. Borrowers paid back the loan plus extra money called interest. The lenders shared some of the interest with the savers. That is still how banks work today: they pay savers a small amount of interest and charge borrowers a larger amount, and the difference helps the bank earn money.

In Canada, the Bank of Canada opened in 1935. It is not a bank where families keep savings. Instead, it is in charge of the country's money: it issues bank notes and helps keep prices steady. When prices keep rising over many years, it is called inflation. A little inflation is normal, but when prices rise too fast, the money in your piggy bank buys less and less. To stop that from happening, the Bank of Canada can raise interest rates, which makes borrowing more expensive and encourages people to save.

Canada's money keeps changing, too. In 1987, the gold-coloured loonie, named for the common loon on one side, replaced the paper one-dollar bill, because coins last far longer than paper in circulation, passing from hand to hand. In 1996, the two-dollar toonie arrived. Starting in 2011, Canada switched to bank notes made of polymer, a thin, tough plastic with see-through windows that are hard to copy. And in 2012, the penny became obsolete: it cost more than one cent to make each one! Today, cash totals are rounded to the nearest five cents.

Now many people never touch coins at all. They tap a card or a smartphone, and the money moves electronically from one bank account to another in seconds. Digital payments are convenient, but they make it easy to spend money without noticing. That is why a prudent saver still keeps a budget, a plan that shows how much money comes in, how much goes out, and how much is set aside for the future.

From salmon and shells to stamped coins, bank notes and tap-to-pay phones, money has changed shape many times. Its job, however, has stayed the same: to make trading easy and fair. Understanding how money and interest work is one of the best tools anyone can have, at any age.
