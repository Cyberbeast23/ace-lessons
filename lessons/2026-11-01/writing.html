✍️ Writing Week 35: Business Pitch: Launch Your Own Mini Business 💼💰

Yesterday you wrote like a reporter. Today, Ace, you'll write like an entrepreneur: someone who starts a business. Before a bank lends money or an investor puts money into a new business, the owner must give a pitch: a short, convincing plan that explains what the business sells, who will buy it, what it costs, and how it will make a profit. A great pitch mixes persuasion (why it's a great idea) with numbers (proof that it can work). Today you'll write a pitch for your own mini business.

Part 1: The Lesson

A business pitch has six building blocks 🧩

Investors are busy, so a pitch must be clear, organized and backed by numbers. Each building block answers a question the investor is thinking.

1. Hook: grab attention with a question, a surprising fact or a problem people have ("Tired of soggy sandwiches?")
2. Product: what you sell or do, and the business name
3. Customers: who will buy it and why they need it (the problem you solve)
4. Costs & price: what supplies cost, what you'll charge, and the profit on each sale
5. Goal & plan: how much you hope to earn, how you'll save or spend it, and what you need to get started
6. The ask: a strong closing that tells the investor exactly what you want ("Will you invest $40?") and why it's a smart choice

Five entrepreneur's moves

  • Show the math: profit = price − cost. "Each cookie costs 40¢ to bake and sells for $1.50, so I earn $1.10 profit per cookie."
  • Know your customer: not "everyone" but "busy parents on my street who have dogs."
  • Answer a worry before it's asked: "Some might worry it will rain. If it does, I'll sell hot chocolate instead."
  • Use persuasive words: affordable, reliable, convenient, guaranteed, local.
  • Stay honest: never promise what you can't deliver. Investors trust a realistic plan more than a wild one.

Pitch tone: confident, friendly and businesslike. Write in the first person ("I will...") and speak directly to the investor ("you").

Model Business Pitch (short version)

Hook + productCustomers + costs & priceGoal & planThe ask

Have you ever come home to a muddy dog and no time to wash it? My business, Paws & Suds, will give dogs a bath right in their own backyard.
My customers are busy families in my neighbourhood. At least eight homes on my street have dogs. Shampoo and towels cost about $2 per bath, and I will charge $10, so I make $8 profit on every dog.
If I wash four dogs every weekend, I will earn $32 a week. I will save 30% in my bank account, give 10% to the animal shelter, and use the rest to buy a better hose. Some might worry about cold weather. In winter, I will offer indoor paw cleaning instead.
To start, I need $25 for a tub, shampoo and towels. Will you invest $25? I will pay you back in four weeks, plus a free bath for your dog!

Notice: the hook is a question about a real problem; the numbers prove the business can make a profit; the writer answers a worry before the investor asks; and the ending makes a clear, specific ask.
