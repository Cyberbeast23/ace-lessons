💰 Math Week 35: Money Smarts: Young Banker's Math Lab

Ace, money is math you use every single day. Shoppers compare unit prices, cashiers add GST and PST, savers watch interest grow, and banks earn money by lending out what people deposit. Today you'll think like a smart shopper and a banker with unit prices, BC sales tax, discounts, simple and compound interest, budget percents, coin masses, cash rounding to the nearest 5¢, percent increase (inflation), bank profit on loans, an equation with the variable on both sides, exchange rates, negative account balances, how long money takes to double, and sharing profit in a ratio.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your banker's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units ($, ¢, kg, %).
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Is the percent taken of the original amount or of a new amount?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down. Money answers usually round to the nearest cent (2 decimal places).
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A discount should make the price smaller; interest should make savings bigger.

Worked Example 1: Unit price (the best buy)

Unit price = price ÷ amount. It tells you what one unit costs, so you can compare packages of different sizes.

500 g of rice for $2.40 → $2.40 ÷ 0.5 kg = $4.80 per kg. A 2 kg bag for $8.80 → $8.80 ÷ 2 = $4.40 per kg, so the big bag is the better buy.

Worked Example 2: Sales tax in British Columbia

In BC, most things you buy have GST of 5% (federal) plus PST of 7% (provincial), for a total of 12%. (A few things, like groceries and kids' clothes, have less or no tax.)

Shortcut: price × 1.12 = price with tax. A $20 board game: 20 × 1.12 = $22.40.

Worked Example 3: Discounts

"30% off" means you pay 100% − 30% = 70% of the price. A $50 shirt: 50 × 0.70 = $35. Tax is added after the discount.

Worked Example 4: Simple interest

Interest is money a bank pays you for keeping your savings there (or money you pay when you borrow). Simple interest: I = P × r × t (principal × rate per year × years).

$200 at 4% for 3 years: I = 200 × 0.04 × 3 = $24 of interest.

Worked Example 5: Compound interest (interest on interest!)

With compound interest, each year's interest is added to the balance, so next year you earn interest on the interest too. Each year, multiply by (1 + rate).

$100 at 5% compounded yearly: Year 1: 100 × 1.05 = $105. Year 2: 105 × 1.05 = $110.25 (simple interest would give only $110).

Worked Example 6: Percent increase (inflation)

Percent increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Always divide by the old (original) price.

A bus ticket goes from $2.50 to $3.00. Increase = 0.50 ÷ 2.50 = 0.2 = 20%.

Worked Example 7: Cash rounding in Canada

Canada stopped making pennies in 2012, so cash totals are rounded to the nearest 5¢. Totals ending in 1¢ or 2¢ round down to 0¢; 3¢ or 4¢ round up to 5¢; 6¢ or 7¢ round down to 5¢; 8¢ or 9¢ round up to the next 10¢.

$4.92 → $4.90. $4.94 → $4.95. (Card payments are still charged to the exact cent.)

Worked Example 8: Negative bank balances

If you spend more than is in your account, the balance goes below zero. Deposits add; withdrawals and cheques subtract.

Balance $20, a $50 withdrawal: 20 − 50 = −$30 (you owe the bank $30).

Worked Example 9: Exchange rates

An exchange rate tells you how much one currency costs in another. If US $1 costs CA $1.30, then US $10 costs 10 × 1.30 = CA $13.

Worked Example 10: Sharing in a ratio

Two friends put in $20 and $30 (a ratio of 2 : 3). There are 2 + 3 = 5 parts. If they earn $100 profit, one part = 100 ÷ 5 = $20, so they get $40 and $60.
