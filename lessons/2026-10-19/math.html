🦠 Math Week 22: Germ Fighters: Immune System & Vaccines Lab

Ace, your body is defended every second by an army of white blood cells, and doctors, nurses, and scientists use math to understand the germs they fight. Germs are tiny, so we measure them in micrometres (µm), and some bacteria can split in two every 20 minutes, so their numbers grow exponentially: faster and faster. Today you'll think like a future doctor and a public-health scientist with powers and exponents, doubling and halving, exponential growth tables, micrometres and microscope magnification, ratios, rates, percents of a population, herd immunity, and reading a line graph of antibodies after a vaccine.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your lab scientist's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Powers and exponents

A power is a short way to write repeated multiplication. 25 means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. The small raised 5 is the exponent: it tells you how many 2s to multiply.

33 = 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. Careful: 33 is not 3 × 3 = 9!

Worked Example 2: Doubling (exponential growth)

Some bacteria split into 2 every 20 minutes. Start with 1. After 1 hour there have been 60 ÷ 20 = 3 doublings, so there are 23 = 8 bacteria.

If you start with 30 bacteria and they double 4 times: 30 × 24 = 30 × 16 = 480. Each step multiplies by 2, so the numbers grow faster and faster. That is called exponential growth.

Halving works the same way backwards: 80 halved 3 times is 80 × ½ × ½ × ½ = 80 ÷ 8 = 10.

Worked Example 3: Tiny units and magnification

1 millimetre (mm) = 1000 micrometres (µm). So 3 µm = 3 ÷ 1000 = 0.003 mm.

A microscope that magnifies 100× makes things look 100 times bigger. A 5 µm cell would look 5 × 100 = 500 µm = 0.5 mm wide.

Worked Example 4: Percents of a population

If 85% of 600 students have had a flu shot: 0.85 × 600 = 510 students. The rest, 100% − 85% = 15%, is 0.15 × 600 = 90 students.

Percent increase = change ÷ starting amount × 100. Going from 40 to 60 is a change of 20, and 20 ÷ 40 × 100 = 50%.

Worked Example 5: Rates from a graph

If a line on a graph goes from 20 on day 2 to 60 on day 10, it rose 60 − 20 = 40 units in 10 − 2 = 8 days. The average rate is 40 ÷ 8 = 5 units per day.
