✍️ Writing Week 22: Science Adventure Story: The Germ Wars 🦠🛡️

Yesterday you wrote a news-style weather report that stuck to the facts. Today, Ace, you'll use real science in a completely different way: you'll write a science adventure story told from inside the human body. Your narrator might be a white blood cell, a B cell, or a memory cell, and the villain is an invading germ. A great science story does two jobs at once: it entertains readers with a hook, a problem, rising action, and an exciting climax, and it teaches them how the immune system really works. By the end, a reader should be on the edge of their seat and know what an antibody does.

Part 1: The Lesson

A science adventure story has six parts 🧩

Think of it as a story mountain: you climb up to the most exciting moment, then come back down.

1. Hook: a first line that grabs the reader: a sound, a surprise, a line of dialogue, or a question
2. Narrator + setting: who is telling the story and where in the body it happens
3. The problem: the germ invades. How does it get in, and why is it dangerous?
4. Rising action: two or three events that build tension as the defenders fight back and things get harder
5. Climax: the turning point, the biggest and most exciting moment of the battle
6. Resolution: how things settle down and what changed (healing, memory cells, a lesson learned)

Five storyteller's moves

  • Pick a point of view and keep it: a first-person narrator uses I, me, we, our. Don't switch to "he" or "you" halfway through. Stay in the past tense for the whole story (I squeezed, I swallowed, we won).
  • Show, don't tell: instead of "it was scary," write what the narrator sees, hears, and feels: "The bacteria wriggled through the torn skin, splitting in two again and again."
  • Use dialogue: a few lines of speech bring characters to life. Start a new line for each new speaker, and put the punctuation inside the quotation marks: "Follow me!" shouted the phagocyte.
  • Make the science real: weave in true facts from today's reading: skin as a wall, mucus, inflammation, phagocytes, antibodies fitting a germ like a key in a lock, memory cells, and vaccines. Readers should learn something!
  • Control the pace: use short, punchy sentences for action ("It split. Then it split again.") and longer sentences for description and calm moments.

Grown-up words: the character who tells the story is the narrator, and the angle it is told from is the point of view. Giving human feelings and actions to something that isn't human, like a brave white blood cell, is called personification. The climax is the peak of the story mountain, and the resolution is how the problem is solved.

Model Science Adventure Story (short version)

Hook + narrator + settingProblem + rising actionClimaxResolution

BEEP! BEEP! BEEP! The danger signal blasted through the bloodstream just after recess. I am Phaggy, a phagocyte, one of the white blood cells that patrol a ten-year-old's body, and I was drifting through a tiny blood vessel in his right knee when the alarm went off.
My boy had slid into second base, and the wall of skin was torn open. Through the scrape poured an army of bacteria, wriggling and splitting in two. "Defenders, follow me!" I shouted. I squeezed out of the blood vessel and swallowed the first bacterium whole. More blood rushed in behind me, so the knee turned red, warm, and puffy. That inflammation meant help was on the way, but the bacteria kept doubling, faster and faster.
Just when I thought we would be overrun, a cloud of Y-shaped antibodies drifted in from the B cells. They clamped onto the germs like keys in locks. The germs froze. We charged, and we swallowed every last one.
A week later, a pink new layer of skin covered the scrape. Deep inside the body, a memory cell quietly saved a picture of the invaders. "If you ever come back," it whispered, "we'll be ready."

Notice: the hook is a sound that grabs you; the narrator (I, a phagocyte) stays in first person and the past tense the whole way; the dialogue has its punctuation inside the quotation marks; real science (skin as a wall, inflammation, antibodies like keys, memory cells) is woven into the action; and the short sentences "The germs froze. We charged." speed up the climax.
