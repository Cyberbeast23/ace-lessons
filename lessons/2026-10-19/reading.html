Your Body's Defence Force: Germs, Immunity, and Vaccines

Right now, as you read this sentence, your body is under attack. Germs are floating in the air you breathe, sitting on the pencil you hold, and hiding on the apple in your lunch bag. Most of them never make you sick, because your body has a defence force that works day and night. Scientists call it the immune system. To understand how it protects you, you first need to meet the enemy.

A germ is a microorganism, a living thing far too small to see without a microscope, that can cause disease. A germ that makes you sick is called a pathogen. The two most common kinds are bacteria and viruses, and they are very different. Bacteria are complete single cells that can eat, grow, and split in two on their own; some can double their numbers every 20 minutes. Surprisingly, most bacteria are harmless, and trillions of helpful ones live in your intestines, where they help you digest food. Viruses are much smaller, about one-twentieth the length of a typical bacterium. A virus is not even a cell. It is a tiny package of instructions wrapped in a protein coat, and it can only make copies of itself by sneaking inside one of your cells and taking over, like a hacker taking over a computer. Colds, the flu, and chickenpox are all caused by viruses.

Think of your body as a castle. Its outer walls are your skin, a tough, waterproof barrier that most germs cannot cross. The castle gates, such as your nose, mouth, and eyes, have their own guards. Sticky mucus in your nose traps germs and dust, and tiny hairs sweep it away so you can sneeze or swallow it. Tears wash your eyes, and strong acid in your stomach destroys many germs that you swallow. But if you scrape your knee, the wall is broken, and invaders rush in. Then the first responders arrive. White blood cells called phagocytes (a name that means "eating cells") squeeze out of your blood vessels and swallow the germs whole. Blood flow to the area increases, so the scrape becomes red, swollen, warm, and sore. This inflammation may feel annoying, but it is a sign that your defenders are hard at work. Sometimes your whole body raises its temperature to make a fever, which slows germs down and speeds your defenders up.

If an invader gets past the first responders, your body calls in its special forces. Some white blood cells, called B cells, make antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that fit onto one particular germ the way a key fits one lock. Antibodies stick to the germ, stop it from entering your cells, and mark it so other white blood cells can destroy it. Building the right antibodies for a brand-new germ can take a week or two, which is why you feel sick for a while before you get better. Afterward, your body keeps memory cells that remember that exact germ for years. If it ever attacks again, your defenders recognize it and wipe it out so quickly that you usually never feel sick at all. This protection is called immunity.

What if you could get immunity without getting sick first? That is exactly what a vaccine does. In 1796, an English doctor named Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who had caught cowpox, a mild disease from cows, did not catch smallpox, a deadly disease that killed millions of people. He scratched material from a cowpox sore into the arm of a boy, and later the boy did not get smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, the Latin word for cow. Modern vaccines use a weakened or killed germ, or just one harmless piece of it, to give your immune system a practice drill. Your body makes antibodies and memory cells, so it is ready if the real pathogen ever shows up.

Vaccines protect more than just the person who gets the shot. Many diseases are contagious, meaning they spread from person to person. When most people in a community are immune, a germ runs out of people to infect, and it cannot spread to babies or people who are too sick to be vaccinated. This is called herd immunity. Thanks to a worldwide vaccination effort, smallpox was officially declared wiped out in 1980, the first human disease ever defeated. You can help your own defence force, too: wash your hands with soap for at least 20 seconds, cough or sneeze into your elbow, sleep well, and eat healthy food. Doctors can also prescribe an antibiotic, a medicine that kills bacteria, but antibiotics do not work on viruses at all, so they will not cure a cold. Your immune system is still the hero of the story, and every day it wins battles you never even notice.
