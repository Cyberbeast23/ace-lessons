Magazines and newspapers often share an interview article: the writer asks a real person smart questions, then turns the answers into an interesting piece with a headline, a hook, background, questions and answers with direct quotations, and a strong ending. Your subject today is someone on an emergency team, such as a paramedic, a 9-1-1 dispatcher, or an ER nurse. You can interview a real grown-up you know (with permission!), or invent a believable one using facts from today's reading.

Part 1: The Lesson

An interview article has six building blocks 🧩

A good interview article reads like a conversation, but it is carefully organized so the reader learns something and enjoys it.

1. Headline: a short, catchy title that tells the reader who the article is about
2. Hook (lead): an opening sentence or two that grabs attention: a vivid moment, a surprising fact, or a strong quotation
3. Background: who the person is, what their job is, where they work, and how long they have done it
4. Questions: open-ended questions (how, why, what, describe) that can't be answered with just yes or no
5. Answers + quotations: the person's exact words in quotation marks, mixed with the writer's own summary
6. Wrap-up: an ending that ties it together: advice from the person, a final quotation, or what the writer learned

Five journalist's moves

  • Ask open-ended questions: "Do you like your job?" gets "Yes." "What is the hardest part of your job, and how do you handle it?" gets a story.
  • Ask a follow-up: when an answer is interesting, dig deeper: "Can you give me an example?" or "How did that make you feel?"
  • Quote the best lines exactly: put the speaker's exact words in quotation marks. Summarize the rest in your own words (that's called paraphrasing).
  • Use strong speaker tags: instead of always "said," try explained, admitted, laughed, added, or recalled.
  • Be accurate and respectful: never invent words for a real person, and leave out private details like home addresses or patients' names.

Punctuating quotations: commas and periods go inside the closing quotation mark. "I love helping people," she said. She said, "I love helping people." A question mark goes inside if the quote is the question: He asked, "Are you okay?"

Model Interview Article (short version)

Calm in the Storm: Meet Paramedic Priya Sandhu
"The siren is loud, but inside the ambulance I try to be the quietest person there," Priya Sandhu told me with a smile.
Sandhu has been a paramedic in British Columbia for nine years. She works twelve-hour shifts, and on a busy day her crew may answer more than ten calls.
What is the first thing you do when you reach a patient?
"I look, I listen, and I introduce myself," she explained. "A scared person needs to know someone is in charge." Then she checks vital signs, like pulse and breathing.
What is the hardest part of the job?
Sandhu admitted that night shifts are tiring. "But when someone squeezes my hand and says thank you, I forget I'm tired," she added.
Before I left, I asked what kids can do to help. "Learn your address and how to call 9-1-1," she said. "You could be the first hero on the scene." I'll never hear a siren the same way again.

Notice: the hook is a strong quotation; the background gives facts about the person; the questions are open-ended (what, what is the hardest); the writer mixes exact quotations with paraphrasing; speaker tags vary (told, explained, admitted, added); and the wrap-up ends with advice and what the writer learned. (Priya Sandhu is an invented paramedic for this model.)

Step 2: Write your interview article

Goal: 250 to 350 words with all six building blocks. Include at least three open-ended questions (in bold or on their own lines), at least four direct quotations punctuated correctly, at least one follow-up question, at least three different speaker tags (explained, admitted, laughed...), at least one paraphrase (an answer in your own words), and a wrap-up. Bonus: read your article aloud to the person you interviewed and ask, "Did I get your words right?"
