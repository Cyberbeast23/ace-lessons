Yesterday you wrote a fast, loud commentary script. Today, Ace, you'll write something that is meant to be sung. Voyageurs sang while they paddled: a song kept fifty strokes a minute in perfect time, made the hours pass, and carried the stories of their journeys from one crew to the next. Many of their songs were ballads. A ballad is a story told as a song, in short verses (called stanzas) with a steady beat, rhyme and a refrain (a chorus that repeats). Ballads are one of the oldest kinds of writing in the world, and people still write them today, from folk songs to country music. Today's genre is a ballad: a true-feeling story with a beginning, a dangerous middle and an ending, told in rhythm and rhyme.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong ballad 🎶

Think of it as a story in six small pieces, with a chorus that keeps coming back.

1. Chorus (refrain): 2–4 catchy lines that repeat between verses and hold the song together
2. Opening verse: who, where and when: introduce the characters and the setting
3. Rising-action verse: the journey gets harder; trouble builds
4. Climax verse: the most exciting, dangerous moment of the story
5. Resolution verse: the problem is solved and things settle down
6. Final chorus with a twist: the refrain comes back, changed a little to show how things have changed

Five habits of great ballad writers

  • Tell the story in order: each verse moves the story forward. If you removed a verse, the listener should notice something missing.
  • Use a rhyme scheme: most ballads use ABCB: lines 2 and 4 of each verse rhyme (strong / song, flame / name). Lines 1 and 3 don't have to.
  • Keep a steady beat: many ballads alternate lines of about 8 syllables and 6 syllables. Clap the beat as you read: at DAWN we LEFT the LA-chine SHORE.
  • Never twist your sentences just to rhyme. ❌ "The canoe we did lift to the shore." ✅ "We lifted our canoe ashore." If a rhyme forces strange word order, find a different rhyme.
  • Show it with concrete images and strong verbs: ❌ "The trip was really hard." ✅ "Our shoulders burned like flame."

Songwriter's rule: read your ballad out loud, or sing it to a tune you know. If you trip over a line, the beat needs fixing.

Mini-lesson: grammar for songs and poems 🎼

Capitals: many songwriters capitalize the first word of each line, and proper nouns always get capitals: Lake Superior, the Fraser River, Fort William, Anishinaabe, Métis. In a title, capitalize the important words: The Song of the Long Portage.

Punctuate lines like sentences: a line break is not a full stop. If the sentence keeps going onto the next line, use a comma or no punctuation at all; when the sentence ends, use a period. A semicolon can join two complete thoughts: The falls appeared; we lifted our canoe.

Apostrophes: in a contraction, the apostrophe shows missing letters (the river's cold = the river is cold). In a possessive, it shows ownership (each rapid's name). Its (belongs to it) has no apostrophe.

Irregular past-tense verbs show up a lot in story songs: sing → sang (has sung), fight → fought, bring → brought, swim → swam, run → ran. Canadian spelling: colour, harbour, travelled, centre, metre.

Model ballad: "The Song of the Long Portage" (about 150 words)

Opening verseChorus (refrain)Rising action + climaxResolution + final chorus

At dawn we left the Lachine shore,
twelve paddlers, young and strong,
with sixty bundles stacked like stone
and Jacques to lead the song.

Pull on, pull on, pull hard and deep,
the river's cold and wide;
we'll paddle till the sun's asleep
and sing with every glide.

For weeks we fought the rushing stream;
our shoulders burned like flame.
Our guide, who knew each bend and rock,
would call each rapid's name.

Then, roaring white, the falls appeared;
we lifted our canoe to land.
Two bundles each, the strap pulled tight,
we ran on rock and sand.

At last we reached the great blue lake,
and Fort William came in view;
we traded cloth for beaver furs,
and the winterers cheered our crew.

Pull on, pull on, pull hard for home,
the river's cold and wide;
we'll race the snow, we'll beat the frost,
and sing with every glide.

Notice: lines 2 and 4 of every verse rhyme (strong / song, flame / name, land / sand, view / crew): that's ABCB. The chorus comes back at the end with a twist (pull hard for home, race the snow) to show the journey is over. Look for the simile stacked like stone, the strong verbs fought, roaring, ran, the irregular past tense fought and ran, the apostrophes in river's (river is) and rapid's (possessive), and the capitals on Lachine and Fort William.
