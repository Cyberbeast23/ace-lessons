🛶 Math Week 53: Paddles and Portages: Fur Trade Quartermaster's Math Lab

Ace, more than 200 years ago, brigades of canoes loaded with trade goods left Montreal every spring and paddled thousands of kilometres into the heart of the continent, then came back loaded with beaver pelts. Nothing could be wasted: every bundle had to be counted, every kilogram of food had to last, and every day of paddling had to be planned before the ice came back. The person who did that planning was the quartermaster, the one in charge of supplies. Today that's you. You'll use map scales written as ratios, rate × time with rounding up, stroke rates, converting pounds to kilograms, percent of a whole, round trips on a portage, unit prices in a trading system, integers, working backward from a percent, a missing value in a mean, speed with and against a river current, probability as a simplified fraction, the area of a trapezoid, and a percent decrease that repeats year after year.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your quartermaster's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (cm, m, km, kg, t, km/h, hours, minutes, °C, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change pounds to kilograms or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A canoe can't paddle 500 km in a day, and you can't take 3.75 trips. Sometimes you must round up!

Worked Example 1: A map scale written as a ratio

A scale of 1 : 1 000 000 means 1 cm on the map = 1 000 000 cm in real life. 100 cm = 1 m and 1 000 m = 1 km, so 1 000 000 cm = 10 000 m = 10 km. A 7 cm line on that map is 7 × 10 = 70 km.

Worked Example 2: Rate × time, then round up

A canoe covers 8 km/h for 10 hours a day = 80 km per day. To go 500 km: 500 ÷ 80 = 6.25 days. You can't arrive after "6.25 days" of planning, so you need 7 days of food. When the question is "how many do we need?", round up.

Worked Example 3: Strokes per minute

40 strokes per minute for 30 minutes = 40 × 30 = 1 200 strokes. If you only paddle 50 minutes of every hour, 3 hours = 3 × 50 = 150 paddling minutes.

Worked Example 4: Pounds to kilograms

Old fur-trade records used pounds (lb). 1 lb ≈ 0.454 kg. So 50 lb ≈ 50 × 0.454 = 22.7 kg.

Worked Example 5: What percent is left over?

A sled can hold 500 kg. The driver is 80 kg and the food is 120 kg. What's left for furs is 500 − 80 − 120 = 300 kg, which is 300 ÷ 500 = 0.6 = 60% of the load.

Worked Example 6: Round trips on a trail

You must carry 5 loads along a 2 km trail, one load at a time, and finish at the far end. You walk loaded 5 times, but you only walk back empty 4 times (you don't go back after the last load). Distance = 9 × 2 = 18 km.

Worked Example 7: Prices in a trading unit

If a knife costs 2 "skins" and a hatchet costs 3 skins, then 4 knives and 1 hatchet cost 4 × 2 + 3 = 11 skins. If 1 skin = 2 muskrat pelts, that's 11 × 2 = 22 muskrat pelts.

Worked Example 8: Integers and temperature

At dawn it is −20 °C. It warms up 12 degrees by noon (−20 + 12 = −8), then cools 15 degrees by night: −8 − 15 = −23 °C.

Worked Example 9: Working backward from a percent

Drying berries removes 80% of their mass, so 20% (0.2) is left. To end with 4 kg of dried berries: fresh × 0.2 = 4, so fresh = 4 ÷ 0.2 = 20 kg.

Worked Example 10: A missing value in a mean

The mean of 4 days is 50 km, so the total is 4 × 50 = 200 km. If three days were 45, 52 and 48 km (145 km), the fourth day was 200 − 145 = 55 km.

Worked Example 11: Paddling with and against a current

In still water a canoe goes 6 km/h. On a river flowing at 1 km/h, going downstream (with the current) the speed is 6 + 1 = 7 km/h; going upstream (against it) the speed is 6 − 1 = 5 km/h. 35 km upstream takes 35 ÷ 5 = 7 hours.

Worked Example 12: Probability as a simplified fraction

A bag has 6 red and 14 blue beads. The probability of picking red is 6/20. Divide top and bottom by 2: 3/10.

Worked Example 13: Area of a trapezoid

A trapezoid has two parallel sides a and b and a height h. Area = (a + b) ÷ 2 × h. With a = 3 m, b = 5 m, h = 2 m: (3 + 5) ÷ 2 × 2 = 8 m².

Worked Example 14: A percent decrease that repeats

If something drops by 10% each year, 90% (0.9) is left each year. Starting at 1 000: after 1 year 900, after 2 years 900 × 0.9 = 810. You multiply by 0.9 again each year; you do not just take away 10% of the starting number each time.
