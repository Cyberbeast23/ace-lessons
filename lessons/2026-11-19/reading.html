Paddles and Portages: The Voyageurs and the Fur Trade

In the 1600s, the most fashionable hat in Europe was a tall, stiff hat made of felt, and the best felt in the world came from the soft underfur of the North American beaver. Each tiny hair of that underfur has microscopic barbs, so when the hairs are pressed, heated and rolled, they lock together into a thick, waterproof cloth. European beavers had been hunted until they were rare. Across the Atlantic, however, beavers lived in almost every lake and stream, and the Indigenous peoples who knew those waters, including the Cree, the Anishinaabe and the Wendat, had been trading with one another along river routes for thousands of years. A trade in furs grew that would shape the map of Canada for two centuries.

Two rival companies. In 1670, King Charles II of England gave a group of merchants a charter, an official document granting the Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) the sole right to trade in all the lands whose rivers drain into Hudson Bay, an area covering more than a third of today's Canada. In theory this was a monopoly: no one else was allowed to trade there. For about a century, HBC traders mostly waited at forts on the shores of the bay, such as York Factory, while Indigenous trappers and traders paddled to them with furs. Traders based in Montreal had a different plan. They would go to the furs. By the 1780s, many of them had joined together as the North West Company, and they sent canoes deep into the interior, buying furs before they ever reached the bay.

The voyageurs. The paddlers who powered the Montreal trade were called voyageurs, French for "travellers." Most were French-Canadian men from farms along the St. Lawrence River; later, many were Métis, people with both Indigenous and European ancestors. A voyageur was usually short, because space in a canoe was precious, but astonishingly strong. Crews paddled about 50 strokes a minute, often for 14 hours or more a day, and they sang to keep the rhythm. Every hour or so they stopped for a short rest to smoke a pipe, and distances were sometimes measured in "pipes" instead of kilometres.

Carrying the load. Where a river was blocked by rapids or a waterfall, or where one river system had to be linked to the next, the crew made a portage: they unloaded everything and carried the canoe and cargo overland along a trail. Goods were packed in standard bundles called pièces of about 41 kg. A voyageur usually carried two at a time, held by a leather strap across his forehead called a tumpline, and he jogged rather than walked. The longest portages took days. Injuries were common, and drownings in rapids were so frequent that travellers passed wooden crosses on the riverbanks, marking the graves of crew members.

Partners and teachers. The fur trade could not have happened without Indigenous peoples. The birchbark canoe itself was an Indigenous invention: light enough to carry on a portage, yet strong enough to hold tonnes of cargo, and easy to repair with bark, spruce roots and gum from a spruce or pine tree. Indigenous guides knew the routes, and Indigenous women often served as an interpreter, translating between languages, and made the moccasins, snowshoes and food that kept traders alive. On the Prairies, Métis and First Nations people made pemmican, dried bison meat pounded and mixed with fat, a high-energy food that could last for years without spoiling. Pemmican fuelled the brigades the way gasoline fuels a truck.

Meeting in the middle. Montreal crews, nicknamed "pork-eaters" because they ate salt pork and dried peas, could not reach the far northwest and return before the rivers froze. So each summer they met the "winterers," voyageurs who lived year-round in the interior, at a rendezvous on Lake Superior, first at Grand Portage and later at Fort William. Furs went east; trade goods went west. The rendezvous was a few weeks of hard work, business meetings, feasting and music before both brigades raced home.

Reaching the Pacific. The search for new beaver country pushed traders toward what is now British Columbia. In 1793, North West Company trader Alexander Mackenzie, guided by Indigenous people along the way, reached the Pacific coast and painted a message on a rock: "Alex Mackenzie from Canada by land." Simon Fraser built posts in the BC interior and, in 1808, followed the river that now carries his name down to its mouth, with help from many First Nations along its canyons.

Timeline: Key moments in the fur trade

  • 1670: The Hudson's Bay Company receives its charter.
  • 1780s: Montreal traders join together as the North West Company.
  • 1793: Alexander Mackenzie reaches the Pacific by land.
  • 1808: Simon Fraser travels the Fraser River to the sea.
  • 1821: After years of bitter, sometimes violent rivalry, the two companies merge under the HBC name.
  • 1830s–1840s: Silk hats become fashionable, and the demand for beaver felt falls.

A mixed legacy. The fur trade built friendships, families and new communities, including the Métis Nation, and many Canadian cities began as trading posts. It also brought diseases that devastated Indigenous communities, caused overtrapping that emptied rivers of beavers, and laid the groundwork for settlers to take over Indigenous lands. Today, historians try to tell the whole story, using company records, voyageurs' songs and Indigenous oral histories together. Next time you paddle a canoe, or see a map dotted with names like Portage la Prairie or Fort St. James, remember the people who carried a country on their backs.
