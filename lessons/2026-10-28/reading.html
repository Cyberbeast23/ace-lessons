Sense, Think, Act: How Robots Work

In a car factory, a row of orange robot arms swings, welds and lifts all day without a coffee break. In a hospital, a robot carries medicine down the hallway and politely waits for the elevator. On Mars, a six-wheeled rover studies a rock, decides it looks interesting, and drives over for a closer look. These machines look nothing alike, but almost every robot follows the same simple pattern: it senses, it thinks, and it acts. Then it does it all over again, sometimes hundreds of times every second.

The first job of any robot is to notice what is around it. People use eyes, ears and skin; robots use sensors. A camera lets a robot see shapes and colours. An ultrasonic sensor sends out a squeak of sound too high for humans to hear and times the echo, the way a bat does, to detect walls and furniture. Touch sensors tell a robot when it has bumped into something, and a tiny device called a gyroscope tells it whether it is tilting or about to tip over. Sensors are not perfect, though. Dust, glare and noise can fool them, so engineers must calibrate each sensor, testing it against known measurements and adjusting it until its readings can be trusted.

The second job is thinking. A robot's "brain" is a computer that runs a program: a list of instructions written by people. The instructions often use simple rules like "IF the front sensor detects an object closer than 20 cm, THEN stop and turn right." A computer can execute millions of these instructions every second without getting bored or distracted. But it only does exactly what it is told. If a programmer forgets to include a rule for stairs, a robot vacuum may happily roll straight off the top step.

The third job is acting, and that is where actuators come in. An actuator is any part that makes a robot move: electric motors that spin wheels, small motors called servos that turn a joint to an exact angle, or pistons pushed by air or liquid. Gears connect the motors to the moving parts. A small gear driving a big gear trades speed for strength, which is why a robot arm can lift a heavy car door slowly and smoothly. Then the loop begins again. The robot senses whether its last move worked, and this feedback lets it correct itself, just as you adjust your hand when you reach for a glass.

Some robots need a person to steer them, like a remote-controlled toy. Others are autonomous, meaning they can make many decisions on their own. Mars rovers must be partly autonomous because a radio message takes several minutes, and sometimes more than twenty, to travel between Earth and Mars. If a rover waited for a human to approve every move, it might roll into a crater before the warning arrived. So engineers give it rules for avoiding obstacles and let it pick its own safe path across the rocks.

Canada is famous for one of the most important robots ever built. Canadarm, first launched on a space shuttle in 1981, was a robotic arm that caught satellites and moved heavy cargo in space. Its bigger cousin, Canadarm2, has helped build and repair the International Space Station, and a two-armed robot called Dextre handles delicate jobs so astronauts don't have to risk dangerous spacewalks. These arms were designed to mimic a human arm, with a shoulder, an elbow and a wrist, but they are many metres long and controlled with great care.

Surprisingly, the things that are hardest for robots are often the easiest for people. A computer can beat a world champion at chess, yet folding laundry, walking on a slippery sidewalk or picking up an egg without crushing it are huge challenges for a machine. That is why the most versatile robots today still work alongside people, not instead of them. Robots are excellent at jobs that are dangerous, dull or require perfect repetition, while humans are better at creativity, kindness and figuring out brand-new problems. The future of robotics may be less about robots replacing us and more about humans and machines working as a team.
