✍️ Writing Week 31: Robot Design Proposal: Invent a Helper Robot 🤖⚙️

Yesterday you wrote like a guide. Today, Ace, you'll write like a robotics engineer. Before anyone builds a real robot, engineers write a design proposal: a clear, organized plan that explains what problem the robot will solve, how it will sense the world, how its program will think, how it will act, and how it will be kept safe. A good proposal is so clear that another engineer could start building from it. You'll invent your own helper robot, sketch a labelled diagram, and write the proposal.

Part 1: The Lesson

A robot design proposal has six building blocks 🧩

Notice that the middle three blocks follow the robot's own loop: sense → think → act. That makes your proposal easy to follow.

1. Title: the robot's name plus what it does, like "FetchBot: A Robot That Finds Lost Socks"
2. Problem: who needs help, what goes wrong now, and why it matters
3. Sensors (sense): what the robot detects and which sensors it uses (camera, bump sensor, ultrasonic, temperature, microphone...)
4. Program rules (think): the decisions it makes, written as IF ... THEN ... rules
5. Actuators (act): the moving parts (motors, wheels, servos, grippers, gears) and what each one does
6. Safety test + conclusion: how you would test it, what keeps people safe, and why your robot is worth building

Five engineer's moves

  • Be specific and measurable: not "it is small" but "it is 30 cm tall and weighs 2 kg." Use metric units.
  • Use IF ... THEN rules: "IF the bump sensor is pressed, THEN the robot backs up 10 cm and turns left." Put a comma after the IF part.
  • Connect cause and effect: explain why each part is needed with words like because, so that, as a result, therefore.
  • Use technical vocabulary: sensor, actuator, program, servo, gear, battery, autonomous, prototype. Explain any word a reader might not know.
  • Think about what could go wrong: great engineers plan for problems. What if the battery runs low? What if a pet gets in the way?

Formal tone: a proposal is written for adults who might pay to build your robot, so avoid slang ("super cool," "kinda") and use complete, precise sentences.

Model Robot Design Proposal (short version)

Title + problemSensors (sense)Program rules (think) + actuators (act)Safety test + conclusion

GardenGuard: A Robot That Waters Plants While You're Away
When families go on vacation, their gardens often dry out, and plants that took months to grow can die in a single hot week.
GardenGuard uses a soil-moisture sensor on a 20 cm probe to check how wet the soil is, a temperature sensor to measure the heat, and a camera so it can follow the garden path.
Its program follows clear rules. IF the soil is dry, THEN GardenGuard waters that plant for 30 seconds. IF the temperature is above 30°C, THEN it checks the plants twice a day instead of once. Two motors drive its wheels, a small pump pushes water through a hose, and a servo turns the nozzle toward each plant.
To test it safely, I would first try GardenGuard on a few potted plants on the deck. It would stop and beep if its bump sensor touched a person or a pet. With GardenGuard on duty, no family would ever come home to a wilted garden again.

Notice: the title names the robot and its job; the problem explains who needs help and why; the sensors paragraph names three sensors with a measurement; the IF ... THEN rules show how it thinks, each with a comma after the IF part; the actuators say what moves and how; and the conclusion includes a safe test and a strong final reason.

Step 2: Write your proposal

Goal: 250 to 350 words with all six building blocks. Include at least three sensors; at least three IF ... THEN rules (with a comma after the IF part); at least two actuators; at least two measurements in metric units (cm, m, kg, °C, seconds); at least three cause-and-effect words (because, so that, as a result, therefore); at least four technical words; and one safety feature. Keep a formal tone. Bonus: give your robot a "what if" plan for a dead battery or a surprise obstacle!
