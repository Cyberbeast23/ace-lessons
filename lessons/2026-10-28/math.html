🤖 Math Week 31: Robot Engineer's Math Lab

Ace, every robot, from a vacuum that cleans the living room to a rover on Mars, runs on math. Engineers use math to choose gears, measure how far wheels roll, program turns, read sensor signals and decide how long a battery will last. Today you'll work like a robotics engineer with gear ratios and gear trains, wheel circumference (π), turning angles for robot paths, binary numbers, echo sensors, battery life, percentages, coordinates with negative numbers, loops in a program, rates and unit conversions, proportional reasoning, and an equation with brackets.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your engineer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (cm with cm, seconds with seconds)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Gear ratios

When two gears mesh, the teeth pass each other one for one. If a small 10-tooth gear drives a big 30-tooth gear, the small gear must turn 3 times to turn the big gear once. So the big gear turns 3 times slower, but with more turning force (torque).

Rule: output speed = input speed × (driver teeth ÷ driven teeth). 90 rpm × 10/30 = 30 rpm (rpm = rotations per minute).

Gear trains: when two gears are fixed on the same axle, they turn at the same speed. Multiply the fractions for each pair of meshing gears.

Worked Example 2: How far does a wheel roll?

In one full turn, a wheel rolls a distance equal to its circumference: C = π × diameter. Use π ≈ 3.14.

A wheel with diameter 10 cm rolls 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm per turn. In 20 turns: 31.4 × 20 = 628 cm = 6.28 m.

Worked Example 3: Programming a robot to draw a shape

A robot that drives around any closed shape and ends up facing the way it started has turned a full 360°. For a regular polygon with n sides, each turn is 360° ÷ n. A square: 360 ÷ 4 = 90°. A hexagon: 360 ÷ 6 = 60°.

Working backwards: if each turn is 40°, the shape has 360 ÷ 40 = 9 sides.

Worked Example 4: Binary, the language of robot brains

Computers store numbers using only 0 and 1. Each place is worth double the place to its right: ... 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.

Binary 10110 = 16 + 0 + 4 + 2 + 0 = 22. Add up the place values wherever there is a 1.

Worked Example 5: Echo sensors

An ultrasonic sensor sends out a "ping" of sound and times how long the echo takes to come back. The sound travels to the wall and back, so the distance to the wall is half the total distance travelled.

Sound travels about 343 m/s. If the echo takes 0.002 s: 343 × 0.002 = 0.686 m there and back, so the wall is 0.686 ÷ 2 = 0.343 m = 34.3 cm away.

Worked Example 6: Coordinates and negative numbers

On a grid, moving east adds to x and moving west subtracts. Moving north adds to y and moving south subtracts. If you go south past the x-axis, y becomes negative: from (2, 3), going 5 south lands at (2, 3 − 5) = (2, −2).

Turning right from north faces you east; turning right again faces you south. Keep track of which way the robot is facing!

Worked Example 7: Equations with brackets

3(x + 2) = 21. Undo the outside first: divide both sides by 3 (x + 2 = 7), then subtract 2 (x = 5). Check: 3 × (5 + 2) = 21 ✔
