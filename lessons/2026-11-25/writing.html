✍️ Writing Week 59: Petition for Change — Ask Your Community to Cut the Waste ♻️📝

Yesterday you wrote a nature documentary script. Today, Ace, you'll write something that ordinary citizens use to change real rules: a petition. A petition is a short, formal request to a person or group with the power to make a decision (a principal, a city council, a parks board). Many people read it and sign it to show they agree. The more clear, fair and specific it is, the more people sign, and the harder it is to ignore. In Canada, people even send petitions to Parliament! Today's genre is the petition.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong petition 📝

A good petition fits on one page. Someone should understand it in one minute, standing at a table with a pen in hand.

1. Title and addressee: a clear title that names the change, and who the petition is for ("To: Principal Okafor and the Parent Advisory Council")
2. Who we are: one sentence saying who is asking ("We, the undersigned students and families of Cedar Grove Elementary...")
3. Background facts: the problem, explained with real facts or numbers, not just feelings
4. The specific request: exactly what you want done, by whom and when. It must be something the reader actually has the power to do.
5. Reasons with evidence: two or three benefits, each backed up by a fact, example or number
6. Answering a concern: name the reader's most likely worry (cost, time, mess) and answer it fairly. Then add signature lines: Name · Grade or role · Signature.

Five habits of petition writers who get results

  • Ask for one clear thing: ❌ "Make our school greener." ✅ "Put a green bin beside every garbage can in the cafeteria by January 2027." A vague request is easy to say yes to and then do nothing about.
  • Use facts, not loaded language: yesterday's ad detectives know that words like disgusting or planet-destroying make readers suspicious. A number from a waste audit is far more convincing.
  • Keep a formal, respectful tone: no slang, no contractions, no insults. You are asking for help, not starting a fight.
  • Make it doable: a principal cannot ban plastic in all of Canada, but she can change what the cafeteria buys.
  • Be fair: admitting a real concern and answering it makes readers trust you more.

Petition writer's rule: before you finish, ask, "If I were the person reading this, would I know exactly what to do, and why it's worth doing?"

Mini-lesson: grammar for formal requests 🖋️

Formal tone, no contractions: in formal writing, spell words out. ❌ We don't want lots of junk going to the dump. ✅ We do not want so much waste to go to the landfill.

Parallel structure: items in a list should have the same grammatical shape. ❌ Composting saves money, less garbage, and it helps gardens. ✅ Composting saves money, reduces garbage and helps gardens.

Polite, precise modal verbs: We ask that the school install... or The council could... sounds respectful; You must... sounds like an order.

Hyphens in compound adjectives: when two words work together before a noun, join them with a hyphen: single-use cups, a three-bin station, a two-week trial. (But: The cups are used once.)

Canadian spelling: centre, neighbour, colour, litre, program, behaviour. The community centre could add a 20-litre green bin for our neighbours!

Model petition: "Green Bins for the Cedar Grove Cafeteria" (about 240 words)

Title, addressee + who we areBackground factsRequest, reasons + answering a concernSignature lines

PETITION: GREEN BINS FOR THE CEDAR GROVE CAFETERIA
To: Principal Okafor and the Cedar Grove Parent Advisory Council
We, the undersigned students and families of Cedar Grove Elementary, are asking for a simple change that will make our school cleaner and less wasteful.

On October 15, our Grade 5 class weighed one day of cafeteria garbage. The total was 40 kg. Fifteen kilograms of it, more than one-third, was food scraps that could have been composted. Right now, all of it goes to the landfill, where food rots without air and gives off methane, a gas that traps heat in the atmosphere.

We ask that the school place a green bin beside each of the four cafeteria garbage cans, starting with a two-week trial in January 2027.
A green bin would reduce our garbage, protect the climate and teach students lifelong habits. Our town already collects green-bin waste every week, so the scraps would become compost for local gardens.
Some people may worry that green bins will be messy or confusing. To prevent this, our class will make picture signs and take turns as "bin helpers" at lunch for the whole trial.

Name · Grade or role · Signature
____________ · ________ · ____________

Notice: the title names the change, and the addressee is someone who can actually say yes. The background uses facts from a real audit (40 kg, 15 kg) instead of loaded words. The request is specific: what (a green bin), where (beside each of four cans), when (a two-week trial in January). The reasons use parallel structure (reduce, protect, teach), and the writer answers a fair concern with a plan. Look for the hyphenated two-week trial and the formal tone: no contractions anywhere.
