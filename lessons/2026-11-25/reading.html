📖 Reading Week 59: Where Does It Go? The Hidden Journey of Your Garbage

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you asked questions while you read and figured out where each answer lives. Today you'll become an ad detective. Writers who want you to buy, believe or do something use persuasive techniques and loaded language. They aren't always lying, but they choose words to make you feel before you think. When companies use these tricks to make a product seem better for the environment than it really is, it's called greenwashing. Watch for these six moves:
  • 1. Loaded language: words packed with strong feelings, such as pure, natural, toxic, guilt-free, loves the Earth. Ask: "What is this word making me feel, and is there a fact behind it?"
  • 2. Vague claims: words that sound good but don't say anything you can check, such as eco-friendly, green, better for the planet. Ask: "Better than what? How much?"
  • 3. Bandwagon: "Everyone is doing it, so you should too!" Ask: "Does the number of people prove it's a good choice?"
  • 4. Testimonials and fuzzy experts: a famous person praises the product, or "experts agree" without saying who. Ask: "Is this person an expert on this topic? Which experts?"
  • 5. Numbers without context: "30% plant-based!" or "50% less plastic!" Ask: "30% of what? What about the other 70%? Less than what?"
  • 6. The fine print: tiny words marked with a star (*) that quietly limit the big claim. Always read the stars!
  • Word detective: Greek bios = "life" + Latin degradare = "to step down," so something biodegradable can be broken down by living things · Latin re = "again" + Greek kyklos = "circle," so to recycle is to send around the circle again · Latin in + cinis = "ashes," so an incinerator burns things to ash · Latin per = "through" + meare = "to pass," so something impermeable won't let anything pass through
  • Words in yellow are vocabulary words. Use the sentence around them (the context) to figure out what they mean.

Text 1: Where Does It Go? The Hidden Journey of Your Garbage

It's garbage day. You drag the cart to the curb, a truck rumbles by, a mechanical arm lifts and tips, and your trash vanishes. Gone! Except it isn't. In Canada, each person throws out roughly 700 kg of waste a year, and every gram of it has to go somewhere. Following your garbage is like following a mystery trail, and the clues reveal a lot about how communities work.

Not just a hole in the ground. Most of the garbage in Canada ends up in a landfill. A modern landfill is a carefully engineered place. Before any garbage arrives, workers line the bottom of a huge pit with thick clay and a heavy plastic sheet, layers that are almost completely impermeable. Why go to all that trouble? Rain trickles down through garbage and picks up chemicals, turning into a dark, polluted liquid called leachate. The liner stops leachate from seeping into the groundwater, and pipes collect it so it can be treated. Each day, bulldozers and heavy machines called compactors drive back and forth over the new garbage until it is tightly compacted, and then they cover it with a layer of soil to keep away gulls, rats and smells.

The hot dog that wouldn't rot. You might think buried food and paper simply rot away. In the 1970s and 1980s, an American archaeologist named William Rathje decided to find out. His team drilled into landfills and dug up what they found. They pulled out newspapers that were still readable after decades, and food, including hot dogs, that could still be recognized. The reason? Garbage in a landfill is squashed so tightly that very little air gets in, and the tiny living things that break down food and paper work very slowly without oxygen. When food does rot without air, it gives off methane, a gas that traps much more heat in the atmosphere than carbon dioxide does. Many landfills now pipe the methane out and burn it to make electricity.

The blue bin's journey. Recycling goes somewhere else: a material recovery facility, where conveyor belts carry a river of cans, cartons, paper and plastic past machines and workers. Spinning screens bounce flat paper one way. Powerful magnets yank out steel cans. A clever machine using a spinning magnetic field actually pushes aluminum cans off the belt, and optical sorters "see" different plastics and blast them aside with puffs of air. Each material is squeezed into giant bales and sold to factories that turn it into new products. But the system has a weakness: contamination. A greasy pizza box or a jar coated in sticky food residue can spoil a bale of clean paper, and a single plastic bag can wrap around a spinning screen and shut down the whole line. Putting something in the blue bin and simply hoping it can be recycled even has a nickname: wishcycling.

The green bin's secret. Food scraps and yard waste that go into a green bin travel to a composting facility. There, unlike in a landfill, the piles are turned and fed with air. With plenty of oxygen, microbes break the scraps down in a few months into dark, crumbly compost that gardeners and farmers spread on the soil, and far less methane is made.

Why the order matters. Experts often describe a waste hierarchy, a ranking of choices from best to worst: reduce (don't create the waste at all), reuse (use things again), recycle, then recover energy, and only last, landfill. Recycling is useful, but it still takes trucks, machines and energy to collect, sort, melt and remake materials. A refillable water bottle used 500 times beats 500 recycled bottles. That's why it pays to scrutinize any product that makes vague claims about being "green": the most important question is not only "Can this be recycled?" but "Do I need it at all?"
