Cedar, Salmon, and Stories: The First Peoples of British Columbia

Long before anyone drew a map with the words "British Columbia" on it, people were already living here. For more than ten thousand years, First Nations have fished the rivers, gathered berries in the mountains, and paddled the long, rocky coast. Today there are more than 200 First Nations in BC, and many of them have their own languages—over thirty First Nations languages are spoken in this province. The Haida live on the islands of Haida Gwaii, the Nisga'a in the Nass Valley, the Secwépemc in the dry Interior, and Coast Salish Nations such as the Musqueam and Squamish around the waters near Vancouver. The land and water that each Nation has lived on and cared for over many generations is called its traditional territory.

How do you keep a history for thousands of years? Many First Nations kept theirs through oral history: knowledge passed on by speaking, singing, and listening. An elder, an older person respected for wisdom and experience, might tell young people the story of a great flood, the name of every creek in the valley, or the deeds of an ancestor who lived hundreds of years ago. Listeners practised these histories carefully until they could repeat them exactly. Oral histories are so important that, in 1997, the Supreme Court of Canada agreed they can be used as evidence in court.

On the coast, one tree shaped daily life more than any other: the western red cedar. Its soft, straight wood resists rot, so people split it into planks for longhouses big enough for several families. Its inner bark was woven into baskets, mats, rope, and even rain-shedding clothing. Bark was often peeled in strips without cutting the tree down, so the cedar kept growing. Most impressive of all, a single giant log could be carved and steamed into a canoe. Some ocean-going canoes were more than fifteen metres long and could carry many paddlers and heavy loads between villages.

Salmon were just as important. Every year, millions of sockeye, chinook, coho, chum, and pink salmon swim back up BC's rivers to the streams where they hatched. Many Nations welcome the first fish of the season with a ceremony of thanks, and some return the bones to the river as a sign of respect. Fishers built wooden weirs and traps that caught fish but let enough salmon pass upstream to lay eggs. Taking only what you need, so that there will be enough for your grandchildren's grandchildren, is a kind of stewardship—caring for the land and water as something borrowed, not owned.

Many coastal Nations also hold the potlatch, a celebration where a family welcomes guests to mark an important event, such as giving a child a name, a wedding, or remembering someone who has died. There are songs, dances, speeches, and feasts, and the host family gives gifts to the guests, who act as witnesses. From 1884 to 1951, Canadian law banned the potlatch. It was an unfair law, but many families quietly kept their traditions alive, and today potlatches are celebrated openly and proudly.

First Nations are not only part of BC's past; they are leaders, artists, teachers, and scientists today. Some Nations have signed a treaty, a formal agreement with the governments of Canada and BC—the Nisga'a treaty took effect in 2000—but much of the province was never covered by treaties, so people are still working out fair agreements together. When a school assembly begins by naming whose traditional territory it is on, that is a small way of showing respect. The best way to learn more is the oldest way of all: listen to the people whose stories they are.
