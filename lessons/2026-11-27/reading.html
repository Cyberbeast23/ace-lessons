📖 Reading Week 61: Lost in the Ice: The Franklin Expedition and the Voices That Found It

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you made and revised predictions. Today's skill is one that historians use every day: comparing perspectives. A perspective (or point of view) is the way a person or group sees an event, shaped by what they saw, what they knew and what they wanted or believed. The same event can look completely different depending on who tells it, and sometimes the most important voices are the ones nobody listened to. Use the S-K-W-M lens for each group in the story:
  • S = Saw: What did this person or group actually witness with their own eyes? Were they there?
  • K = Knew: What knowledge or experience did they bring? (Someone who has lived in the Arctic all their life knows things a visitor doesn't.)
  • W = Wanted: What did they want, hope or believe? A person who badly wants a story to end one way may refuse to accept evidence that it didn't.
  • M = Missing: Whose voice is missing or ignored? Ask, "Who isn't being heard, and why?"
  • Perspective signal words: believed, insisted, claimed, according to, in their view, refused to accept, remembered. These words tell you you're reading someone's point of view, not just a fact.
  • Perspective checks: Today's passage has three teal 👁️ boxes. When you reach one, stop and think about whose eyes you're looking through.
  • Word detective: Latin per = "through" + specere = "to look," so a perspective is what you see when you look through someone's eyes · Latin credere = "to believe," so a credible source is believable · Latin com- = "together" + robur = "strength," so evidence that corroborates a story strengthens it · Latin vindicare = "to claim, to defend," so a person who is vindicated is finally proven right
  • Words in yellow are vocabulary words. Use the sentence around them (the context) to figure out what they mean.

Lost in the Ice: The Franklin Expedition and the Voices That Found It

On 19 May 1845, crowds cheered as two ships slipped down the River Thames in England. HMS Erebus and HMS Terror were the pride of the Royal Navy. They had iron-plated bows for smashing ice, steam engines taken from railway locomotives, heated cabins and enough food for three years. On board were 129 officers and sailors led by Sir John Franklin, a famous 59-year-old explorer. Their mission was to sail through the icy islands of the Canadian Arctic and find the Northwest Passage, a shortcut from the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific. In late July, whalers in Baffin Bay waved to the two ships. They were the last Europeans ever to see them.

The view from London. To the Admiralty, the navy office that planned the voyage, success seemed certain. Britain ruled the largest empire in the world, and finding the passage would add even more prestige to the navy's name. The officers believed that British science and courage could conquer any landscape. When two years passed with no news, few people worried. By 1848, though, worry turned to alarm, and the first rescue ships headed north. They found three lonely graves on Beechey Island, where the expedition had spent its first winter, but no sign of where the ships had gone.

👁️ Perspective check #1: The Admiralty "believed" British science and courage could conquer the Arctic. Who were they not asking for advice? Keep that question in mind.

A wife who would not give up. Lady Jane Franklin refused to accept that her husband was gone. In her view, the government was not searching hard enough, so she wrote letters, gave speeches and spent much of her own money on search ships. Her campaign was relentless. Over about ten years, more than 30 expeditions went looking for Franklin. They never found him alive, but by sailing, sledging and mapping, they charted thousands of kilometres of Arctic coastline that Europeans had never mapped before. In 1859, one of Lady Franklin's search parties found a note in a stone pile called a cairn at Victory Point, on King William Island. It said that Franklin had died on 11 June 1847, that the ships had been trapped in ice for nineteen months, and that the 105 survivors had abandoned them in April 1848 and were walking south.

What the Inuit saw. The Arctic was not an empty wilderness. It was home to Inuit families who had lived, hunted and travelled there for centuries. Inuit hunters saw things no British officer could have seen. Some remembered thin, exhausted strangers dragging heavy boats across the ice, too weak to hunt seals. Others told of a large wooden ship trapped in the ice, and later of a ship that sank near an island to the south. These memories were passed down carefully from elders to children, the way Inuit history has always been kept: through oral history, spoken and remembered rather than written.

👁️ Perspective check #2: The Inuit and the British were looking at the same events. What did the Inuit know about the Arctic that the expedition did not?

The messenger nobody wanted. John Rae was a Scottish doctor who explored the Arctic for the Hudson's Bay Company. Unlike most navy officers, Rae learned from Indigenous people. He travelled light, wore fur clothing, hunted his own food and slept in snow houses. In 1854, Inuit he met in the Arctic told him what had happened to Franklin's men: the starving sailors had died one by one on their march, and some had been driven to desperate acts to survive. To prove it, they sold Rae objects from the expedition, including silver forks and spoons engraved with officers' crests. Rae reported everything to the Admiralty. The British public was horrified. Lady Franklin and the famous author Charles Dickens dismissed the report. Dickens insisted that Inuit testimony could not be trusted and suggested that the Inuit themselves might have attacked the sailors. To many people in Victorian England, a story told by Inuit simply did not seem credible, even though the Inuit were the only people who had actually been there. Rae was treated coldly for the rest of his life. Unlike many other Arctic explorers, he was never knighted.

Listening at last. For more than 150 years, the ships stayed lost. Then historians began paying closer attention to Inuit oral history. Louie Kamookak, an Inuk historian from Gjoa Haven, spent decades gathering stories from elders, including stories his own great-grandmother had told. In 2014, a search led by Parks Canada, using sonar and Inuit knowledge, found the Erebus in about 11 metres of water, in the same area where Inuit stories had long said a ship went down. Two years later, an Inuk crew member named Sammy Kogvik told searchers that he had once seen a tall wooden pole sticking out of the sea ice in a bay on King William Island. The searchers checked, and there, 24 metres down, sat the Terror, amazingly intact. The modern discoveries did more than corroborate the old Inuit accounts; they showed that the Inuit had been right all along. Rae and the people who trusted Inuit testimony were finally vindicated.

👁️ Perspective check #3: Imagine telling the story of 2014 from the point of view of an Inuk elder whose grandparents' stories were ignored. How might that story sound different from a newspaper headline that said "Canada finds lost ship"?

What really killed them? Even scientists see the evidence from different points of view. In the 1980s, researchers from the University of Alberta examined the sailors buried on Beechey Island and found high levels of lead. They suggested that lead from the solder sealing the food tins, or from the ships' water system, slowly poisoned the crews. Other scientists disagree. A 2016 study of one sailor's thumbnail suggested that his lead levels were normal until the last weeks of his life, and that poor nutrition was a bigger problem. Most experts agree that many causes worked together: scurvy, lung disease, freezing cold, starvation and two summers in which the sea ice never melted. Meanwhile, DNA testing has begun to give names back to the dead. In 2024, scientists identified the remains of Captain James Fitzjames, one of the expedition's leaders.

Whose story is it? Today, the wrecks of the Erebus and the Terror are a National Historic Site in Nunavut, cared for jointly by Parks Canada and Inuit. Inuit guardians watch over the site, and in 2018, Britain transferred ownership of the ships to Canada and Inuit. For a long time, the Franklin story was told as a tale of heroic British explorers defeated by a cruel, empty land. Told from more perspectives, it becomes a different story: about people who wouldn't listen, a land that was never empty, and knowledge that waited more than a century and a half to be heard.

Model answer:
Event: John Rae's 1854 report.
Perspective 1 (a London reader, 1855): I read Dr. Rae's report in the newspaper, and I simply cannot believe it. Our brave British sailors, driven to such desperate acts? Mr. Dickens is right: the report comes from people who weren't British officers, so how can we trust it? I want to remember Sir John as a hero.
Perspective 2 (an Inuk hunter): We saw the strangers walking south, so thin they could barely pull their boats. We know how to hunt seals on the ice, but they did not. We told Dr. Rae the truth and showed him their silver spoons, yet the people far away called us liars.
Missing perspective: The Inuit perspective was missing, because many Victorians did not think Inuit testimony was "credible," even though "the Inuit were the only people who had actually been there." It matters because when people finally listened, the ships were found right where the stories said. Listening to more perspectives gives a truer, fairer history.
