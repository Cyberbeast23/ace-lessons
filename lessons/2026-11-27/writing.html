✍️ Writing Week 61: Poem for Two Voices — Lost in the Ice 🧭❄️

Yesterday you wrote a story where the reader chooses the path. Today, Ace, you'll write a poem that lets the reader hear two perspectives at once: a poem for two voices. It's written in two columns and performed by two readers. The left voice reads the left column, the right voice reads the right column, and lines in the middle are read by both voices together. The two speakers might be enemies, strangers or people from different centuries, but by the end, the reader hears both sides of the same story. It's the perfect genre for today's reading skill: comparing perspectives. Today's genre is the poem for two voices.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six building blocks of a poem for two voices 🎭

Think of it like a conversation where the two voices sometimes argue, sometimes echo and sometimes agree.

Voice 1 line⇄Voice 2 line→BOTH: shared line→contrast again…→the turn→BOTH: refrain
1. Two contrasting voices: choose speakers who see the same thing differently (what each one saw, knew and wanted).
2. Paired lines: line up matching lines side by side so the contrast jumps out: "Behind me, the Terror groans," / "Below me, the Terror waits,"
3. Shared lines (BOTH): lines the two voices say together, the things they agree on or both feel.
4. A refrain: a short line that repeats (often as a BOTH line) to tie the poem together, like a chorus in a song.
5. Sensory details and real facts: what each voice sees, hears and feels, built from true facts so the poem teaches as it sings.
6. The turn: a moment near the end where something shifts: the voices understand each other, or the reader sees the story in a new way.

Five habits of great two-voice poets

  • Mirror your lines: use the same sentence pattern on both sides, changing only the key words: her masts white with frost / her hull green with sea life.
  • Every word earns its place: poems are short, so choose strong verbs (groans, slices, bites) instead of weak ones (is, goes, gets).
  • Be fair to both voices: give each perspective real feelings and real reasons, even the one you disagree with.
  • Respect real people: when a voice belongs to a real person or community, like Inuit hunters, base what they say on facts from today's reading, and show them with respect.
  • Read it aloud with a partner: two-voice poems are meant to be performed. Your ear will catch clunky lines your eye misses.

Two-voice rule: if you covered up one column, the poem should feel like it's missing half its heart.

Mini-lesson: grammar for poems about history 🖋️

Possessive apostrophes: one ship → the ship's mast. Two ships → the ships' masts (add the apostrophe after the s). A name → Franklin's crew, Lady Franklin's letters.

Parallel structure: items in a list or in matching lines use the same form. ❌ The sailors were cold, hungry and they felt exhausted. ✅ The sailors were cold, hungry and exhausted.

Proper nouns and respectful names: capitalize Arctic, Inuit, Nunavut, King William Island. Inuk means one person; Inuit means more than one person, or the people as a whole. Ship names are capitalized and often written in italics: Erebus, Terror.

Canadian spelling: grey, metre, harbour, travelled, colour, centre. The grey ice stretched for a hundred metres beyond the harbour.

Model poem for two voices: "Ice Has a Long Memory" (about 170 words)

Voice 1 (left reader)Voice 2 (right reader)BOTH lines (read together)The turn

🧭 Voice 1: Sailor, HMS Terror, 1848🤿 Voice 2: Parks Canada diver, 2016
April 1848.September 2016.
I step down from the deck onto the ice,I step off the boat into the grey water,
BOTH: and the cold bites through everything.
Behind me, the Terror groans,Below me, the Terror waits,
her masts white with frost,her hull green with sea life,
trapped for nineteen months.silent for 168 years.
BOTH: Ice has a long memory.
We drag the sledge south, the harness biting my shoulders,I sink 24 metres, my flashlight slicing the dark,
our biscuits nearly gone,past plates still stacked on a shelf,
our compasses spinning, useless.past the captain's empty cabin.
An Inuk hunter watches us from a ridge. I wish we knew what he knows.An Inuk crew member remembered a mast in the ice. We listened, and here she is.
BOTH: Someone always knew where she was.
Will anyone ever find us?We found you.
BOTH: Ice has a long memory.

Notice: the paired lines mirror each other (her masts white with frost / her hull green with sea life), so the 168 years between the voices jump out. The facts are real: the ships were trapped for about nineteen months, compasses were nearly useless near the North Magnetic Pole, the Terror was found 24 metres down, and an Inuk crew member's memory led searchers to it. The refrain "Ice has a long memory" opens and closes the middle of the poem, and the turn comes when both voices mention Inuit knowledge. Then the last pair answers a question across 168 years.
