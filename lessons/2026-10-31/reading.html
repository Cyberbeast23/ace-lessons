Not Dinosaurs! The Sea Dragons of Ancient British Columbia

In 1988, Mike Trask and his daughter Heather were exploring the banks of the Puntledge River near Courtenay, on Vancouver Island, when they noticed bones poking out of the dark rock. The bones belonged to an elasmosaur, a long-necked reptile that had swum in the sea there about 85 million years ago. It was the first elasmosaur ever found in Canada west of the Rocky Mountains, and it would become one of the most famous fossils in the province.

During the Mesozoic Era, from about 252 to 66 million years ago, dinosaurs ruled the land. However, no dinosaur ever lived in the ocean. In the seas, other groups of reptiles reigned. People often call them "swimming dinosaurs," but that is a mistake. Ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and mosasaurs were reptiles, like dinosaurs, yet each belonged to its own branch of the reptile family tree. Their ancestors were land animals that returned to the water, and over millions of years their legs slowly changed into flippers.

Ichthyosaurs appeared first. With a pointed snout, a fin on the back and a tail shaped like a half-moon, many of them resemble modern dolphins, even though the two groups are not closely related. Scientists think animals that live in similar ways can evolve similar shapes, because a smooth, torpedo-like body is the best design for fast swimming. Ichthyosaurs could not crawl onto a beach to lay eggs, so they gave birth to live babies in the water. Some had eyes as wide as a dinner plate, which helped them hunt in dark, deep water. Along the Sikanni Chief River in northeastern BC, scientists found Shonisaurus sikanniensis, an ichthyosaur about 21 metres long. Its skeleton was so immense that digging it out took years of careful work.

Plesiosaurs had a short, wide body and four large flippers, which they may have flapped like underwater wings. Elasmosaurs, a type of plesiosaur, had an amazingly long neck. Elasmosaurus had about 72 neck bones; you have seven. Fish and squid-like animals were abundant in these warm seas, so a long neck may have let an elasmosaur sneak its small head close to a school of fish before its big body came near. Some plesiosaur fossils even contain piles of swallowed stones that may have helped them grind food or control how deep they floated.

Mosasaurs arrived last, near the end of the Mesozoic. They were cousins of today's monitor lizards and snakes. Some grew longer than a city bus, and they had powerful jaws with an extra row of teeth on the roof of the mouth to grip slippery prey. By the end of the Age of Reptiles, mosasaurs had become the top predators of the sea.

How do we know all this? A fossil usually forms when an animal sinks to the seafloor and is quickly buried in mud and sand. Over millions of years, minerals seep into the bones and the mud hardens into rock. Each fossil must be unearthed slowly with picks and brushes, wrapped in a plaster jacket and cleaned in a lab, a job that can take hundreds of hours. The Courtenay elasmosaur was studied for more than thirty years. Finally, in 2025, scientists showed that it was distinct from every other elasmosaur known, and they gave it a new name, Traskasaura sandrae, in honour of the family who found it.

None of these sea dragons survive today. Ichthyosaurs died out about 90 million years ago, long before the end of the Mesozoic. Plesiosaurs and mosasaurs disappeared 66 million years ago in the same mass extinction that wiped out the dinosaurs. Yet their legacy is still written in BC's rocks. In 2023, after a public vote, British Columbia named the elasmosaur its official provincial fossil. Thanks to dedicated fossil hunters like the Trask family, and museums that care for what they find, we can still meet these ancient giants. The next time you walk along a BC riverbank, look closely. The rocks might be hiding the next great discovery.
