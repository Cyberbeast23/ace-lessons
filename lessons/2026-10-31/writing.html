✍️ Writing Week 34: News Report: Fossil Find on the Riverbank! 📰🦴

Yesterday you wrote like a scientist. Today, Ace, you'll write like a reporter. When the Trask family found sea-reptile bones by the Puntledge River in 1988, people learned about it from news stories. A news report is built in a special shape called the inverted pyramid: the most important facts go at the top, and less important details come later. That way, a busy reader who stops after the first paragraph still knows what happened. Today you'll write your own news report about a fossil discovery.

Part 1: The Lesson

A news report has six building blocks 🧩

Reporters answer the 5 W's + H: Who? What? When? Where? Why? How? The biggest answers go in the first paragraph, called the lead. Then the story moves from most important to least important, like an upside-down pyramid. 🔻

1. Headline: a short, punchy title in the present tense, like "Father and Daughter Find Sea Monster in BC Riverbank"
2. Lead: one or two sentences that answer who, what, when and where
3. Key details: the most important facts next: how it happened, sizes, ages, numbers
4. Quote: the exact words of someone involved, inside quotation marks, with a speaker tag ("said Heather")
5. Background: extra facts that help readers understand (what an elasmosaur is, how old the rock is)
6. What's next: a closing that tells readers what will happen now (the museum, more digging, a new name)

Five reporter's moves

  • Be objective: report facts, not your opinion. Write in the third person ("the team," "she"), never "I think."
  • Be specific: not "a big fossil" but "a 12-metre skeleton about 85 million years old."
  • Keep paragraphs short: one to three sentences each. News is easy to skim.
  • Use strong, exact verbs: unearthed, discovered, announced, confirmed, instead of got or found again and again.
  • Attribute your facts: tell readers where information came from: "according to museum scientists," "said the palaeontologist."

Reporter's tone: clear, exciting but fair, and exact. Let the facts make the story amazing.

Model News Report (short version)

Headline + leadKey details + quoteBackgroundWhat's next

Hikers Spot Giant Sea Reptile Bones in Northern BC
Two hikers discovered the fossil skeleton of an ichthyosaur along a riverbank in northeastern British Columbia last Saturday.
The hikers noticed a row of round bones sticking out of a grey cliff. Scientists later confirmed that the backbone was about 8 metres long. "At first we thought it was just a pile of rocks," said one of the hikers. "Then we saw the shape of a spine."
Ichthyosaurs were dolphin-shaped reptiles that swam in ancient seas more than 200 million years ago. They were not dinosaurs. A famous 21-metre ichthyosaur, Shonisaurus, was found in the same region.
According to museum staff, a team will return next summer to wrap the bones in plaster and carry them to a lab, where cleaning could take more than a year.

Notice: the headline is short and in the present tense; the lead answers who, what, when and where in one sentence; the quote has quotation marks and a speaker tag; the background explains the science; and the ending tells readers what happens next.
