🦕 Math Week 34: Sea Dragon Palaeontologist's Math Lab

Ace, long before there were people, giant reptiles swam in the seas that covered parts of British Columbia. A 21-metre ichthyosaur called Shonisaurus was found by the Sikanni Chief River in northeastern BC, and a long-necked elasmosaur, now named Traskasaura, was found by the Puntledge River on Vancouver Island and became BC's official provincial fossil. Palaeontologists use math every day to date, measure and rebuild these animals. Today you'll work like one with geologic time, scale models, powers of ten, a missing value in a mean, the circumference of a circle, volume, rates, percents, probability, an equation with the variable on both sides, negative numbers, area in cm² and m², and working backwards with fractions.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your palaeontologist's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (cm with cm, m² with m²)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Scale models

A scale of 1:40 means every 1 cm on the model stands for 40 cm in real life. To find the model size, change the real size to cm, then divide by 40.

A 6 m animal: 6 m = 600 cm, and 600 ÷ 40 = 15 cm on the model.

Worked Example 2: Powers of ten

10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000. The little number (the exponent) tells you how many zeros follow the 1.

So 3 000 000 = 3 × 1 000 000 = 3 × 10⁶. Scientists write huge ages like this so they are easy to compare.

Worked Example 3: The missing value in a mean

Mean = total ÷ number of values, so total = mean × number of values.

Three fossils have a mean length of 5 m, and two are 4 m and 6.5 m. Total = 5 × 3 = 15 m, so the third is 15 − 4 − 6.5 = 4.5 m.

Worked Example 4: Circumference of a circle

The distance around a circle is its circumference: C = π × d, where d is the diameter and π ≈ 3.14.

A circle with a 10 cm diameter: C ≈ 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm.

Worked Example 5: Volume of a box shape

Volume of a rectangular prism = length × width × height, and the answer is in cubic units (m³).

A pit 3 m × 2 m × 0.5 m holds 3 × 2 × 0.5 = 3 m³ of rock.

Worked Example 6: Probability as a percent

Probability = number of ways you want ÷ total number of outcomes.

A bag holds 3 red and 7 blue stones. P(red) = 3 ÷ 10 = 0.3 = 30%.

Worked Example 7: The variable on both sides

Solve 2 + 3x = 10 + x. Take x away from both sides: 2 + 2x = 10. Take 2 away: 2x = 8, so x = 4. Check: 2 + 12 = 14 and 10 + 4 = 14 ✔

Worked Example 8: Negative numbers for depth

Use 0 for the sea surface and negative numbers for below it. Going down means subtract; going up means add.

At −50 m, dive 30 m deeper: −50 − 30 = −80 m. Then rise 20 m: −80 + 20 = −60 m.

Worked Example 9: Square metres to square centimetres

1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m² = 100 cm × 100 cm = 10 000 cm² (not 100!). Safest plan: change each length to cm first, then multiply.

A slab 0.5 m × 0.2 m = 50 cm × 20 cm = 1000 cm².

Worked Example 10: Working backwards with fractions

"I spent 1/2 of my money, then 1/3 of what was left, and had $10 remaining." After spending 1/2, 1/2 is left. 1/3 of that is 1/6, so 1/2 − 1/6 = 1/3 of the money remains. 1/3 of the total = $10, so the total was $30. Check: 15 spent, 5 spent, 10 left ✔
