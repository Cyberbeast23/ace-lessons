✍️ Writing Week 51: Artist's Statement — Fold by Fold: The Story Behind My Creation 🦢🖼️

Yesterday you built a dichotomous key like a scientist. Today, Ace, you'll write like an artist. Walk into any gallery or museum and you'll see a small card or panel beside the art: an artist's statement. It's a short piece of writing, in the artist's own voice, that tells viewers what the work is, what inspired it, how it was made, and why the artist made the choices they did. A good statement doesn't brag ("This is the best crane ever!") and doesn't just list steps. It helps a stranger see the work more deeply. It's a brand-new genre for you: part description, part explanation, part reflection, all in the first person ("I folded…", "I chose…").

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong artist's statement 🖼️

Think of it as a guided tour of your artwork, given by the person who knows it best: you.

1. Title + medium: the name of the work and what it's made of ("one uncut 15 cm square of washi paper")
2. Description: what a viewer will see: shape, size (metric!), colour, texture, how it stands or hangs
3. Inspiration: the idea, memory, person, place or reading that gave you the idea
4. Process + challenge: how you made it, with precise technique words (valley fold, squash fold), and one problem you solved
5. Choices + meaning: why you chose this colour, size, pose or paper, and what those choices express
6. Invitation to the viewer: what you hope people notice, feel or wonder about when they look

Five habits of strong artist's statements

  • First person, present tense for the work: "In this piece, I show…" Use past tense for what you did: "I folded it twelve times before…"
  • Precise art and technique words instead of vague ones. ❌ "I did some folds and stuff." ✅ "I made a squash fold, then two inside-reverse folds for the neck and tail."
  • Explain choices, not just steps: every "I chose…" should be followed by a "because…" or "so that…".
  • Show, don't brag: let the details do the work. ❌ "It looks amazing." ✅ "The wings tilt 30° upward, as if the crane has just pushed off from water."
  • Be honest about the struggle: viewers love hearing what went wrong and how you fixed it. It shows patience (耐心!).

Artist's rule: after reading your statement, a viewer should look at your work again and notice something they missed the first time.

Mini-lesson: grammar for writing about art 🎨

Titles of artworks: when you type, put the title of a piece of art, a book or a film in italics: Crane at First Light. When you write by hand (or can't use italics), use quotation marks: "Crane at First Light." Capitalize the first word, the last word and every important word in between, but not short words like a, the, at, of, and (unless they come first or last).

Commas between coordinate adjectives: if two adjectives describe a noun equally and you could put and between them (or swap their order), separate them with a comma: a crisp, thin sheet (crisp and thin ✔). If you can't, no comma: a bright red crane ("bright and red" ✘ because bright describes the red).

Capitalize languages and nationalities: Japanese paper, a Canadian artist, an English title. But art words stay lowercase: origami, valley fold, washi.

Its vs. it's, one more time: its = belonging to it (the crane spread its wings); it's = it is (it's made of one square). Canadian spelling: colour, centre, metre, grey, favourite.

A Thousand Small Steps
Medium: one uncut 15 cm square of grey-blue Japanese washi paper. My crane stands 6 cm tall on a plain white card. Its wings are spread wide and tilted slightly upward, and the soft paper has a faint, cloudy texture that catches the light. I was inspired by Sadako Sasaki, the girl from Hiroshima who folded paper cranes as a wish to get well. When I read her story, I wanted to fold one crane slowly and carefully, as if it were the first of a thousand. The hardest step was the petal fold. My first three tries tore at the centre because I pressed too hard, so I learned to make every crease gently, then sharpen it with my thumbnail. I chose grey-blue instead of a bright colour because it reminds me of early morning, when the day is quiet and hopeful. I left the crane small so that viewers have to lean in close, the way you lean in to hear someone whisper a wish. When you look at my crane, I hope you notice that it's made from one sheet with no cuts. Then I hope you wonder: what wish would you fold into it?

Notice: the title is in italics; the medium and size are precise and metric; the writer uses the first person; the inspiration names a real source; the process includes one honest problem and how it was solved; every choice comes with a because or so that; the ending speaks directly to the viewer. Look for the comma in faint, cloudy texture (faint and cloudy ✔), the capital J in Japanese, and its vs. it's.
