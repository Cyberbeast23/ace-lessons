🦢 Math Week 51: Fold by Fold: Origami Designer's Math Lab

Ace, take one square of paper. No scissors, no glue, no tape. With only folds, it can become a crane, a hopping frog, a box, or even a solar panel that opens up in space! The Japanese word origami (or-ih-GAH-mee) means "folding paper," and every origami designer is secretly a mathematician: each crease is a straight line, each corner is an angle, and each fold makes a shape that is congruent (exactly the same size and shape) to the one it lands on. Today you are the designer in an origami studio. You'll use area of squares and triangles, order of operations, doubling (powers of 2), angles you can fold, the angle sum of a triangle, division that must round up, proportions for scaling a model, how area grows when you scale up, the area of a parallelogram, percent and profit, a missing value in a mean, and perimeter after folding into equal parts.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your origami designer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (mm, cm, m, cm², m², degrees, minutes, $, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change metres to centimetres or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A paper crane is a few centimetres tall, not a few metres!

Worked Example 1: Area of a square, and of half a square

A square sheet 10 cm on each side has area 10 × 10 = 100 cm².

Fold it corner to corner along the diagonal and you get two congruent triangles. Each one is half the square: 100 ÷ 2 = 50 cm². (Triangle area = base × height ÷ 2 = 10 × 10 ÷ 2.)

Worked Example 2: Order of operations

Brackets first; inside the brackets, × and ÷ before + and −. 50 − (2 × 6 + 3 × 4) = 50 − (12 + 12) = 50 − 24 = 26.

Worked Example 3: Doubling (powers of 2)

Every time you fold a sheet in half, the number of layers doubles: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16. After n folds there are 2 × 2 × … × 2 (n times) = 2ⁿ layers. After 5 folds: 2⁵ = 32 layers.

If one sheet is 0.1 mm thick, 32 layers are 32 × 0.1 = 3.2 mm thick.

Worked Example 4: Folding angles in half

Each corner of a square is a right angle (90°). Folding along the diagonal splits it into two equal angles: 90 ÷ 2 = 45°. The triangle you get has two equal sides and two equal angles, so it is an isosceles right triangle (45°, 45°, 90°).

Folding an edge onto a crease splits that angle in half again: 45 ÷ 2 = 22.5°.

Worked Example 5: The angles of a triangle add to 180°

If a folded triangle has angles of 90° and 30°, the third angle is 180 − 90 − 30 = 60°.

Worked Example 6: Division that must round UP

You need 50 cranes and can fold 8 a day. 50 ÷ 8 = 6.25 days. After 6 days you have only 48, so you need 7 days. When you can't stop partway, round up.

Worked Example 7: Scaling a model (proportions)

Fold the same model from a bigger square and every length grows by the same factor. A 15 cm square makes a frog 5 cm long. A 30 cm square is 30 ÷ 15 = 2 times as wide, so the frog is 5 × 2 = 10 cm long.

Worked Example 8: When lengths double, area grows 4 times

A 10 cm square has area 100 cm². A 20 cm square has area 400 cm². The side grew 2 times but the area grew 2 × 2 = 4 times. If the side grows k times, the area grows k × k times.

Worked Example 9: Area of a parallelogram

A parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides. Cut the triangle off one end and slide it to the other end: it becomes a rectangle! So area = base × height, where the height is perpendicular (at 90°) to the base, not the slanted side.

Base 5 cm, height 2 cm, slanted side 3 cm: area = 5 × 2 = 10 cm² (the 3 cm is a distractor!).

Worked Example 10: Percent, then profit

75% of 20 = 0.75 × 20 = 15. If you sell 15 bookmarks for $2 each, you take in $30. If the paper cost $8.50, your profit is 30 − 8.50 = $21.50.

Worked Example 11: A missing value in a mean

Mean = total ÷ how many. If 4 scores must have a mean of 7, the total must be 4 × 7 = 28. If the first 3 add to 20, the last one must be 28 − 20 = 8.

Worked Example 12: Folding into equal parts

Fold a 12 cm square in half one way and in thirds the other way. The creases make rectangles 12 ÷ 2 = 6 cm by 12 ÷ 3 = 4 cm. Each one has perimeter 2 × (6 + 4) = 20 cm and is 1/6 of the sheet.
