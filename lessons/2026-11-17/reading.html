Fold by Fold: How a Square of Paper Became Art, Math and Space Science

Pick up a plain square of paper. You are not allowed to cut it, glue it or tape it. All you can do is fold. In a few minutes, that flat square can become a crane with outstretched wings, a frog that hops when you press its back, or a box with a lid. In the hands of an engineer, the same idea can become a solar panel that opens up in outer space. The art of paper folding is called origami, from the Japanese words for "fold" and "paper," and its story connects artists, mathematicians, doctors and rocket scientists.

A precious gift. Paper was invented in China about 2 000 years ago, and papermaking reached Japan roughly 1 400 years ago. At first, paper was rare and expensive, so folding it was something special. In Japan, folded paper decorations were attached to gifts, and paper butterflies were part of some wedding ceremonies. Over hundreds of years, the tradition grew from ceremonies into a pastime that families shared. In 1797, a book called Senbazuru Orikata ("How to Fold a Thousand Cranes") showed readers how to fold chains of cranes still joined together at the wingtips. Paper folding also grew up in other places, such as Spain, where children fold a little paper bird called the pajarita, and Germany, where teachers in the 1800s used folding to help young children learn about shapes.

The master and his symbols. For most of history, people learned origami by watching someone else fold. That changed because of a Japanese artist named Akira Yoshizawa (1911–2005). As a young man working in a factory, he used paper folding to help explain geometry to new workers. He went on to design tens of thousands of models, and he was so meticulous that he would study a real animal for days before he folded it, so his paper gorillas and birds seem almost alive. He even invented wet-folding: dampening thick paper so it can be shaped into soft curves. Most importantly, Yoshizawa created a set of symbols for drawing folding instructions: a dashed line for a valley fold (the paper folds toward you, making a V), a line of dashes and dots for a mountain fold (the paper folds away from you), and arrows to show where the paper moves. Two writers, Samuel Randlett and Robert Harbin, helped spread these symbols in their books, and today folders around the world use them. A child in British Columbia can follow a diagram drawn in Japan or Brazil without reading a single word of Japanese or Portuguese.

Hidden math. Every crease in origami is a straight line, and every flat-folded model hides patterns that mathematicians have proven are always true. Here is one, discovered by a Japanese scientist named Jun Maekawa (and, separately, by the French mathematician Jacques Justin): if you unfold a model that lies flat and look at any point inside the paper where creases meet, the number of mountain folds and the number of valley folds will always differ by exactly two. Count them yourself, and you'll see it works every time. Rules like this let mathematicians predict whether a crease pattern can fold flat before anyone picks up a sheet of paper.

The physicist who folds. For a long time, some models were thought to be almost impossible, such as a beetle with six legs, two antennae and horns, all folded from one uncut square. Robert Lang, an American physicist who had worked on lasers and spent time at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, helped change that. He wrote computer programs that use geometry to plan where every flap must come from on the square, and in 2001 he left his job to become a full-time origami artist. His insects, with their intricate legs and wings, are made of hundreds of folds, and each one is planned with math before the paper is ever touched.

Origami in space and medicine. Rockets are narrow, but space telescopes and solar panels need to be wide. Engineers solved that problem with folding. In the 1970s, the Japanese astrophysicist Koryo Miura designed a pattern now called the Miura fold. It covers a sheet with slanted parallelograms, and when you pull two opposite corners, the whole sheet opens in one smooth motion; push them together and it closes again. A collapsible solar panel using this idea flew on a Japanese satellite launched in 1995, and some maps are folded the same way so they never get stuck. Later, engineers at NASA worked with origami artists, including Robert Lang, to test folding solar arrays. The James Webb Space Telescope's sunshield, about the size of a tennis court, also had to be folded carefully to fit inside its rocket and then unfold in space. Here on Earth, researchers at the University of Oxford designed a tiny, folded tube called a stent that can travel through a blood vessel while folded small and then open up to hold the vessel wide.

A thousand cranes for peace. Origami can also carry a message. In Japan, there is a saying that anyone who folds a thousand paper cranes may be granted a wish. In 1955, a twelve-year-old girl named Sadako Sasaki, who had become very sick from the effects of the atomic bomb dropped on her city of Hiroshima ten years earlier, folded paper cranes in the hospital, hoping to get well. Her family says she folded more than a thousand. Sadly, she died that year, but her classmates helped raise money for a statue of her holding a golden crane in the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park. Today, people around the world, including many Canadian students, fold strings of cranes and send them to Hiroshima as a wish for a world without war. Sadako's story became an inspiration to millions of people.

So the next time you crease a square of paper, remember how much is folded inside it: hundreds of years of tradition, an artist's set of symbols, mathematical rules that never fail, solar panels in space and a wish for peace. Origami asks for patience and careful hands, but it gives back something surprising: proof that a simple sheet of paper, folded one step at a time, can become almost anything you imagine.
