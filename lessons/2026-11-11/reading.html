Moon Power: Why the Bay of Fundy Has the Highest Tides on Earth

At Hopewell Rocks in New Brunswick, visitors can walk on the sea floor in the morning, weaving between tall stone pillars shaped like giant flowerpots. Six hours later, the same spot is under more than ten metres of water, and people paddle kayaks where they once stood. This is the Bay of Fundy, home of the highest tides on Earth. At Burntcoat Head in Nova Scotia, the difference between low and high tide can reach about sixteen metres, about the height of a five-storey building. But why does the ocean rise and fall at all, and why here most of all?

The answer begins about 384 000 kilometres away, with the Moon. Every object with mass pulls on every other object; this pull is called gravity. The Moon's gravity tugs on all of Earth, but it pulls hardest on the side of Earth that faces it. Solid rock can barely stretch, but ocean water can move, so water piles up into a bulge on the side nearest the Moon. A second bulge forms on the far side of Earth, where the Moon's pull is weakest and the water is, in a sense, left behind. As Earth spins, each coastline passes through both bulges every day. That is why most places have two high tides and two low tides daily. Because the Moon also moves along its orbit, the tides arrive about fifty minutes later each day.

The Sun pulls on the oceans too, though its effect is less than half as strong as the Moon's because it is so much farther away. When the Sun, Earth and Moon line up, at the new moon and full moon, their pulls combine and cause extra-high and extra-low spring tides (which have nothing to do with the season). When the Sun and Moon pull at right angles, at the quarter moons, they partly cancel each other out, and the gentler neap tides follow.

In the open ocean, the tidal bulge is less than a metre high, so something special must happen in the Bay of Fundy. The first reason is shape. The bay is long and gets narrower and shallower toward its end, so it funnels the incoming water into a smaller and smaller space, and the water has nowhere to go but up. The second reason is resonance. Water in a bay sloshes back and forth, like water in a bathtub when you slide forward and back. The Bay of Fundy's natural slosh takes about thirteen hours, very close to the tide's rhythm of about twelve and a half hours. Like a parent pushing a swing at just the right moment, each new tide gives the sloshing water another push, so the motion grows larger and larger.

The results are dramatic. During each tide, roughly one hundred billion tonnes of seawater flow in and out of the bay, more than the flow of all the world's rivers combined. In some rivers that empty into the bay, the incoming tide arrives as a single wave called a tidal bore, which rolls upstream against the current. The swirling water also carries huge amounts of sediment, tiny bits of mud and sand, which settle to form the bay's famous red-brown mud flats. These same currents slowly erode the cliffs, carving shapes like the Hopewell "flowerpots."

Those mud flats are part of a rich intertidal zone, the area that is underwater at high tide and exposed at low tide. Tiny mud shrimp live there by the billions. Each summer, hundreds of thousands of small shorebirds called semipalmated sandpipers stop in the bay to gobble them up, nearly doubling their weight before flying nonstop to South America. Every tide also mixes the water and helps replenish the nutrients that feed plankton, fish and whales.

People have long wondered whether the bay's tides could be used to make electricity. From 1984 until 2019, a power station at Annapolis Royal in Nova Scotia used tidal water to spin a turbine. Today, engineers are testing underwater turbines that harness the fast currents, the way windmills capture the wind. Tidal power is renewable and as predictable as the Moon itself, but the currents are so powerful that they can damage machines, and scientists must make sure turbines do not harm fish and marine mammals. The next time you see the Moon, remember: it is quietly lifting entire oceans.
