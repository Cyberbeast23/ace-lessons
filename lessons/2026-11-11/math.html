🌊 Math Week 45: Moon Power: Tides of the Bay of Fundy Tide Engineer's Math Lab

Ace, twice a day, the ocean on Canada's east coast breathes in and out. In the Bay of Fundy, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, the water can rise higher than a four-storey building and then drain away until boats sit on the mud. These are the highest tides in the world, and they happen because the Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans. Today you are a tide engineer. You'll use negative numbers for the tidal range, elapsed time for tide cycles, rates in cm per minute, the volume of a tidal basin, energy from a tidal turbine, percent decrease in a tide-pool survey, speed and time for a tidal bore, degrees per day for the Moon's orbit, map scale, a two-step equation, the mean from a tide table, GST + PST on beach gear, the sailors' "rule of twelfths," spring and neap tides, and the mass of seawater.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your tide engineer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (m, cm, km, km/h, min, h, m², m³, kg, kWh, MW, °, $, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change metres to centimetres or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A tide might rise 15 m, but not 15 km!

Worked Example 1: Range with negative numbers

Tide heights are measured from a reference line called chart datum. A very low tide can be below that line, so it is negative. Range = highest − lowest, and subtracting a negative is the same as adding.

High 6.5 m, low −0.5 m: 6.5 − (−0.5) = 6.5 + 0.5 = 7 m.

Worked Example 2: Working in minutes

Change hours and minutes into minutes before you divide: 1 h = 60 min, 1 day = 24 × 60 = 1 440 min.

2 h 30 min = 120 + 30 = 150 min. How many complete 150-minute cycles fit in one day? 1 440 ÷ 150 = 9.6, so 9 complete cycles (the 0.6 isn't a whole cycle).

Worked Example 3: Volume of water in a basin

When the tide rises, the extra water in a bay is about area × rise. Make sure all lengths are in metres first (1 km = 1 000 m).

A basin 2 km × 1 km is 2 000 m × 1 000 m = 2 000 000 m². If the tide rises 5 m: 2 000 000 × 5 = 10 000 000 m³ = 10 million m³.

Worked Example 4: Power, energy and kilowatt-hours

Energy = power × time. 1 megawatt (MW) = 1 000 kilowatts (kW). A 1 MW machine running for 1 hour makes 1 MWh = 1 000 kWh.

A 2 MW turbine for 3 h: 2 × 3 = 6 MWh = 6 000 kWh. If a home uses 20 kWh a day: 6 000 ÷ 20 = 300 homes for a day.

Worked Example 5: Percent decrease

Percent decrease = (old − new) ÷ old × 100. Always divide by the old (starting) value.

From 40 crabs to 30 crabs: decrease = 10. 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25, so a 25% decrease.

Worked Example 6: The rule of twelfths

Sailors know that a tide doesn't rise at a steady speed. Over about 6 hours it rises 1/12, 2/12, 3/12, 3/12, 2/12, 1/12 of the range, hour by hour: slow, then fast in the middle, then slow again.

Range 6 m: in the 1st hour it rises 1/12 × 6 = 0.5 m; in the 3rd hour 3/12 × 6 = 1.5 m.

Worked Example 7: Speed, distance and time

Time = distance ÷ speed. If the speed is in km/h, the answer is in hours; multiply by 60 to get minutes.

2 km at 8 km/h: 2 ÷ 8 = 0.25 h. 0.25 × 60 = 15 min.

Worked Example 8: Two taxes at once

In British Columbia many items have 5% GST + 7% PST = 12% tax. Multiply the subtotal by 1.12, then round to the nearest cent.

$25.00 × 1.12 = $28.00.
