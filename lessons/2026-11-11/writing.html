✍️ Writing Week 45: Data Report — Read the Chart, Explain the Pattern 🌊📊

Yesterday you wrote a biographical sketch. Today, Ace, you'll try something brand-new: a data report. Scientists, engineers and journalists all have to turn a table of numbers into clear sentences that anyone can understand. A data report does three jobs: it tells the reader what the numbers show, backs that up with exact figures, and explains why the pattern happens and why it matters. You just read how the Moon makes the tides; now you'll be the scientist explaining the data.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong data report 📊

A data report is not a list of every number in the table. It is built around one big headline finding, like "The tide pool warmed quickly while the ocean was away."

1. Headline finding: a title and opening sentence that state the most important thing the data shows
2. Data source: what was measured, where, when, and how (so the reader can trust it)
3. Overall pattern: the big trend: rising, falling, peaking, staying steady, or one group much bigger than the others
4. Precise numbers: the highest, the lowest, and a comparison or calculation (difference, how many times bigger, mean)
5. Scientific explanation: why the pattern happens, using science you know
6. So what? (conclusion): why the finding matters, a prediction, or a new question to investigate

Four tricks data writers use

  • Pattern first, numbers second: say what's happening ("The tides shrank all week"), then prove it ("from 14.8 m on Day 1 to 11.0 m on Day 7").
  • Do a little math for the reader: "a drop of 3.8 m," "about three times as many," "a mean of 8.1 m."
  • Precise trend verbs: rose, climbed, peaked, levelled off, dipped, fell, plunged are clearer than "went up" and "went down."
  • Separate facts from guesses: use "the data shows…" for facts and "this suggests…" / "one possible reason is…" for explanations.

Data writer's rule: every number you include should help prove your headline finding.

Mini-lesson: comparing with numbers 🔧

Comparatives and superlatives: use -er / more to compare two things and -est / most for three or more. Never double them up: ❌ more higher, ✅ higher. And remember: than compares; then is about time.

Fewer or less? Use fewer for things you can count (fewer crabs, fewer days) and less for amounts you can't count one by one (less water, less seaweed).

Writing numbers: use numerals with units (12.5 m, 384 000 km), spell out a number that starts a sentence (Forty-two birds landed.), and hyphenate a measurement used as an adjective before a noun: a 16-metre tide (but the tide rose 16 metres). Canadian spelling: metre, centimetre, kilometre, centre.

❌ On day 7 the tide was more lower then on day 1.
✅ On Day 7 the tide was lower than on Day 1.

Stranded in the Sun: A Tide Pool Heats Up
When the ocean pulled away, one small tide pool turned into a warm bath. Our class measured the water in a rock pool every hour from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m. on a sunny June day, starting just as the tide went out. The data shows the pool warming steadily for four hours and then cooling quickly. It began at 10 °C, the same as the sea, and climbed to 19 °C by 2 p.m., a rise of 9 degrees, almost twice as warm as at the start. At 3 p.m., when the incoming tide refilled the pool, the temperature plunged to 11 °C. One possible reason is that a shallow pool holds much less water than the ocean, so sunlight heats it faster. When the cold seawater returned, it mixed with the warm water and cooled it. This suggests that sea stars and snails in high pools must survive big temperature swings every day. Next, we could test whether deeper pools warm more slowly.

Notice: the title and first sentence give the headline; the source says what, where, when and how; the writer gives the pattern first and then exact numbers with units; a calculation (a rise of 9 degrees) helps the reader; "the data shows" is used for facts and "one possible reason" for the explanation; the ending looks ahead with a new question.
