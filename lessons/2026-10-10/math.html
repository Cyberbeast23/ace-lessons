Ace, every song you have ever heard is secretly a maths problem! Sound is a vibration travelling through the air, and musicians, instrument makers and sound engineers use numbers to control it. Worked Example 1: Frequency and the time for one vibration

When a guitar string is plucked, it moves back and forth very quickly. The number of vibrations each second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). 1 Hz = 1 vibration per second.

A string at 200 Hz makes 200 vibrations every second, so in 3 seconds it makes 200 × 3 = 600 vibrations.

The time for one vibration is called the period: period = 1 ÷ frequency. At 200 Hz, one vibration takes 1 ÷ 200 = 0.005 s = 5 milliseconds (1 s = 1000 ms). Higher frequency = higher pitch = shorter period.

Worked Example 2: The ratios inside music

Notes that sound good together have frequencies in simple ratios. Over 2500 years ago, Greek thinkers (the followers of Pythagoras) and Chinese musicians both discovered this.

Octave = 2 : 1. Going up one octave doubles the frequency: 220 Hz → 440 Hz. Going down one octave halves it: 220 Hz → 110 Hz.

Perfect fifth = 3 : 2. Going up a fifth multiplies by 3/2 = 1.5: 220 Hz × 1.5 = 330 Hz. Going down a fifth multiplies by 2/3.

Worked Example 3: String length and pitch (inverse proportion)

On the same string, a shorter vibrating length gives a higher note. Half the length = double the frequency. When one quantity doubles while the other halves, they are inversely proportional: frequency × length stays the same.

A 50 cm string plays 240 Hz, so frequency × length = 240 × 50 = 12 000. For a 300 Hz note: length = 12 000 ÷ 300 = 40 cm. Check: 300 × 40 = 12 000 ✔

Worked Example 4: The speed of sound

In air at room temperature, sound travels about 343 m/s. Distance = speed × time, so in 4 s sound travels 343 × 4 = 1372 m.

Thunder: you see lightning almost instantly, but the thunder takes time to arrive. Count the seconds and multiply by 343 to find how far away the storm is.

Echo: an echo travels there and back, so divide the total distance by 2.

Wavelength (the length of one sound wave) = speed ÷ frequency. A 343 Hz note has a wavelength of 343 ÷ 343 = 1 m.

Worked Example 5: Decibels and protecting your ears

Loudness is measured in decibels (dB). The scale grows by powers of 10: every +10 dB means the sound carries 10 times as much energy. So +20 dB = 10 × 10 = 100 times, and +30 dB = 10³ = 1000 times.

Hearing experts use a safe-listening rule: 8 hours is the limit at 85 dB, and every +3 dB halves the safe time. At 88 dB: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 hours. (Doctors care about this because the tiny hair cells in your ears do not grow back!)

Worked Example 6: Note values, time signatures and tempo

Note lengths are fractions of a whole note: half note = 1/2, quarter note = 1/4, eighth note = 1/8. A dot adds half the note's value: a dotted half = 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4.

The time signature 4/4 means each bar holds 4 quarter notes (one whole note). In 6/8, each bar holds 6 eighth notes.

Tempo is measured in BPM (beats per minute). At 60 BPM each beat lasts 1 s; at 120 BPM each beat lasts 60 ÷ 120 = 0.5 s.
