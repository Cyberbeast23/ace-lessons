Good Vibrations: How Sound Becomes Music

Close your eyes and listen. Perhaps you can hear a car passing, a bird outside, or the hum of a fridge. Every one of those sounds began the same way: something, somewhere, was shaking. Sound is a vibration, and music is what happens when people learn to control vibrations on purpose. For tens of thousands of years, in every culture on Earth, humans have found ways to turn shaking air into songs.

When you pluck a guitar string, it swings back and forth hundreds of times every second. Each swing pushes on the air molecules next to it, squeezing them together, and then pulls away, spreading them apart. Those molecules bump into their neighbours, which bump into theirs, sending a wave of squeezes and stretches rippling outward at about 343 metres per second. The air itself does not travel across the room; only the pattern does, the way a wave moves through a crowd at a hockey game while each fan stays in their seat. When the wave reaches you, it enters your ear canal and makes your eardrum tremble. Three tiny bones, the hammer, the anvil, and the stirrup, pass the vibration along and make it stronger. The stirrup is the smallest bone in your body, about the size of a grain of rice. It pushes on the cochlea, a snail-shaped tube filled with fluid and lined with about 15 000 microscopic hair cells. Different hair cells respond to different pitches, and they turn the motion into electrical signals that race along a nerve to your brain. Only then do you actually perceive a sound. The whole journey takes a tiny fraction of a second.

Two features make one sound distinct from another. The first is pitch: fast vibrations make high notes, and slow vibrations make low ones. That is why a short, tight string squeaks while a long, loose one rumbles, and why a tiny piccolo sings far above a giant tuba. The second is loudness: bigger vibrations push the air harder and carry more energy. Hair cells are fragile, though. Very loud sounds can damage them, and once they are destroyed, they never grow back. Many people who spent years beside roaring machines or booming speakers without ear protection now struggle to hear quiet voices.

People discovered how to make music long before they understood any of this science. Some of the oldest instruments ever found are flutes carved from bird bones and mammoth ivory in caves in Germany. They are more than 35 000 years old. In Jiahu, in central China, archaeologists dug up flutes made from the wing bones of cranes about 9000 years ago, and one was still in good enough condition to play a tune. Across the world, cultures shaped music to fit their own lives and languages. In West Africa, the Yoruba talking drum has cords along its sides. When a drummer squeezes the drum under one arm, the cords tighten the drumhead and the pitch rises, so skilled players can copy the rising and falling tones of spoken Yoruba and "speak" through the drum. For many First Nations peoples in Canada, the drum holds deep meaning and is often described as the heartbeat of the community.

In China, the guzheng, a long plucked instrument usually strung with 21 strings, has been played for more than 2000 years. Each string rests on a movable bridge, and a player can press down on the far side of the bridge to bend a note up and down like a voice. The erhu has only two strings, yet its sound is so expressive that people say it can weep. Its bow is trapped between the strings, and its small sound box is covered with snakeskin, which vibrates like the skin of a drum. In Europe, orchestras grew from small groups of musicians into huge ensembles of string, woodwind, brass, and percussion instruments. By the 1800s, a composer could write a symphony for nearly a hundred players. Each section has a role, and the conductor keeps them all together so that the music can sustain a single mood or change it in an instant.

Today, scientists who study acoustics design concert halls so that every seat hears clearly, and doctors called audiologists test hearing and protect it. Yet the most profound discovery about music may be the simplest one. A bone flute, a guzheng, a talking drum, and a full orchestra look nothing alike, but they all do the same intricate job: they shake the air in patterns that human brains find beautiful. Perhaps that is why music is found in every culture that has ever existed.
