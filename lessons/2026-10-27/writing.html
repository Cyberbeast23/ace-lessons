✍️ Writing Week 30: Explanatory Directions with a Map: Guide a Visitor to a Secret Spot 🗺️🧭

Yesterday you wrote like a journalist. Today, Ace, you'll write like a guide. Hiking guides, park brochures and treasure hunts all use explanatory directions: writing that leads a reader who has never been there from a starting point to a destination, step by step, so clearly that they can't get lost. The best directions work together with a map, using compass directions, distances, landmarks and transition words. You'll draw a simple map and write directions to a "secret spot" near your home, school or a park you know (just don't include your real address!).

Part 1: The Lesson

Explanatory directions have six building blocks 🧩

Good directions read like a friendly guide walking beside you. They are carefully ordered, so the reader always knows where they are and what to do next.

1. Title: tells the reader exactly where the directions go, like "How to Find the Hidden Waterfall"
2. Introduction: names the starting point and the destination, and explains why the place is worth finding
3. Map + key: tells the reader how to use your map: the compass rose, the scale, and what each symbol means
4. Step-by-step directions: numbered steps with a compass direction (or left/right), a distance, and a landmark in each step
5. Checkpoint or warning: a way for the reader to check they are on track ("If you reach the bridge, you've gone too far")
6. Conclusion: tells the reader how they will know they have arrived, plus a final tip

Five guide's moves

  • Start every step with a command verb: Walk, Turn, Follow, Cross, Look for. These are called imperative sentences.
  • Give direction + distance + landmark: "Walk north for about 200 metres until you reach the red mailbox." All three together make a step impossible to misread.
  • Use sequence transitions: first, next, after that, when you reach, as soon as, finally.
  • Use precise position words: across from, beside, beyond, at the fork, on your left, straight ahead.
  • Think like a stranger: your reader has never been there. Don't say "the usual spot" or "that tree." Say which tree, and how to recognize it.

Capitalizing directions: use a lowercase letter when a direction tells which way to go (walk north, the east side of the park). Use a capital letter when it is part of a name (North Vancouver, the North Pole, the Northern Hemisphere).

Model Explanatory Directions (short version)

Title + introductionMap + keySteps + checkpointConclusion

How to Find the Secret Fort in Maple Grove Park
Starting at the front doors of the library, these directions will lead you to a hidden fort made of fallen branches, the best hide-and-seek spot in the whole park.
On my map, north is at the top, 1 cm stands for 50 m, the green circles are trees, and the red X marks the fort.
1. First, walk north along the gravel path for about 150 metres until you reach the playground.
2. Next, turn right (east) at the water fountain and follow the fence for 100 metres.
3. When you see a giant cedar with a split trunk, leave the path and walk 20 steps toward the creek. If you reach the wooden bridge, you've gone too far, so turn back!
Finally, look under the low branches on your left. When you spot a doorway of crossed sticks, you've arrived. Please leave the fort the way you found it!

Notice: the title names the destination; the introduction gives the start, the end and a reason to go; the map key explains direction, scale and symbols; every step starts with a command verb and has a direction, a distance and a landmark; the checkpoint warns the reader if they go too far; and the conclusion tells them how they'll know they've arrived. (Maple Grove Park is an invented park for this model.)

Part 4: Your Turn to Write

Step 1: Choose your route

Pick one option. Draw a quick map on paper first: add a compass rose (N, E, S, W), a scale, and a key with at least three symbols.

Plan your directions: jot a few words for each box. (These notes save with your writing.)

Step 2: Write your directions

Goal: 250 to 350 words with all six building blocks. Include at least five numbered steps, each starting with a command verb; at least four compass directions (north, south, east, west, or a bearing like 090°); at least three distances in metric units (metres, kilometres, or steps); at least four landmarks; at least four different transition words; and at least one checkpoint or warning. Bonus: hand your map and directions to someone and see if they can follow them without asking you a single question!
