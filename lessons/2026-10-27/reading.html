Where in the World? How Navigators Find Their Place

Imagine you are the captain of a wooden sailing ship in the year 1750. For weeks, all you have seen is grey ocean in every direction. There are no road signs out here, no coastline, and no phone to show a blue dot on a screen. Somewhere ahead are dangerous rocks, and your crew is running low on fresh water. To survive, you must answer one question correctly: Where in the world are we? For thousands of years, people who wanted to navigate across oceans, deserts and frozen lands have needed clever ways to answer it.

The first part of the answer is latitude, which measures how far north or south of the equator you are. Latitude was the easier half of the puzzle, because the sky gives it away. In the Northern Hemisphere, the North Star, Polaris, sits almost exactly above the North Pole. The higher Polaris appears above the horizon, the farther north you are. A sailor who measured Polaris at 49 degrees above the horizon could determine that the ship was at about 49 degrees north, the same latitude as Vancouver. During the day, sailors measured the angle of the sun at noon and used tables to work out the same thing.

The second part, longitude, tells you how far east or west you are, and it was a much harder problem. The stars can't help much, because the whole sky turns as Earth spins. The clue turned out to be time. Earth turns 15 degrees every hour, so if a sailor knew the exact time at a reference point, such as Greenwich in England, and compared it with local noon on the ship, the difference would reveal the longitude. If noon on the ship came eight hours after noon in Greenwich, the ship was 120 degrees west. The trouble was that in the 1700s, ordinary clocks used swinging pendulums, and on a rolling ship they sped up, slowed down or stopped. A clock that was off by just a few minutes could put a ship many kilometres away from where the captain thought it was.

The British government offered a huge prize to anyone who could solve the longitude problem. A carpenter and clockmaker named John Harrison spent most of his life on the challenge. His sea clock, called a chronometer, used springs and clever balances instead of a pendulum, so it kept precise time even in storms and heat. When Captain James Cook sailed to the Pacific Northwest coast in 1778, he carried a copy of a Harrison-style chronometer, and his charts of the coastline were remarkably accurate for the time.

Knowing your position is only useful if you have a good map, and mapmakers, called cartographers, face a puzzle of their own. Earth is round, but paper is flat. Try flattening an orange peel and it tears and stretches. In the same way, every flat map must distort something: shapes, sizes, distances or directions. A famous map called the Mercator projection keeps directions correct, which is why sailors loved it, but it stretches lands near the poles. On it, Greenland looks about as big as Africa, even though Africa is actually about fourteen times larger. No flat map is perfect; each one is designed for a different job.

Of course, Europeans were not the only skilled navigators. Long before compasses, Polynesian wayfinders crossed thousands of kilometres of the Pacific by reading star paths, ocean swells and the flight of birds. Inuit travellers in the Arctic learned to orient themselves using wind-carved snowdrifts, the shape of the coastline and the position of the sun, and some carved wooden maps of the coast that could be felt with mittened hands in the dark.

Today, a phone finds its position using the Global Positioning System, or GPS. About thirty satellites rotate around Earth, each one constantly sending out a signal with the exact time it was sent. Your phone measures how long each signal took to arrive and calculates how far away each satellite is. With distances to at least four satellites, the phone can pinpoint the only spot on Earth that fits them all. The result is usually approximate, often within a few metres, but that is close enough to guide a driver to the right street. Behind that tiny blue dot are the same ideas sailors used centuries ago: angles, distances, and very, very accurate clocks.
