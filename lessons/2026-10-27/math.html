🧭 Math Week 30: Navigator's Math Lab

Ace, before phones could tell us where we are, sailors, explorers and pilots had to work it out with maps, compasses, clocks and math. Even today, a map is useless if you can't read its scale, and a GPS only works because computers do lightning-fast distance math. Today you'll work like a navigator with map scale ratios, converting centimetres to kilometres, reading latitude and longitude on a grid, degrees of latitude to kilometres, longitude and time zones (15° per hour), compass bearings and back bearings, turning angles, speed-distance-time, real area from a grid map, signal speed, contour lines, average speed for a whole trip, and an equation.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your navigator's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (cm with cm, km with km, minutes with minutes)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Map scale ratios

A scale of 1 : 100 000 means 1 cm on the map is 100 000 cm in real life. Since 100 cm = 1 m and 1000 m = 1 km, there are 100 000 cm in 1 km. So 1 cm on the map = 1 km, and 3.5 cm on the map = 3.5 km.

Shortcut: to change cm into km, divide by 100 000 (that's ÷ 100, then ÷ 1000).

Worked Example 2: Latitude and longitude

Latitude lines run east-west and tell you how far north or south of the equator you are (0° to 90°). Longitude lines (meridians) run north-south and tell you how far east or west of the Prime Meridian in Greenwich, England you are (0° to 180°). Always say latitude first: Vancouver is about 49°N, 123°W.

Every 1° of latitude is about 111 km north or south. So 2° of latitude ≈ 2 × 111 = 222 km.

Worked Example 3: Longitude and time

Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so it turns 360 ÷ 24 = 15° every hour. Two places 30° of longitude apart have sun-times 30 ÷ 15 = 2 hours apart. Places farther east see the sun first, so their clocks are ahead.

Going the other way: a 3-hour difference means 3 × 15 = 45° of longitude.

Worked Example 4: Compass bearings

A bearing is an angle measured clockwise from north, written with three digits. North = 000°, east = 090°, south = 180°, west = 270°.

To go straight back the way you came, use the back bearing: add 180° (or subtract 180° if the bearing is already 180° or more). The back bearing of 040° is 040 + 180 = 220°.

A clockwise turn from 330° to 020° passes through north: 360 − 330 = 30°, then 30 + 20 = 50°.

Worked Example 5: Speed, distance and time

time = distance ÷ speed. A boat goes 30 km at 20 km/h: 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 h = 90 minutes.

Average speed for a whole trip = total distance ÷ total time. Don't just average the two speeds!

Worked Example 6: Real area from a grid map

If each grid square is 2 km wide, a rectangle 3 squares by 4 squares is really 6 km by 8 km, so its area is 6 × 8 = 48 km². Change the side lengths to real units first, then multiply.

Worked Example 7: Equations

20 + 3h = 35. Undo each step in reverse: subtract 20 (3h = 15), then divide by 3 (h = 5).
