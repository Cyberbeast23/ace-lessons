✍️ Writing Week 60: Choose-Your-Path Adventure — Into the Hidden Kingdom 🍄🧭

Yesterday you wrote a petition to change something real. Today, Ace, you get to write a story where the reader is the hero and makes the decisions: a choose-your-path adventure (also called a branching story or interactive fiction). Instead of one path from beginning to end, the story splits like the threads of a mycelium. At each decision point, the reader picks a choice and jumps to a different section, so one story can have three, four or even ten endings! Video game designers plan stories exactly like this. Today's genre is the choose-your-path adventure.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six building blocks of a branching story 🧭

Before you write a single sentence, branching-story writers draw a story map: boxes for sections and arrows for choices.

1. Opening→Choice A → 2/Choice B → 3→2: Ending ⭐/3: Choice C → 4 · Choice D → 5→Endings 4 & 5
1. Opening hook: drop the reader into the action using "you" and the present tense: "You kneel beside a rotting log..."
2. Decision point: end a section with a real dilemma, where both choices are tempting: "Do you climb toward the light, or follow the stream?"
3. Navigation lines: clear instructions so the reader never gets lost: "→ To climb, go to Section 3."
4. Consequences: each choice must change what happens. If both paths lead to the same place, the choice was fake!
5. Science woven in: real facts become part of the plot, such as a spore cloud, a glowing fungus or mycelium linking tree roots.
6. Several endings: at least one triumph, one setback and one surprise. Each ending should feel earned by the choices that led to it.

Five habits of great branching-story writers

  • Map first, write second: number your sections before you write, so every arrow points somewhere real.
  • Make choices matter: ❌ "Do you go left or right?" (a coin toss) ✅ "Do you trust the map, or the old ranger's warning?" (a real decision with clues)
  • Plant clues: careful readers should be able to predict which choice is wiser, just like you did in today's reading.
  • Keep each section short: 50 to 120 words, then a choice or an ending. Momentum matters!
  • Be accurate: fiction can be wild, but the science facts must be true. (And no ending should ever reward eating a wild mushroom!)

Branching-story rule: before you finish, follow every path from start to end, the way a game tester would. Does each one make sense?

Mini-lesson: grammar for second-person adventure stories 🖋️

Stay in the present tense: don't slip into the past. ❌ You crawl under the log and saw a glowing mushroom. ✅ You crawl under the log and see a glowing mushroom.

Keep the point of view: the hero is always you. ❌ You grab the flashlight, and I run to the cave. ✅ You grab the flashlight and run to the cave.

Punctuate dialogue: the comma or end mark goes inside the quotation marks. ✅ "Don't touch it," Dr. Lee warns. ✅ "Is it poisonous?" you ask.

Comma after an introductory word, phrase or clause: Suddenly, the ground shakes. · After the rain, mushrooms appear. · When the light fades, you hear a crack.

Canadian spelling: mould, colour, grey, metre, centre, favourite. The grey mould was the colour of ash, one metre from the trail.

Model branching story: "The Glowing Log" (about 330 words, 5 sections)

Opening hook + decision pointsScience woven inConsequences + navigationEndings

SECTION 1
It is almost dark when you realize the trail has disappeared. Your flashlight flickers and dies. Then, beside a rotting log, you notice something impossible: a soft green glow. The log is covered in tiny mushrooms that make their own light. Some fungi really can glow; scientists call it bioluminescence. Far away, you hear a stream. Do you stay by the glowing log, or head toward the sound of water?
→ To stay by the log, go to Section 2.
→ To follow the stream, go to Section 3.

SECTION 2
You sit beside the log and wait. You remember that mycelium threads may run through the soil under your feet, connecting the trees around you like a hidden web. Somehow, that makes you feel less alone. An hour later, you see bobbing headlamps.
"There you are!" calls the ranger. Staying put is exactly the right choice. THE END ⭐

SECTION 3
You stumble downhill toward the stream. Suddenly, your boot slips on a slimy patch, and you slide into a hollow full of fat, white mushrooms. A puff of brown dust explodes into the air: millions of spores! You're hungry, and the mushrooms look like the ones Dad buys at the store. Do you take a bite, or keep following the stream?
→ To eat a mushroom, go to Section 4.
→ To keep going, go to Section 5.

SECTION 4
You lift the mushroom toward your mouth, then remember: some deadly mushrooms look almost exactly like safe ones. You drop it. Phew! Too close. Go back to Section 3 and choose again. TRY AGAIN 🔁

SECTION 5
You follow the water to a road, where a car's headlights sweep across you. You're safe, muddy and full of stories. THE END 🏁

Notice: the opening drops the reader into the action with you and the present tense, and ends with a real dilemma. Every navigation line points to a real section. The science (bioluminescence, mycelium, spores, look-alike mushrooms) is part of the plot, not a lecture. Section 4 is a setback that teaches a real safety rule, and look for the commas after Suddenly and An hour later.
