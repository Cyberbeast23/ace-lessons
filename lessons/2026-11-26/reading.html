Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you were an ad detective. Today's skill is one that strong readers use without even noticing: making, confirming and revising predictions. A prediction isn't a wild guess. It's a smart bet about what comes next, based on clues. While you read, keep a running "bet" in your head and check it against the text. Use the P-C-R cycle:
  • P = Predict: Before a section, use clues to make a bet. Good clues are headings, questions the author asks, what you already know and signal words like but, however, surprisingly and at first (these warn you that something is about to change).
  • C = Confirm: As you read, find the sentence that proves your bet was right. Say to yourself, "I predicted ___, and the text says ___."
  • R = Revise: If the text surprises you, that's not failure! Change your prediction and say, "I thought ___, but now I think ___ because ___." Scientists do exactly this when an experiment surprises them.
  • Stop and predict: Today's passage has three purple 🔮 boxes. When you reach one, stop, make a prediction in your head (or say it out loud) before you read on. Then see how you did.

The Hidden Kingdom: The Secret Life of Fungi

After an autumn rain in a BC forest, something strange happens overnight. Mushrooms appear where there were none the day before: orange ones on stumps, white ones in the moss, brown ones lined up along a fallen log. It's tempting to think a mushroom is a whole living thing, like a flower. But a mushroom is really just the fruit of something much bigger. Picking a mushroom is a lot like picking an apple. The "tree" that grew it is still there, hidden from view.

🔮 Stop and predict #1: If the mushroom is only the fruit, where do you think the rest of the fungus is, and what might it look like? Make your bet, then read on.

The threads beneath your feet. The main body of a fungus is a tangle of threads called mycelium, spreading through soil, leaves and rotting wood. Each thread, or hypha, is thinner than a human hair, so a single one is almost invisible. Together, though, they can be enormous. Because mycelium is so inconspicuous, people walk over giant fungi every day without noticing. In Oregon, scientists testing samples of tree-killing honey mushroom kept getting the same surprising result: samples taken kilometres apart had identical DNA. They were all one fungus, spreading through about 965 hectares and possibly thousands of years old. Fungi spread in a second way, too. A mushroom releases millions of microscopic spores that drift on the wind. A spore can stay dormant for months or even years, waiting until the moisture and temperature are just right before it sprouts a new hypha.

Nature's recyclers. You might expect fungi to eat the way animals do, but they have no mouths and no stomachs. Instead, a fungus digests its food outside its body. Its hyphae push into a log and release chemicals called enzymes that break the wood into simple, dissolved nutrients, which the hyphae then soak up. Wood is tough to break down because it contains lignin, a material that makes tree trunks stiff. Very few living things can digest lignin, but some fungi can. Without them, fallen trees would pile up for centuries, and the nutrients locked inside would never return to the soil to feed new plants.

🔮 Stop and predict #2: The next heading is "The mould that saved millions." Use the heading and what you already know about mould. How could a mould possibly save lives?

The mould that saved millions. In September 1928, a Scottish scientist named Alexander Fleming came back to his London laboratory after a holiday. He had left dishes of bacteria on his bench, and one had grown a fuzzy patch of blue-green mould. Most people would have thrown the spoiled dish away. However, Fleming noticed something odd: all around the mould, the bacteria had died. The mould, called Penicillium, was making a substance that killed bacteria. Fleming named it penicillin. Yet for more than ten years, penicillin was not a medicine. It was hard to make, and it fell apart easily. Finally, around 1940, a team at Oxford University led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain found a way to purify it. In 1941, they treated their first patient, a police officer with a terrible infection. He began to recover, but the tiny supply ran out, and he died. The team needed far more. Factories in the United States learned to grow the mould in huge tanks, where it would proliferate quickly, and by the end of the Second World War, penicillin was saving the lives of soldiers and other patients. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945.

🔮 Stop and predict #3: In the 1990s, scientists in British Columbia asked: do the trees in a forest only compete for sunlight, or might they help each other? Predict what they found, and how a fungus might be involved.

Trees that share? Most trees have fungal partners. Mycorrhizal fungi wrap around and even into tree roots. The fungus gathers water and minerals, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, from far through the soil and passes them to the tree. In return, the tree gives the fungus sugar it makes by photosynthesis. Because each side gives something and gets something, scientists call this a reciprocal partnership. Then forest scientist Suzanne Simard, who later became a professor at the University of British Columbia, tested a bold idea. She covered young birch and Douglas fir trees with plastic bags and fed each kind of tree a different form of carbon gas that she could trace. Later, using a Geiger counter and other tests, she found carbon from the birch inside the fir, and carbon from the fir inside the birch. Sugar had travelled between different trees through the fungal threads, and fir seedlings growing in deep shade seemed to receive more. Her team later mapped a forest patch where one big, old tree was linked to dozens of others. Newspapers called the network the "wood wide web."

Not so fast. At first, the story spread around the world: trees talk, share and care for their young! Surprisingly, some other forest scientists are skeptical. They agree that fungi link many roots, but they argue that the evidence that trees deliberately "share" is not yet conclusive. In some experiments, only small amounts of carbon moved, and the fungus might move sugar for its own benefit rather than to help a seedling. Scientists are now designing new experiments to find out. That's not a weakness of science. It is exactly how science is supposed to work.

A warning from mycologists. The next time you see a mushroom, remember the giant hidden under it. But look with your eyes, not your mouth. Some deadly mushrooms look almost exactly like delicious ones, and poisonous death cap mushrooms have even been found growing under city trees in BC. Never eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has identified it.
