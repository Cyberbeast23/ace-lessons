📖 Reading Week 57: Land Between the Rivers: How Mesopotamia Gave the World Writing

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you combined two sources. Today you'll read one informational article, then two short "voices from the clay": real messages written by people in ancient Mesopotamia almost 4 000 years ago, retold in modern English. Today's skill is one that editors, historians and advertisers think about all the time: author's purpose and tone. Every writer has a reason for writing and an attitude toward the topic, and the words they choose give them away. As you read, ask:
  • 1. Purpose: why did the author write this? To inform (teach facts), to persuade (convince you to think or do something), to entertain (tell a story or amuse), or to express feelings. Many texts have a main purpose and a second one.
  • 2. Tone: what is the author's attitude? Tone words describe it: admiring, neutral, angry, humorous, worried, sarcastic, proud, regretful.
  • 3. Connotation: what feelings do the words carry? Words can mean almost the same thing but feel different. A house can be cozy (positive), small (neutral) or cramped (negative). Writers pick words with the connotation that fits their purpose.
  • 4. Evidence for your answer: always point to the exact words that show the purpose or tone. "It sounds angry" is a guess; "the writer says How dare you" is evidence.
  • Word detective: Greek meso- = "middle" + potamos = "river," so Mesopotamia = "the land between the rivers" (and a hippopotamus is a "river horse"!) · Latin cuneus = "wedge," so cuneiform = "wedge-shaped" · Latin scribere = "to write" (scribe, script, describe, inscription) · Latin civis = "citizen" (civilization, civic, city)
  • Words in yellow are vocabulary words. Use the sentence around them (the context) to figure out what they mean.

Text 1: Land Between the Rivers: How Mesopotamia Gave the World Writing

Picture a hot, flat land with almost no rain, no forests, no stone for building and very little metal. It doesn't sound like the place where the world would change forever. Yet between two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, in what is now mostly Iraq, people built some of the world's first cities, invented one of its first writing systems, and made discoveries that still shape your day, right down to the 60 minutes in every hour.

A gift from the rivers. Every spring, melting snow in the mountains to the north made the rivers flood. When the water went down, it left behind a layer of silt, which made the soil extremely fertile. But the floods were unpredictable, and the long summers were scorching and dry. To grow barley and wheat all year, farmers learned to irrigate their fields, digging long canals to carry river water exactly where it was needed. Digging and repairing canals took the work of hundreds of people, so villages had to cooperate. Over many generations, some villages grew into cities. By about 3000 BCE, the Sumerian city of Uruk may have had 40 000 people or more, which would have made it one of the largest cities on Earth at the time.

Why writing was invented. A city that size has a problem: how do you keep track of everything? Temples collected and stored huge amounts of barley, oil, wool and livestock, and paid workers in food. At first, officials used small clay tokens to stand for goods. Around 3200 BCE, they began pressing simple pictures and number marks into soft clay tablets. Over hundreds of years, the pictures became groups of wedge-shaped marks, made by pressing the cut end of a reed into the clay. We call this writing cuneiform. The surprising truth is that writing was not invented to record poems or stories. It was invented to keep accounts. Most of the earliest tablets are receipts and ration lists.

The scribes. Learning cuneiform was hard. It used hundreds of different signs, so only a small number of people, called scribes, could read and write. Boys (and a few girls) from wealthier families went to a school called the edubba, or "tablet house," where they copied signs over and over for years. Because writing was such a rare and powerful skill, a scribe could become an important official, working for a temple, a palace or a merchant.

Laws in stone. Around 1754 BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had 282 laws carved onto a tall black stone pillar for everyone to see. Many follow an "if…then" pattern: if a person does something, then this is the punishment. Some of the punishments seem very harsh to us today, and the code did not treat everyone equally: the penalty often depended on whether you were wealthy, an ordinary free person, or enslaved. Still, the idea that laws should be written down, so that a judge cannot simply make them up, was an important step.

Why we know so much. Paper, papyrus and parchment rot away over time, but clay is surprisingly durable, especially when it has been baked. When enemies burned the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal in Nineveh around 612 BCE, the fire actually baked many of its tablets hard, preserving them for us. Archaeologists have dug up hundreds of thousands of cuneiform tablets. For centuries, no one could read them. Then, in the 1800s, scholars worked out how to decipher cuneiform, partly by comparing the same message carved in three different languages on a cliff in Iran. Suddenly, the voices of ancient Mesopotamians could speak again.

Text 2: Voices from the Clay

These are short retellings, in modern English, of two real ancient texts. The originals were written in cuneiform almost 4 000 years ago.

A. An angry customer (from a tablet found in the city of Ur, about 1750 BCE)
Tell Ea-nasir: this is what Nanni says. When you came, you promised me, "I will give you good copper." Then you left, and you did not do what you promised. You offered my messenger poor-quality copper and said, "Take it or leave it!" Who do you think I am, that you treat me with such contempt? I am indignant! I sent you a full purse of silver. Now I will not accept any copper from you that is not good. I will choose the copper myself, piece by piece. And because you have treated me so badly, I will make you pay for it.

B. A schoolboy's day (from a story copied by many student scribes)
I woke early, and my mother gave me two rolls for lunch. At the tablet house, I recited my tablet and copied a new one. But my handwriting was messy, the monitor said I talked without permission, and I stood up when I should have stayed seated. Each time, I was punished. That evening, I begged my father to invite my teacher home. Father welcomed him, gave him the best seat, served him a fine dinner, and gave him a new robe and a ring. The teacher smiled and said, "Young man, because you did not neglect my words, may you reach the top of the scribal art!"

Part 2: Think Deeper (not scored)

Change the tone! Rewrite two or three sentences of Nanni's angry message so the purpose stays the same (Nanni still wants good copper or the money back) but the tone becomes polite and businesslike. Then explain in one sentence which words you changed and how their connotation is different.

Model answer: "Dear Ea-nasir, when we met, you agreed to sell me good-quality copper. Unfortunately, the copper offered to my messenger did not match that agreement. Since I have already paid you in silver, I would appreciate it if you would send copper of the quality we agreed on, or return my payment." I changed "Who do you think I am?" and "contempt" to "unfortunately" and "did not match that agreement," because those words describe the same problem without insulting him. The purpose (get good copper or a refund) is the same, but the polite words make Ea-nasir more likely to cooperate.
