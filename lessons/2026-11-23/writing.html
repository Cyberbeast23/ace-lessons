✍️ Writing Week 57: Code of Laws with Reasons — Write a Fair Law Code ⚖️📜

Yesterday you wrote a cover letter to convince an employer. Today, Ace, you'll write something that people have argued about for 4 000 years: laws. When King Hammurabi carved his laws in stone, he wanted everyone to know the rules ahead of time. But many of his laws were harsh, and they didn't treat everyone equally. Today you'll do better. You'll write a code of laws with reasons: a short, numbered set of rules for a community, where every rule is clear enough that nobody can argue about what it means, every consequence is fair, and every law comes with a reason that explains why it makes the community better. Today's genre is the code of laws (a charter of rules with reasons).

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong code of laws ⚖️

A good code is short and organized: about 6 to 10 numbered laws, grouped under headings.

1. Title and purpose statement: the name of the code and one or two sentences about who it is for and why it exists ("So that every member is safe and treated fairly…")
2. Definitions: explain any word that could be misunderstood ("A member means anyone who has signed the club list.")
3. Clear rule: one rule per law, using precise words like must, must not and may
4. If-then consequence: what happens if the rule is broken, and the consequence should fit the problem ("If a member breaks a tool, then they must help repair or replace it.")
5. Reason: one sentence that explains why the law is fair or why it helps the community ("Reason: …")
6. Fairness and changes: a final law that says the rules apply to everyone equally, and how the code can be changed (a vote, a meeting)

Five habits of great law writers

  • Be precise: ❌ "Don't be loud." (How loud is loud?) ✅ "Members must use indoor voices in the reading corner." A law that people can argue about is a weak law.
  • Choose modal verbs carefully: must = required; must not = forbidden; may = allowed, but not required; should = a strong suggestion. These words are not interchangeable!
  • Make the consequence fit: a consequence should help fix the problem, not just hurt someone. Hammurabi's code often did the opposite.
  • Treat everyone equally: the same law and the same consequence apply to the leader and the newest member.
  • Give a reason for every law: people follow rules more willingly when they understand why. If you can't think of a good reason, maybe the law isn't needed.

Law writer's rule: read each law and ask, "Could someone find a sneaky loophole?" If yes, make the wording more precise.

Mini-lesson: grammar for laws and rules 📏

If-then sentences and commas: when the if part comes first, put a comma after it: If a member borrows a book, they must return it within one week. When the if part comes second, you usually don't need a comma: A member must return a book within one week if they borrow it.

Modal verbs: must (required), must not (forbidden), may (allowed), should (suggested). "Members may bring snacks" means snacks are allowed, not required!

Subject-verb agreement with each, every and everyone: these words are singular: Each member has one vote. Everyone is equal under this code.

Numbered lists and parallel structure: start each law the same way when you can (Members must… / Members must not… / Members may…).

Canadian spelling: behaviour, honour, neighbour, offence, defence, centre. In Canada, a fair code protects your honour and punishes an offence!

Model code of laws: "The Canal Keepers' Code" (about 230 words)

Title, purpose + definitionsClear rulesIf-then consequences + reasonsFairness and changes

The Canal Keepers' Code of the Village of Two Rivers
Purpose: This code exists so that every family in Two Rivers gets a fair share of water and no field goes dry.
Definition: A canal keeper means any adult who farms land beside the village canal.

Law 1. Each canal keeper must clear the mud from their part of the canal before the spring flood.
Law 2. Canal keepers must not open their gates for more than one day in a row during a dry month.
Law 3. Any family may ask the village council for extra water if a child or animal is sick.

Law 4. If a canal keeper leaves their gate open and floods a neighbour's field, then they must help that neighbour replant the damaged crops.
Reason: A consequence should repair the harm, so the neighbour does not lose a whole harvest.
Law 5. If a canal keeper does not clear their mud, then the others will clear it, and the keeper must give them each one day of work in return.
Reason: Everyone depends on the same canal, so everyone must share the work.

Law 6. This code applies equally to every family, including the families of the council.
Law 7. This code may be changed only if more than half of the canal keepers vote for the change at the harvest meeting.

Notice: each law has one rule and uses a precise modal verb (must, must not, may). Law 2 is precise ("more than one day in a row") so no one can find a loophole. The consequences in Laws 4 and 5 repair the harm instead of just punishing, and each comes with a Reason. Law 6 makes the code equal for everyone, which Hammurabi's code was not. Look for the comma after each if clause, the singular verb in Each canal keeper must, and the Canadian spelling neighbour.
