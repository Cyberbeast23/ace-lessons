Canada Reaches for the Stars

Look up on a clear night and you are staring into a neighbourhood far bigger than any map. Our Solar System has one star, the Sun, and eight planets that travel around it. Each planet follows a curved path called an orbit. What keeps the planets from flying off into the dark? The answer is gravity, the invisible pull that every object has on every other object. The Sun is so massive that its gravity holds all eight planets, plus moons, comets, and millions of rocky asteroids, in a giant spinning system.

The four planets closest to the Sun, Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, are small and rocky. The four farther out, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, are giants made mostly of gas and ice. Earth is special for one big reason: it is the only planet we know of that has life. Part of the secret is our atmosphere, the blanket of air that wraps around the planet. It gives us oxygen to breathe, keeps temperatures from swinging wildly between day and night, and burns up most small space rocks before they reach the ground. Mars has only a thin atmosphere, so it is freezing and dusty. Venus has the opposite problem: its thick atmosphere traps so much heat that it is the hottest planet of all, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun.

Canada has played a surprisingly large part in exploring space. In 1962 Canada launched a satellite called Alouette 1, becoming the third country to design and build its own spacecraft. Alouette studied the upper atmosphere, which helped improve radio communication across our huge, northern country. Today, satellites help Canadians forecast storms, track wildfires, guide ships through Arctic ice, and connect phone calls to remote communities.

Canada's most famous space invention is a robotic arm. The first Canadarm flew on a NASA space shuttle in 1981. It could grab satellites, move heavy equipment, and help astronauts work outside the spacecraft. Its bigger successor, Canadarm2, has worked on the International Space Station since 2001. Engineers on the ground can control it, and it has helped catch cargo ships arriving with food, water, and science experiments. When Canadian engineers solve a problem, the whole world often benefits.

Canadian astronauts have also shown that space science and medicine are closely linked. Dr. Roberta Bondar, a neurologist (a doctor who studies the brain and nerves), became the first Canadian woman in space in 1992. Dr. David Saint-Jacques, a family physician as well as an engineer, lived on the space station for more than six months. In microgravity, where everything floats, the human body changes. Without gravity pulling on them, bones lose calcium and muscles weaken, much like a patient who stays in bed for weeks. That is why astronauts exercise about two hours every day. Doctors study these changes closely, and what they learn helps older people on Earth who are fighting bone loss.

Exploring space is never just about rockets. It takes mathematicians who calculate orbits, engineers who build arms and satellites, and doctors who keep the crew healthy. Most of all, it takes curious people who ask questions and test their ideas carefully. Ace, the next time you see the Moon rising, remember that a few Canadians once looked up the same way and wondered, "Could I go there?" Then they did the hard work to find out.
