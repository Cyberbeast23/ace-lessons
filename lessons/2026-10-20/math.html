🏯 Math Week 23: Ancient China & Its Great Inventions Lab

Ace, more than 2000 years ago, Chinese emperors, engineers, and inventors gave the world the Great Wall, paper, printing, the magnetic compass, and silk that travelled thousands of kilometres along the Silk Road. Historians, archaeologists, and navigators all use math to understand that world. Today you'll think like all three with BCE/CE timelines (including the tricky jump across the year 1), map scales and scale models, area and perimeter of the Terracotta Army pits, hectares, estimating with density, unit rates, percent increase and tax, compass bearings, and volume.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your historian's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: BCE and CE timelines

CE (Common Era) years count up from the year 1. BCE (Before the Common Era) years count backwards, so 300 BCE is earlier than 250 BCE. Think of BCE years like negative numbers on a number line.

Both BCE: subtract. From 300 BCE to 250 BCE is 300 − 250 = 50 years.

Across the middle: there was no year 0! The year 1 BCE was followed straight away by 1 CE. So add the two numbers, then subtract 1. From 50 BCE to 50 CE is 50 + 50 − 1 = 99 years.

Worked Example 2: Map scales and scale models

On a map with the scale 1 cm : 200 km, every centimetre stands for 200 km. A road 700 km long is drawn 700 ÷ 200 = 3.5 cm long.

A scale of 1 : 20 means the real thing is 20 times bigger than the model. A 15 cm model stands for 15 × 20 = 300 cm = 3 m in real life.

Worked Example 3: Area, perimeter, and hectares

Rectangle: area = length × width, and perimeter = 2 × (length + width). For a field 10 m by 8 m: area = 80 m², perimeter = 36 m.

L-shapes: find the area of the big rectangle and subtract the missing corner. If a 3 m by 2 m corner is cut out of the 10 m by 8 m field, the area is 80 − 6 = 74 m². Surprise: the perimeter stays 36 m, because the two edges around the missing corner add up to exactly the same lengths as the edges that were removed!

Big areas are measured in hectares: 1 ha = 100 m × 100 m = 10 000 m². So 25 000 m² = 25 000 ÷ 10 000 = 2.5 ha.

Worked Example 4: Unit rates and percent increase

A camel caravan walks 90 km in 3 days. Its unit rate is 90 ÷ 3 = 30 km per day. In 10 days it would walk 30 × 10 = 300 km.

Percent increase = change ÷ starting amount × 100. A merchant buys tea for 20 coins and sells it for 70 coins: the change is 50, and 50 ÷ 20 × 100 = 250%.

Worked Example 5: Compass bearings

A bearing is an angle measured clockwise from North, written with three digits. North is 000°, East is 090°, South is 180°, and West is 270°. Northeast is halfway between North and East: 045°.

Turning clockwise adds to the bearing. If the total goes past 360°, subtract 360°. A ship on 300° that turns 100° clockwise ends up on 400° − 360° = 040°.

To go back the way you came (the back bearing), add 180° if the bearing is less than 180°, or subtract 180° if it is 180° or more. The back bearing of 060° is 240°; the back bearing of 300° is 120°.

Worked Example 6: Volume

Volume of a box (rectangular prism) = length × width × height. A block 30 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm has a volume of 1500 cm³.

1 m = 100 cm, so 1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 = 1 000 000 cm³. Change metres to centimetres before you multiply when the units don't match!
