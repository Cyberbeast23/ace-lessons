Walls, Paper, and a Needle That Points South: The Genius of Ancient China

Imagine a world with no paper: no books, no notebooks, no maps, and no birthday cards. Imagine sailing across an ocean with no compass, or watching a Canada Day sky with no fireworks. Every one of these things began in ancient China. For thousands of years, China was ruled by powerful families. Each ruling family was called a dynasty, and it passed power from parent to child, sometimes for hundreds of years. Historians often organize Chinese history by dynasty, the way you might organize a photo album by year.

For centuries, the land we now call China was split into rival kingdoms that fought one another so often that this time is called the Warring States period. Then, in 221 BCE, the king of the state of Qin (say "chin") conquered the last of his rivals and unified the kingdoms into one empire. He gave himself a new title: Qin Shi Huang, which means "First Emperor of Qin." Many historians think the word China comes from his dynasty's name. The First Emperor was harsh, but he was also a brilliant organizer. He ordered everyone to use the same style of writing, the same coins, and the same weights and measures. He even set one standard width for cart axles, so that wagons everywhere could roll in the same grooves on the roads. These rules helped hold a huge country together long after his dynasty fell.

The First Emperor also ordered his workers to connect older walls into one long barrier against raiders from the north. Later dynasties repaired, extended, and rebuilt these fortifications for nearly 2000 years. Most of the stone-and-brick wall that tourists walk on today was built during the Ming dynasty, more than 1500 years after the First Emperor. The Great Wall is not one single wall, but a network of walls, watchtowers, and forts. Guards in the towers could send warnings with smoke by day and fire by night, passing a message along the wall far faster than a rider on horseback. The wall came at a terrible cost, though. Hundreds of thousands of workers laboured in freezing winters and scorching summers, and many of them died. And despite a popular myth, you cannot see the Great Wall from the Moon with your eyes alone. It is incredibly long, but in most places it is only about as wide as a road.

The First Emperor desperately wanted to live forever, and he also planned for the afterlife. In 1974, farmers digging a well near the city of Xi'an struck pieces of baked clay. When archaeologists arrived, they uncovered something astonishing: thousands of life-sized clay soldiers, along with horses and chariots, lined up in battle formation to guard the emperor's tomb. Skilled artisans built the warriors' bodies from standard parts, almost like a factory, and then shaped the faces, hairstyles, and armour by hand, so that no two faces seem exactly alike. The soldiers were once painted in bright colours, but most of the paint flaked away within minutes of meeting fresh air. Digging continues slowly today, partly because scientists want to protect the remaining colours.

Paper might just be the greatest gift that ancient China gave the world. Before paper, Chinese writers wrote on strips of bamboo tied together with string, which were bulky and heavy, or on silk, which was very expensive. Around 105 CE, an official named Cai Lun improved a way to make paper by soaking and pounding tree bark, hemp, old rags, and worn-out fishing nets into a mushy pulp. Workers spread the pulp thinly on a screen and let it dry into sheets. The new paper was cheap, light, and surprisingly durable. Later, printers carved an entire page of characters backwards into a block of wood, inked it, and pressed paper on top. Then, around 1040, a man named Bi Sheng invented movable type: a separate little clay block for each character, which could be arranged to print one page and then rearranged for the next. However, written Chinese uses thousands of different characters, so a printer needed an enormous collection of type. For that reason, carved woodblocks stayed popular in China for centuries. Movable type had its biggest effect in Europe, where the alphabet has only a few dozen letters.

Long ago, the Chinese noticed that a lodestone, a naturally magnetic rock, always lined up in a north–south direction. At first they used it for fortune-telling and for deciding how to face new buildings. By about 1100 CE, sailors were using a magnetized needle to navigate when clouds hid the stars and the coast was out of sight. Chinese writers usually described the needle as pointing south! Around the same time, Chinese alchemists searching for a potion that would let people live forever mixed chemicals and created gunpowder instead. It was used first for fireworks and only later for weapons. China also guarded one secret for centuries: how to make silk from the cocoons of silkworms. Merchants carried rolls of silk west along the Silk Road, a network of trade routes stretching thousands of kilometres across deserts and mountains. Horses, glass, and silver travelled east in return, and so did ideas, religions, and new foods.

None of these inventions appeared all at once. Each one grew out of earlier ideas, careful observation, and a lot of trial and error. The ingenuity of ancient China's inventors still surrounds you today. The next time you open a book, check the compass on a phone, or watch fireworks light up the night, remember the people who first figured it all out more than a thousand years ago.
