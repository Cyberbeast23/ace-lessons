Fortress of Stone: The Rise and Fall of the Medieval Castle

Imagine standing on a hilltop in England in the year 1100. Below you, workers haul blocks of stone up wooden ramps while masons tap away with chisels. They are building a castle, and it will take years to finish. For about four hundred years, from roughly 1000 to 1500, castles like this one were the most powerful buildings in Europe. They were homes, army bases, courtrooms and treasure chests all in one.

The first castles were not made of stone at all. In the 1000s, Norman lords built "motte-and-bailey" castles. A motte was a tall mound of earth with a wooden tower on top, and the bailey was a fenced yard below where people, animals and workshops were kept. These castles could be thrown up in a few weeks, which was useful when a lord had just conquered new land. However, wood rots, and wood burns. An enemy with a torch could destroy a wooden tower in an afternoon.

So lords began to build in stone. They chose strategic places: the top of a steep hill, a bend in a river, or a cliff above the sea, where attackers would have to climb or cross water to reach the walls. At the centre stood the keep, a massive tower whose walls could be several metres thick. Around it ran a curtain wall topped with battlements, the notched stone tops that let archers shoot out and then duck behind cover. Many castles were also circled by a moat, a deep ditch often filled with water, crossed only by a drawbridge that could be raised in seconds.

The main gate was the weakest point in any wall, so builders made it the most heavily fortified. Behind the drawbridge hung a portcullis, a heavy iron-tipped grate that dropped down to block the gateway. Above the passage, holes in the ceiling let defenders drop stones or pour water on anyone trapped inside. A narrow, twisting entrance meant attackers could not rush in all at once.

Attackers had clever ideas too. If they could not break in, they could lay siege: surround the castle and wait for the people inside to run out of food and water. Some sieges lasted months. Attackers also built siege towers to roll up against the walls, and dug tunnels under them. The most fearsome weapon was the trebuchet, an ingenious machine that used a falling counterweight to fling stones weighing more than 100 kilograms at the walls. Every new attack taught builders something. Square towers had corners that could be knocked loose, so later towers were built round, with no weak corners to dig under. Each idea, on both sides, was a solution to the other side's last problem.

Life inside a castle was not as glamorous as it looks in films. Rooms were dark, smoky and cold in winter, because windows had to stay narrow. Dozens of servants cooked, cleaned, cared for horses and repaired weapons. A well in the courtyard was the most precious thing in the castle, because without water, even the strongest walls could not help the people endure a long siege.

Then, in the 1400s, gunpowder cannons spread across Europe. A cannonball could smash through stone walls that had resisted trebuchets for centuries. Tall, thin walls that once kept enemies out were now vulnerable targets. Armies began to build low, thick forts shaped like stars instead, and rich lords moved into comfortable palaces with big glass windows. Little by little, the castle became obsolete.

Yet castles have not disappeared. Hundreds still stand across Europe, some as crumbling ruins and some lived in to this day. Even in Canada, the stone walls of Québec City and the fortress at Louisbourg in Nova Scotia show that the old ideas of walls, gates and high ground travelled across the ocean. Every one of these buildings is a stone record of a long contest between people who wanted to get in and people who wanted to keep them out.

For example, wooden castles could be burned with a torch, so lords started building in stone. Later, attackers dug tunnels to knock loose the corners of square towers, so builders made round towers with no weak corners. In the end, gunpowder cannons were a problem that tall stone walls could not solve, because a cannonball could smash right through them. That is why armies switched to low, thick star forts and castles became obsolete.
