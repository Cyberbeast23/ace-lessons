🏰 Math Week 37: Fortress of Stone: Castle Engineer's Math Lab

Ace, about 900 years ago, kings and lords across Europe built stone castles to protect their land. A castle was a giant math problem: master masons had to calculate how much stone to haul, how thick to make the walls, how deep to dig the moat and how much food to store in case of a siege. Today you'll work as the master mason of the imaginary Castle Ironhold, using volume and mass, rounding up, scale drawings, angles in polygons, area of a "ring" shape, Roman numerals, the law of the lever, speed conversions, rates, percents from a table, the median, ratios, two-step equations, "fence-post" patterns and fractions of a remainder.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your master mason's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (m, cm, m³, kg, tonnes, s).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Draw a quick sketch of the wall, moat or tower.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A castle wall might be 3 m thick, but not 300 m!

Worked Example 1: Volume, then mass

A wall is a rectangular prism: volume = length × thickness × height. Then mass = volume × mass of 1 m³.

A wall 10 m long, 2 m thick and 5 m high holds 10 × 2 × 5 = 100 m³ of stone. If 1 m³ of stone has a mass of 2.5 tonnes, the wall is 100 × 2.5 = 250 tonnes.

Worked Example 2: Scale drawings

A scale of 1 : 200 means 1 cm on the plan is 200 cm (2 m) in real life. Multiply, then convert cm to m.

A hall that is 6 cm long on the plan is 6 × 200 = 1200 cm = 12 m long.

Worked Example 3: Angles in a regular polygon

The interior angles of a polygon with n sides add up to (n − 2) × 180°. In a regular polygon all angles are equal, so divide by n.

A regular hexagon (6 sides): (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°, and 720° ÷ 6 = 120° per corner.

Worked Example 4: Area of a "ring" (a path or moat)

Area of the ring = area of the big outer shape − area of the inner shape.

A 10 m × 8 m garden with a 6 m × 4 m pond inside: 80 − 24 = 56 m² of grass around the pond.

Worked Example 5: Roman numerals

I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500, M = 1000. Add the values, but a smaller numeral before a bigger one is subtracted (IV = 4, XC = 90, CM = 900).

MCDXCII = 1000 + 400 (CD) + 90 (XC) + 2 = 1492.

Worked Example 6: The law of the lever

A lever (like a seesaw or a trebuchet arm) balances when mass × distance from the pivot is the same on both sides.

A 30 kg child sits 2 m from the middle. To balance, a 60 kg adult must sit 30 × 2 ÷ 60 = 1 m from the middle.

Worked Example 7: Metres per second to kilometres per hour

There are 3600 seconds in an hour and 1000 m in a kilometre, so 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h. Multiply m/s by 3.6.

A galloping horse at 15 m/s is going 15 × 3.6 = 54 km/h.

Worked Example 8: The median

The median is the middle value when the data are in order. With an even number of values, it is the mean of the two middle values.

Data 3, 9, 5, 7 → in order 3, 5, 7, 9 → middle two are 5 and 7 → median = 6.

Worked Example 9: Fence-post patterns

If a row starts and ends with a post, there is always one fewer gap than posts. Write it as an equation.

Posts 1 m wide, gaps 2 m wide, total length 10 m: 1n + 2(n − 1) = 10 → 3n − 2 = 10 → 3n = 12 → n = 4 posts (and 3 gaps). Check: 4 + 6 = 10 ✔

Worked Example 10: Two-step equations

A squire earns 2 pennies a day plus a 10-penny gift and ends up with 30 pennies: 2d + 10 = 30 → 2d = 20 → d = 10 days. Undo the adding first, then undo the multiplying.
