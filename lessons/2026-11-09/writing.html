✍️ Writing Week 43: Public Service Announcement — Fire Safety Campaign 📣🚒

Yesterday you wrote museum exhibit labels. Today, Ace, you'll try a brand-new form: a public service announcement, or PSA. A PSA is a short message made to help the public, not to sell anything. You hear them on the radio, see them as posters at school, and watch them as 30-second videos. Every October, fire departments across Canada create PSAs for Fire Prevention Week. Today you are the writer for Station 7's new campaign. Your job: write a 30-second radio script and a poster that make kids and families actually do something to stay safe.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong PSA 📣

A PSA has one audience (who you're talking to), one purpose (what you want them to do), and very little time. Every part pushes toward action.

1. Hook: a sound, a question or a surprising moment that grabs attention in the first 3 seconds
2. The problem: what can go wrong and why it matters to this audience
3. Fact with a number: one exact, true fact that makes the problem real
4. Action steps: 2–3 short commands that tell people exactly what to do
5. Slogan: a short, catchy line that is easy to remember
6. Call to action + sign-off: what to do today, and who the message is from

Five tricks PSA writers use

  • Talk to "you": direct address makes listeners feel the message is meant for them.
  • The rule of three: lists of three are easy to remember ("Test it. Plan it. Practise it.").
  • Calm, not scary: a PSA should make people feel able to act, not frozen with fear.
  • One message only: don't try to teach everything about fire. Pick one big idea.
  • Sound and picture cues: in a script, write sound effects in [square brackets], like [SMOKE ALARM BEEPS].

PSA writer's rule: if a listener remembers only your slogan, they should still know what to do.

Mini-lesson: imperatives and parallel structure 🔧

An imperative sentence gives a command. Its subject (you) is hidden, and it starts with a verb: Test your alarm. Crawl low under smoke. Call 9-1-1 from outside.

Parallel structure means the items in a list use the same grammatical form, so they sound strong and balanced.

❌ Test your alarm, planning two ways out, and you should practise.
✅ Test your alarm, plan two ways out, and practise your escape.

Canadian spelling bonus: practise is the verb (we practise our drill); practice is the noun (practice makes perfect). Same pair: license / licence.

Model PSA (30-second radio script)

Hook + problemFact with a numberAction stepsSlogan + call to action

[SMOKE ALARM BEEPS THREE TIMES]
KID: Dad, what's that sound?
NARRATOR: That beep is the most important sound in your home. When smoke fills a room, you may have only a few minutes to get out.
Smoke rises and is full of toxic gases, and modern furniture can burn hot and fast.
So test your smoke alarms every month. Find two ways out of every room. Pick a meeting spot outside, like the big tree by the mailbox.
Remember: Hear the beep, get out, stay out. Practise your escape plan with your family tonight. A message from Station 7 and your local fire department.

Notice: the sound cue grabs attention; the narrator speaks to "you"; the action steps are short imperatives in parallel form; the slogan has a rhythm of three; the ending says when to act (tonight) and who is speaking.
