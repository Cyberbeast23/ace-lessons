Ready to Respond: How Firefighters Fight Fire, and How We Stop It Before It Starts

At 2:14 a.m., the lights snap on in a quiet fire hall. A tone blares, and a voice reads out an address. Less than two minutes later, a red engine rolls out the door with four firefighters aboard, already wearing their heavy coats and boots. Speed matters, because a house fire can change from "small" to "deadly" faster than most people imagine.

To fight fire, firefighters first have to understand it. Every fire needs three things: heat, fuel (something to burn, like wood, paper or cooking oil) and oxygen from the air. Scientists draw these as the three sides of a "fire triangle." Take away any one side and the fire goes out. Water mostly removes heat. A lid slid onto a burning pan cuts off oxygen. Clearing away dry brush in front of a wildfire removes fuel.

Inside a burning building, the biggest danger is often not the flames but the smoke. Smoke is a hot cloud of tiny particles and toxic gases, such as carbon monoxide, that can make a person confused and sleepy within a few breaths. Hot smoke also rises and collects near the ceiling, which is why safety experts teach people to crawl low, where the air is cooler and clearer. As the heat builds, everything in a room can suddenly burst into flame at once. This is called flashover. Modern homes are full of plastics and foam furniture that burn hot and fast, so researchers have found that flashover can now happen in just a few minutes.

That is why firefighters rely on special equipment. Their turnout gear has layers that block heat and water. On their backs they carry a breathing tank, called a self-contained breathing apparatus, that supplies clean air for a limited time. A thermal imaging camera shows heat as bright colours on a screen, so a crew can "see" a hidden fire inside a wall or find a person in thick smoke. Crews also work together to contain a fire, keeping it from spreading to the next room or the house next door.

Many people are surprised to learn that most calls to a fire department are not fires at all. Firefighters respond to medical emergencies, car crashes, gas leaks, floods and rescues. In many small Canadian towns, the firefighters are volunteers: neighbours who train in the evenings and leave their regular jobs when the pager goes off. In British Columbia, wildland crews spend summers fighting forest fires, sometimes digging fire lines by hand.

Still, every firefighter will tell you the same thing: the best fire is the one that never starts. Each October, fire halls across Canada mark Fire Prevention Week by visiting schools and holding open houses. Their message is simple. Put a smoke alarm on every level of your home and test it every month. Keep anything that can burn at least a metre away from heaters. Never leave cooking unattended. Make a home escape plan with two ways out of every room and an outside meeting place, then practise it with family drills. Once you are out, stay out, and call 9-1-1 from outside.

A smoke alarm costs less than a pizza, and it can give a family the extra minutes they need to escape. Firefighters are ready to respond at any hour, but a prepared family is the first line of defence.
