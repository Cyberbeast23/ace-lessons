Rivers in the Sky: How Storms Reach British Columbia

Every autumn, British Columbia waits for a special kind of storm. Satellite pictures show it coming: a long, thin ribbon of cloud stretching thousands of kilometres across the Pacific Ocean, often all the way from the warm waters near Hawaii. Scientists call this ribbon an atmospheric river. It is not made of liquid water like the Fraser River. Instead, it is a narrow band of invisible water vapour flowing through the atmosphere, the layer of air that wraps around Earth. An average atmospheric river carries about as much water as flows out of the mouth of the Mississippi River, one of the largest rivers in North America, and the strongest ones carry many times more. When one arrives from the tropics near Hawaii, British Columbians give it a friendlier nickname: the Pineapple Express.

How does all that water get into the sky? It starts with evaporation. The sun warms the ocean, and some of the water turns into a gas called water vapour. The amount of water vapour in the air is called humidity. Warm air can hold much more water vapour than cold air, which is why the air over tropical seas is so moist. When air is holding all the water vapour it possibly can, scientists say it is saturated. If saturated air cools down, it cannot keep all of its water as a gas. The extra vapour turns back into tiny liquid droplets, a process called condensation. You see condensation every time a cold glass of milk gets "sweaty" on a warm day. In the sky, billions of these droplets crowd together to form clouds. When the droplets bump into one another and grow heavy enough, they fall as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

Air also has weight, and it presses down on everything. We call this push air pressure, and we measure it with a barometer. At sea level, the air pushes down with about 101 kilopascals (kPa) of pressure. Air always flows from places of high pressure toward places of low pressure, and that flowing air is the wind. In a low-pressure area, air rises, cools, and condenses, so lows usually bring clouds, rain, and wind. In a high-pressure area, air sinks and warms, which usually means clear, calm weather. That is why a falling barometer is a warning sign. If the pressure drops quickly, a storm is probably on its way.

Storms often arrive along a front, the boundary where two large masses of air with different temperatures meet. At a warm front, warm air slides gently up and over colder air, bringing long, steady rain. At a cold front, heavy cold air shoves underneath warm air like a snowplough, forcing it to rise quickly. The result can be towering clouds, sudden downpours, gusty winds, and sometimes thunder. Weather maps show fronts as coloured lines: red half-circles for warm fronts and blue triangles for cold fronts.

British Columbia's mountains make its weather even more dramatic. When moist Pacific air blows inland, it runs into the Coast Mountains and is forced upward. As the air climbs, it cools, its water vapour condenses, and it drops huge amounts of rain and snow on the windward side, the side facing the wind and the ocean. Some coastal rainforests receive more than 3000 millimetres of rain a year. By the time the air crosses the peaks and sinks down the leeward side, it has lost most of its moisture, and sinking air warms and dries even more. This creates a rain shadow. Parts of the southern Interior, such as the Okanagan Valley, are so dry that cactus and sagebrush grow there, only a few hundred kilometres from dripping rainforests.

Atmospheric rivers can be both helpful and harmful. They refill reservoirs, build the mountain snowpack that melts into rivers in spring, and help put out late-summer wildfires. But too much rain, too fast, can cause floods and landslides. In November 2021, a powerful atmospheric river flooded farms in the Fraser Valley, forced thousands of people to leave their homes, and washed out sections of major highways. That is why a meteorologist's job matters so much. Forecasters gather measurements from weather stations, weather balloons, radar, and satellites, then feed them into powerful computer models that predict what the atmosphere will do next. When a forecast says there is a "70 percent chance of precipitation," it means that on days with conditions like these, it rains about seven times out of ten. No forecast is perfect, but good warnings give families, farmers, and road crews time to prepare before the river in the sky arrives.
