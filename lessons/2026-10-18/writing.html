✍️ Writing Week 21: Weather Report: Storm Warning! 🌧️📰

Yesterday you wrote a persuasive letter to change one reader's mind. Today, Ace, you'll write to inform a whole community, like a meteorologist on the evening news or a reporter at a newspaper's weather desk. A weather report is a kind of explanatory, news-style writing: it tells people what is happening, uses exact numbers and units, explains why it is happening with real science, predicts what will happen next, and tells people how to stay safe. Your goal is to write a report so clear that every reader knows what is coming and what to do.

Part 1: The Lesson

A weather report has six parts 🧩

Think of it like a news story with a science lesson tucked inside:

1. Headline + lead: a short, strong headline, then one sentence that answers who, what, when, where
2. Current conditions: what is happening now, with exact numbers and units (mm, °C, km/h, kPa, %)
3. The science: why it is happening, explained with cause-and-effect words (because, so, as a result)
4. The forecast: what will happen next, in the future tense, with ranges or chances
5. Safety advice: two or three clear things readers should do
6. Sign-off: a short closing line and your name, like a TV forecaster

Five forecaster's moves

  • Most important first: news writers use the inverted pyramid. The biggest, most useful facts go at the top, and smaller details come later, so a busy reader who stops after one sentence still knows what is coming.
  • Be precise: "lots of rain" is vague. "60 to 120 mm of rain by Monday morning" is a fact readers can use. Always write a space between the number and the unit: 12 °C, 70 km/h.
  • Explain the cause: link facts with because, so, as a result, and this means. A great report teaches readers something.
  • Translate science words: if you use a word like condensation or rain shadow, explain it in a few words for readers who don't know it.
  • Be honest about uncertainty: forecasts can change, so use careful words: likely, up to, a 70% chance. Use the future tense for forecasts (will arrive) and the past tense for storms that already happened (arrived).

Grown-up words: journalists call the first sentence the lead (sometimes spelled lede). Writing that explains how or why something happens is called expository (explanatory) writing, and an objective report sticks to facts instead of opinions.

Model Weather Report (short version)

Headline + leadConditions + the scienceThe forecastSafety advice + sign-off

PINEAPPLE EXPRESS TO SOAK THE SOUTH COAST
A powerful atmospheric river will reach the BC coast on Saturday night, bringing heavy rain to the mountains and strong winds to the islands, forecasters warned on Friday.
Right now, the air pressure at coastal weather stations is 101.2 kPa and falling, and the humidity is already 92%. The storm is a long, narrow band of warm, moist air flowing from near Hawaii. When it hits the Coast Mountains, the air will be forced upward and cool. As a result, its water vapour will condense, or turn into droplets, and pour down as rain on the windward slopes.
Rain will begin Saturday evening and become heavy overnight, with 60 to 120 mm likely by Monday morning. Because the air is so warm, around 12 °C, snow will only fall above about 2000 m. On Monday afternoon, a cold front will sweep through with gusts of up to 70 km/h, followed by cooler, clearer air.
Clear leaves from the drains near your home, stay away from fast-flowing creeks, and check road reports before driving through mountain passes. Stay safe and stay dry! This is Ace, reporting from the Weather Desk.

Notice: the lead answers who, what, when, and where in one sentence; every measurement has a number and a unit; the science paragraph uses cause-and-effect words and explains condense; the forecast uses the future tense, a range (60 to 120 mm), and careful words (likely, up to); and the safety advice is short and easy to follow.
