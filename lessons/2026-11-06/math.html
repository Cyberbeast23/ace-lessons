🦴 Math Week 40: Bone Hunters: Fossil Detective's Math Lab

Ace, palaeontologists are fossil detectives! They measure bones, map dig grids, estimate walking speeds of long-extinct animals, and date rock layers that are millions of years old. Today you are a junior fossil detective at the imaginary Badlands Dig Camp in Alberta, using scale models, tooth and bone measurements, distance-speed-time, Mesozoic timelines, area of a dig grid, ratios of carnivore to herbivore finds, percent of a skeleton recovered, the mean from a table, volume of a plaster jacket cylinder, elapsed dig days, two-step equations, unit rates, percent increase from a bar graph, GST on museum tickets, and rounding.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your fossil detective's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (mm, cm, m, km, million years, $, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change metres to centimetres or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A T. rex tooth might be 18 cm long, but not 18 m long!

Worked Example 1: Scale models

A real T. rex was about 12 m long. A museum model is built at a scale of 1 : 40 (every 1 cm on the model stands for 40 cm in real life).

12 m = 1 200 cm. Model length = 1 200 ÷ 40 = 30 cm.

Worked Example 2: Area of a dig grid

A rectangular dig trench is length × width for area, and 2 × (length + width) for perimeter.

A 5 m by 9 m trench: area = 5 × 9 = 45 m²; perimeter = 2 × (5 + 9) = 28 m.

Worked Example 3: Ratio parts

If carnivore finds and herbivore finds are in the ratio 1 : 4, think of it as 1 + 4 = 5 equal parts. Divide the total by the number of parts to find one part.

30 fossils in the ratio 1 : 4. One part = 30 ÷ 5 = 6, so there are 6 carnivores and 24 herbivores.

Worked Example 4: Volume of a cylinder (plaster jacket)

Fossil bones are wrapped in plaster jackets shaped like cylinders. Volume = π × r × r × h. We'll use π ≈ 3.14.

A jacket with radius 10 cm and height 50 cm: V = 3.14 × 10 × 10 × 50 = 15 700 cm³.

Worked Example 5: Percent of a whole

Percent recovered = (parts found ÷ total parts) × 100.

A skeleton has 200 bones and 150 are found: 150 ÷ 200 × 100 = 75%.

Worked Example 6: Mean from a table

The mean (average) = add all the values, then divide by how many there are.

Lengths 10, 14, 12: mean = (10 + 14 + 12) ÷ 3 = 12.

Worked Example 7: Percent increase

Percent increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Always divide by the starting amount.

From 8 fossils to 14: (14 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = 6 ÷ 8 × 100 = 75% increase.

Worked Example 8: Two-step equations

A dig permit costs $20, plus $5 for each fossil cast. A bill of $55: 20 + 5c = 55 → 5c = 35 → c = 7. Undo the adding first, then undo the multiplying.

Worked Example 9: GST in British Columbia

BC charges 5% GST on many goods. Multiply the total by 1.05 to add the tax in one step.

A $40 museum gift: 40 × 1.05 = $42.
