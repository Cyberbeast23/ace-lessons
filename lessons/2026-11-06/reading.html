Bone Hunters: How Fossils Form, and What Palaeontologists Do

Imagine standing in the Alberta badlands on a hot summer day. The ground is dry and cracked, and striped cliffs of sandstone and mudstone stretch as far as you can see. Somewhere under your boots may lie the bones of a Tyrannosaurus rex that died about 66 million years ago. Finding those bones, digging them up without breaking them, and figuring out what they mean is the job of a palaeontologist — a fossil detective.

Fossils are the remains or traces of ancient living things that have been preserved in rock. Bones, teeth, eggs, footprints and even burrows can become fossils. But fossilization is rare. Most plants and animals rot away or are eaten long before they can turn to stone. For a dinosaur to become a fossil, its body usually had to be buried quickly under mud, sand or volcanic ash, so scavengers and weather could not destroy it.

Once buried, a remarkable change can begin. Water seeping through the ground carries dissolved minerals. Over thousands and then millions of years, those minerals slowly replace the original bone, or fill its tiny spaces, until the bone is as hard as rock. This slow swap is called mineralization. The rock around the fossil is often made of layers of sediment that settled in rivers, lakes or seas. Those layers, studied through stratigraphy, help scientists tell which fossils are older: deeper layers are usually older than the ones above them.

Tyrannosaurus rex was one of the largest meat-eating dinosaurs that ever lived. A full-grown T. rex could be about twelve metres long and weigh as much as a small truck. It walked on two powerful hind legs, had a huge skull packed with banana-sized teeth, and belonged to a group of two-legged hunters called theropods. Birds today are the living relatives of theropods, so when you watch a chicken run, you are seeing a distant echo of T. rex! Scientists still debate details such as how fast it ran and whether it was closer to warm-blooded or cold-blooded animals, but they agree it was a top predator of the late Cretaceous Period.

Canada is famous for dinosaur fossils. Dinosaur Provincial Park in southern Alberta has yielded more than 150 dinosaur species and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The dry hills there are perfect for fossil hunting because wind and rain wear away the rock and slowly uncover bones. Palaeontologists map each find, chip rock away with small tools, wrap fragile bones in plaster jackets, and later clean and study them in a laboratory or museum.

Dating a fossil does not mean looking at a calendar. Instead, scientists use the age of the rock layers and, when they can, radioactive clocks in volcanic ash nearby. The Mesozoic Era — the Age of Dinosaurs — lasted from about 252 to 66 million years ago and included the Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. T. rex lived near the end of the Cretaceous, not long before a giant asteroid struck Earth and helped cause a mass extinction.

A palaeontologist's work is careful and slow. One wrong swing of a hammer can shatter a bone that has waited sixty million years to be found. But the reward is enormous: every tooth, vertebra and footprint is a clue. Put enough clues together, and you can rebuild not just a skeleton, but a whole lost world. The next time you stand in a museum under a towering T. rex, remember that every bone was dug, cleaned and fitted by people who read the rocks the way detectives read evidence.
