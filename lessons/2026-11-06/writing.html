Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a field-journal dig entry 📓

A field journal is a scientist's memory on paper. Each dig-log entry should let another palaeontologist understand exactly what happened that day — even years later.

1. Date + location header: when and where (e.g. "6 November 2026 — Quarry B, Dinosaur Provincial Park area")
2. Conditions: weather, light, team, tools (helps explain why the dig went well or slowly)
3. Sensory observations: what you see, hear, feel, smell — concrete details, not vague words like "amazing"
4. Hypothesis: what you think the find is or means, clearly labelled as a guess you will test
5. Evidence: measurements, rock layer, tooth shape, comparison to known fossils — facts that support (or challenge) your hypothesis
6. Next steps: what the team will do tomorrow (jacket the bone, map the grid, photograph, ask a senior scientist)

Five moves of a great fossil diarist

  • Be specific: "a 28 cm curved tooth with serrated edges" beats "a big tooth."
  • Separate facts from guesses: write "I observe…" and "I hypothesize…" so readers know which is which.
  • Use the senses: dust on your tongue, the scrape of a dental pick, the cool shade of the canyon wall.
  • Measure when you can: length, depth in the layer, grid square (A4), temperature.
  • Plan the next move: science is a chain of careful next steps, not one lucky day.

Diarist's rule: after reading your entry, another palaeontologist should know where you were, what you found, what you think it is, and what you will do next.

Model Dig Entry (short version): "Something Huge in Square C3"

Header + conditionsSensory observations + hypothesisEvidenceNext steps

6 November 2026 — Quarry B, Square C3
Clear sky, 24°C, light west wind. Team of four. Tools: brushes, dental picks, plaster rolls.
The sandstone under my knees feels warm. I hear the tick-tick of picks and smell dry sage. In Square C3 a dark curve poked out of the rock after we brushed for twenty minutes. Hypothesis: this may be a tyrannosaur tooth, possibly T. rex.
Evidence: exposed length already 16 cm; edges feel serrated through the glove; cross-section is oval, not flat like a plant-eater's tooth; we are in a late Cretaceous mudstone layer known for theropod finds.
Next steps: photograph in place, measure depth in the layer, widen the jacket area by 10 cm, and ask Dr. Chen to confirm before we lift anything.

Notice: the header has a date and square; conditions include weather and tools; sensory details put you in the trench; the hypothesis is labelled; evidence uses measurements and rock-layer knowledge; next steps are clear and careful.
