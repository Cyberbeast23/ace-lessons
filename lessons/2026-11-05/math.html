👁️ Math Week 39: Windows on the World: Eye Doctor's Math Lab

Ace, your eyes are working right now, reading these words! Eye doctors (called optometrists) use math every single day: fractions describe how sharp your vision is, the area of a circle tells how much light gets through your pupil, lenses are measured in diopters (a reciprocal!), and health researchers use ratios, percents and averages to study eyes. Today you are an eye doctor at the imaginary Clearview Eye Clinic, using rates and unit conversions, decimal fractions, circle area with π, scale factors and area, discounts plus tax, reciprocals with negative numbers, fractions of a number, the mean from a table, percent increase from a graph, two-step equations, ratio parts, very large numbers, and the path of light in a mirror.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your eye doctor's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (mm, cm, m, km, s, min, h, $, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change seconds to minutes or metres to centimetres before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A pupil might be 5 mm wide, but not 5 m wide!

Worked Example 1: Vision fractions

In Canada, eye charts are often read from 6 metres. Perfect "normal" vision is written 6/6. If your vision is 6/12, it means: what you can read at 6 m, a person with normal vision could read from 12 m away.

As a decimal, 6/12 = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5. The smaller the decimal, the blurrier the vision. (In the United States, 6/6 is called "20/20," using feet.)

Worked Example 2: Area of a circle

The pupil (the black opening in the middle of your eye) is a circle. Area = π × r × r, where r is the radius (half the diameter). We'll use π ≈ 3.14.

A pupil 4 mm across has r = 2 mm: area = 3.14 × 2 × 2 = 12.56 mm².

Worked Example 3: Scale factor and area (Grade 7 idea!)

If you make a circle's diameter k times bigger, its area becomes k × k times bigger, because both the length and the width grow.

A pupil that widens from 3 mm to 6 mm is 2 times as wide, so it lets in 2 × 2 = 4 times as much light.

Worked Example 4: Lens power in diopters

Eye doctors measure lenses in diopters (D). Power = 1 ÷ focal length in metres. A focal length is how far from the lens the light comes into focus.

A lens with focal length 0.5 m: 1 ÷ 0.5 = +2 D. Lenses for nearsighted people spread light out, so their focal length and power are negative: 1 ÷ (−0.25) = −4 D. Going backwards: focal length = 1 ÷ power.

Worked Example 5: Discount on part of a bill, then tax

If a discount is only on one item, take it off that item, add the other items, and then add the tax to the new total.

Frames $100 at 20% off = $80. Add $50 lenses = $130. Add 5% GST: 130 × 1.05 = $136.50.

Worked Example 6: Ratio parts

If two things are in the ratio 3 : 1, think of it as 3 + 1 = 4 equal parts. Divide the total by the number of parts to find one part.

80 marbles in the ratio blue : red = 3 : 1. One part = 80 ÷ 4 = 20, so there are 20 red and 60 blue.

Worked Example 7: Percent increase

Percent increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Always divide by the starting amount.

From 5 to 8: (8 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = 3 ÷ 5 × 100 = 60% increase. An increase can be more than 100%: from 2 to 6 is a 200% increase.

Worked Example 8: Two-step equations

An eye exam costs $40, plus $10 for each pair of contact lens samples. A bill of $90: 40 + 10p = 90 → 10p = 50 → p = 5. Undo the adding first, then undo the multiplying.

Worked Example 9: Time = distance ÷ speed (with huge numbers)

Light is the fastest thing in the universe: about 300 000 km every second. Time = distance ÷ speed. Cross off the same number of zeros from both numbers to make dividing easier.

The Moon is about 384 000 km away: 384 000 ÷ 300 000 = 1.28 seconds for moonlight to reach your eyes.
