Yesterday you weighed up two sides of an argument like a fair judge. Today, Ace, you'll write in a brand-new form: an advice column. In newspapers and magazines, readers send in letters asking for help, and an expert writes back. A great advice column sounds warm and friendly (like a kind doctor talking to you), but it is also accurate: it explains the science behind the problem and gives clear, practical steps. Today you'll be Dr. Iris, the eye expert at the Clearview Eye Clinic, answering a worried reader's letter.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of an advice column 💌

An advice column has two voices: the reader who asks, and the expert who answers. The expert's job is to make the reader feel understood and leave them knowing exactly what to do.

1. Headline: a short, catchy title that names the problem ("Help! The Board Looks Blurry!")
2. Reader's letter: "Dear Dr. Iris," a few sentences describing the problem, signed with a fun pen name ("Squinting in Grade 5")
3. Caring opening: the expert greets the reader by their pen name, shows they understand the feeling, and says the problem is common or fixable
4. Science explanation: why the problem happens, explained simply but accurately, with a comparison and a fact
5. Advice steps: three or more clear, practical tips, each starting with an action verb (try, take, ask, wear)
6. Encouraging closing + sign-off: a hopeful last line, then the expert's name ("See you clearly, Dr. Iris")

Five moves of a great advice columnist

  • Talk to one person: use "you" and the reader's pen name, as if you're sitting beside them.
  • Name the feeling first: "It can be frustrating when…" or "You're not alone…" before giving any advice.
  • Explain the "why": people follow advice better when they understand the reason behind it.
  • Use imperative verbs for tips: Take a break. Ask your teacher. Spend time outside.
  • Know your limits: a responsible expert says when to see a real doctor or a trusted adult.

Columnist's rule: after reading your answer, the reader should feel better and know exactly what to do next.

Model Column (short version): "Help! My Eyes Feel Tired After Gaming"

Help! My Eyes Feel Tired After Gaming
Dear Dr. Iris, after I play video games for a while, my eyes feel dry and sore, and sometimes things look a bit blurry. Is something wrong with me? Signed, Bleary-Eyed Gamer
Dear Bleary-Eyed Gamer, sore eyes are no fun, but you are definitely not alone! When you stare at a screen, you blink only about half as often as usual, so the front of your eyes dries out like a sponge left in the sun. The tiny muscles that focus your lens also get tired from looking at something close for so long.
Try the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something about 6 metres away for 20 seconds. Remind yourself to blink fully. Ask an adult to help you set a timer, and spend time playing outdoors each day.
If your eyes still feel sore after a week, ask a parent to book an eye exam. With a few small changes, you'll be back in the game with happy eyes! See you clearly, Dr. Iris

Notice: the headline names the problem; the letter has a pen name; Dr. Iris names the feeling first ("not alone"); the science uses a fact (blinking half as often) and a comparison (a sponge in the sun); the tips start with action verbs (Try, Remind, Ask); and the closing says when to see a real doctor before ending on a hopeful note.
