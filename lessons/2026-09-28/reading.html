The Pump That Never Rests

Make a fist and look at it. Your heart is roughly that size, and right now, while you read this sentence, it is working harder than any other muscle in your body. It started beating before you were born, and it has not taken a single break since: not during sleep, not during a birthday party, not even for a second. Scientists estimate that the heart beats about 100 000 times every day. Over a long life, that adds up to more than two and a half billion beats. No machine built by humans can match that kind of endurance.

So what exactly does this tireless organ do? The heart's job is to pump blood, and blood is the body's delivery service. Every cell in your body, from the cells in your toes to the cells in your brain, needs a constant supply of oxygen and nutrients. Blood carries these supplies to the cells and then hauls away wastes such as carbon dioxide. Without a steady flow, cells begin to suffer. Brain cells are especially sensitive; they can be damaged after only a few minutes without oxygen.

The heart is divided into four hollow chambers. The two upper chambers are called atria (just one is an atrium). They act like waiting rooms, collecting blood as it returns to the heart. The two lower chambers are called ventricles, and they are the powerful pumps that push blood out. A thick wall of muscle called the septum separates the right side of the heart from the left side, so the blood on the two sides never mixes.

Each side has a different mission. The right side receives blood that has already delivered its oxygen and sends it on a short trip to the lungs. There, the blood releases carbon dioxide, which you breathe out, and picks up fresh oxygen, which you breathed in. The refreshed blood then travels back to the left side of the heart. The left ventricle has the toughest job of all: it must squeeze hard enough to push blood through the entire body, all the way to your fingertips and back. That is why its muscular wall is much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle.

If you have ever pressed your ear against someone's chest, you may have heard a sound like "lub-dub, lub-dub." Those sounds are not made by the muscle itself. They are made by valves, small flaps of tissue that snap shut to keep blood flowing in only one direction. Doctors listen to these sounds with a stethoscope. An unusual sound, called a murmur, can be a clue that a valve is not closing properly, although many murmurs, especially in children, turn out to be harmless.

What tells the heart when to beat? Tucked into the wall of the right atrium is a tiny cluster of special cells called the sinoatrial node. It produces an electrical signal that spreads across the heart and causes the muscle to contract. Because it sets the rhythm, it is often called the heart's natural pacemaker. If this system stops working properly, surgeons can implant an artificial pacemaker, a small battery-powered device that sends regular electrical pulses to keep the heartbeat steady.

The blood that leaves the heart travels through an astonishing network of blood vessels. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins carry it back, and tiny capillaries, many thinner than a strand of hair, connect the two. If all of an adult's blood vessels were laid end to end, they would stretch roughly 100 000 kilometres, enough to circle Earth about two and a half times.

Even though your heart works without any instructions from you, the choices you make still affect it. Regular exercise makes the heart muscle stronger and more efficient, so a fit person's heart can pump the same amount of blood with fewer beats. Eating vegetables, fruits, and whole grains, getting enough sleep, and never smoking all help protect the heart for decades. Your heart will keep faithfully beating for you every second of your life. The least you can do is take good care of it in return.
