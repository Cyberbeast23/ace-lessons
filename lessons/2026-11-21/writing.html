✍️ Writing Week 55: Picture Book for Younger Readers — The Sugar Bug Battle 📚🦷

Yesterday you wrote an FAQ page for readers your own age. Today, Ace, you'll write for a much younger audience: a picture book for kindergarten or Grade 1 kids. This is harder than it sounds! A picture-book author has to take a real science idea (like how cavities form) and explain it in short sentences and simple words, without saying anything false, and make it fun enough that a five-year-old begs to hear it again. Authors also plan what the pictures will show, because in a picture book the words and the pictures share the job. Today's genre is the picture book for younger readers: a story with a character, a problem, a repeating line, simple true science, and a happy ending.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong science picture book 📚

Most picture books are about 12 to 16 pages, with only 1 to 3 short sentences on each page.

1. Title and cover line: a catchy title (rhyme or alliteration helps) and a line that tells the young reader what's inside ("A story about keeping your smile strong")
2. Meet the main character: a character little kids care about (a kid, an animal, even a talking tooth) and what makes them special
3. The problem: something goes wrong that the character has to deal with (a sweet snack, a sore tooth, a scary first visit)
4. Repeating pattern line: a fun line that comes back again and again, so young listeners can join in
5. Simple science explanation: the true idea, in words a five-year-old knows, often with a comparison ("Plaque is like sticky glue for germs.")
6. Happy ending and reader tip: the problem is solved, and the last page gives the reader something to do ("Now it's your turn to brush!")

Five habits of great picture-book authors

  • Know your reader: a five-year-old knows words like sticky, germs and sweet, but not enamel, bacteria or acid. If you use a science word, explain it right away in kid words.
  • Simplify, but never say something false: ❌ "Candy drills holes in your teeth." ✅ "Germs love sugar. When they eat it, they make sour acid that can make holes in teeth."
  • Use sound and pattern: repetition, rhyme, rhythm and sound words (scrub, swish, crunch!) make a book fun to read aloud.
  • Let the pictures help: write [Picture: …] notes so the illustrator shows what the words don't need to say. You don't have to write "Sam had a red shirt" if the picture shows it.
  • Show, don't lecture: let the character learn the lesson through what happens, instead of the narrator saying "You must brush!" on every page.

Picture-book author's rule: read your book out loud. If you run out of breath in a sentence, or a little kid would ask "What does that mean?", fix it.

Mini-lesson: grammar for storytellers 💬

Dialogue punctuation: put quotation marks around the exact words spoken, and put the comma, question mark or exclamation mark inside the closing quotation mark: "Open wide," said Dr. Patel. "Is it my turn?" asked Sam. Start a new paragraph each time a new character speaks.

Commas after introductory words: First, Sam brushed the top teeth. Every night, Sam flosses. After lunch, Sam swished with water.

Their / there / they're: their = belonging to them (their toothbrushes), there = a place (over there), they're = they are (they're brushing).

Exclamation marks: one is exciting; three in every sentence is shouting. Save them for the big moments: Crunch!

Canadian spelling: colour, favourite, flavour, neighbour, centre. A picture book's favourite colour keeps its u!

Model picture book: "Sam and the Sugar Bugs" (about 200 words)

Sam and the Sugar Bugs
A story about keeping your smile strong

PAGE 1: This is Sam. Sam has twenty shiny teeth, and Sam loves to smile. [Picture: Sam grinning in a mirror.]

PAGE 2: One day, Sam ate a sticky, sweet candy. Then another. Then ANOTHER. [Picture: a pile of wrappers.]
PAGE 3: Tiny sugar bugs (germs too small to see) cheered. "Yum, yum, sugar for us!"
PAGE 4: That night, Sam was sleepy. "I'll skip brushing," Sam yawned.
The sugar bugs giggled. "Yum, yum, sugar for us!"

PAGE 5: Sugar bugs eat sugar, and then they make sour acid. The acid is like a tiny eraser that can rub a hole in a tooth. [Picture: a small, worried tooth.]
PAGE 6: The next morning, Sam's tooth felt funny. Mom took Sam to see Dr. Patel, the dentist. "Open wide," she said.

PAGE 7: Dr. Patel showed Sam how to brush for two whole minutes. Scrub, scrub, swish! "No sugar for you!" Sam laughed.
PAGE 8: Now Sam brushes every morning and every night. Sam's smile is strong again.

Now it's your turn. Grab your toothbrush. Scrub, scrub, swish!

Notice: the sentences are short, and every hard idea is put into kid words (sugar bugs (germs too small to see), acid is like a tiny eraser). The repeating line "Yum, yum, sugar for us!" comes back, then flips into "No sugar for you!" at the turning point. Sam learns the lesson through the story. Look for dialogue punctuation ("Open wide," she said.), the sound words scrub, scrub, swish, the picture notes, and the reader tip on the last page.
