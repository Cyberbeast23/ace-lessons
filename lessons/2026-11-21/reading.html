Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you read precisely, noticing small qualifiers like most and rarely. Today's passage is different from most of the ones you've read: it is an argument. The author isn't just explaining; the author is trying to convince you of something. Today's skill is one that lawyers, scientists, judges and smart voters use all the time: tracing an argument. As you read, find these five parts:
  • 1. Claim: the main point the author wants you to believe. It can usually be stated in one sentence, and someone could disagree with it.
  • 2. Reasons: the "because" statements that support the claim.
  • 3. Evidence: facts, numbers, history, expert findings or examples that back up each reason. Ask: is this evidence strong and relevant?
  • 4. Counterargument: an opposing view that a fair author admits some people hold ("Some people say…", "It's true that…").
  • 5. Rebuttal: the author's answer to the counterargument, explaining why it doesn't change the claim ("But…", "However…").

Open Wide! Why Every Tooth Deserves Care, Even the Ones That Fall Out

Every night, millions of Canadian kids stand at the bathroom sink, toothbrush in hand, and wonder whether this really matters. Grown-ups nag. Dentists remind. But here is a question that deserves a serious answer: why should anyone take care of baby teeth, when every single one of them is going to fall out anyway? This article argues that baby teeth deserve just as much care as adult teeth, and that the habits you build with them protect your health for the rest of your life.

People have always fought tooth trouble. Humans have been cleaning their teeth for thousands of years. In ancient times, people in many parts of the world chewed the frayed ends of twigs to scrub their teeth. By about the year 1500, people in China were making toothbrushes with stiff hog bristles set into handles of bone or bamboo. In 1728, a French surgeon named Pierre Fauchard published a book that explained how cavities form and how to treat them, and he is often called the father of modern dentistry. Around 1780, an Englishman named William Addis began making toothbrushes in large numbers. Toothache, it seems, is one of humanity's oldest enemies.

How a cavity forms. Your mouth is home to hundreds of kinds of bacteria. Most are harmless, but some live in plaque, the sticky, colourless film that builds up on teeth. When you eat or drink something with sugar, these bacteria feast on it and produce acid. For about 20 minutes or more after each snack, that acid can dissolve minerals out of your enamel. Your saliva fights back: it washes away food and helps neutralize the acid. Fluoride, found in toothpaste and in the tap water of many towns, helps enamel become harder and more resistant to acid. But if acid attacks happen again and again, all day long, saliva can't keep up. A weak spot becomes a hole, and the hole is a cavity.

Some people say baby teeth don't matter. It's true that baby teeth are temporary. A child's first teeth usually erupt through the gums at around six months old, and the last baby teeth usually fall out somewhere between ages 10 and 12. So why bother? It is a fair question, and the author will concede one point: a small cavity in a baby tooth that is about to fall out may not always need a filling. A dentist decides that case by case.

But baby teeth have important jobs. First, baby teeth hold space in the jaw for the permanent teeth growing underneath. If a baby tooth is lost too early because of decay, the neighbouring teeth can drift into the gap, and the adult tooth may come in crooked or get stuck. That can mean years of visits to an orthodontist. Second, children use their baby teeth to chew and to speak clearly for most of their childhood. Some baby molars stay in the mouth until age 12, which is longer than you've been alive, Ace! Third, a cavity in a baby tooth is not just a cosmetic problem. It can grow into a painful infection, and pain makes it hard to eat, sleep and concentrate at school.

The evidence is serious. Canadian health experts often describe tooth decay as the most common chronic disease of childhood, more common than asthma. A 2013 report from the Canadian Institute for Health Information found that treating severe tooth decay accounted for about one-third of all day surgeries for Canadian children aged 1 to 5. Those children had to be put to sleep in a hospital so dentists could repair or remove many teeth at once. Yet almost all of that decay is preventable. That is why many communities add fluoride to tap water, starting in 1945, when Brantford, Ontario, became the first city in Canada to do so, and why dental hygienists visit schools to teach brushing.

Habits last longer than teeth. The strongest reason to care for baby teeth may be the least obvious one. The consequence of brushing every night isn't only a cleaner mouth today; it's a routine. A kid who brushes for two minutes, twice a day, and flosses before bed is practising exactly the habits their permanent teeth will need for the next 70 or 80 years. Your adult teeth don't come with a spare set.

So, does it really matter? Yes. Baby teeth save space for adult teeth, help you chew and talk for years, and can cause real pain and infection when they decay. Most importantly, they are where lifelong habits begin. The next time you're tempted to skip brushing because "these teeth are falling out anyway," remember: the habits you're building aren't going anywhere.

Part 2: Think Deeper (not scored)

A friend says: "I only brush in the morning. My teeth look fine, so brushing at night is a waste of time." Write a short rebuttal like the author's. First concede something fair about your friend's view ("It's true that…"). Then answer it with a reason and evidence from the passage ("But…").

Model answer: It's true that your teeth might look fine right now, and morning brushing is better than nothing. But the passage explains that cavities start as invisible weak spots: bacteria in plaque turn sugar into acid that dissolves minerals from enamel, and once a hole forms, enamel can't repair itself because it has no living cells. If you skip the night brush, plaque and leftover food sit on your teeth all night. "Looks fine" isn't strong evidence, because the damage starts before you can see it. That's why dentists recommend brushing twice a day.
