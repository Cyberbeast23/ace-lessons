📖 Reading Week 52: Hockey by the Numbers: Can Statistics Tell the Truth About a Game?

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you paraphrased and summarized. Today's skill is one that scientists, reporters, coaches and smart voters all need: evaluating evidence, especially numbers. A statistic can sound convincing and still not prove what someone claims. As you read, test every number with four detective questions:
  • 1. Is the sample big enough? A sample is the group of games, shots or people that were counted. Three games can't tell you much; eighty games can tell you a lot more.
  • 2. Total or rate? A total ("20 goals") depends on how much someone played. A rate ("goals per game" or "goals per 100 shots") lets you compare fairly.
  • 3. Linked, or the cause? Two things can happen together (a correlation) without one causing the other. Ask: could something else explain both?
  • 4. Cherry-picked? Did someone choose only the numbers that help their side and leave out the rest?

Hockey by the Numbers: Can Statistics Tell the Truth About a Game?

On March 3, 1875, a crowd squeezed into the Victoria Skating Rink in Montreal to watch something new: a game of hockey played indoors, with rules written down ahead of time. The organizer, a young engineer named James Creighton, had one worry. A bouncing ball might fly off the ice and hit the spectators, so the players chased a flat, round piece of wood instead. That flat disc was the ancestor of today's puck, and the game played that night is often called the first recorded indoor hockey game. Almost 150 years later, hockey is still Canada's winter game, but something about it has changed: people now study it with numbers as carefully as scientists study an experiment.

A game full of numbers. Today's puck is made of hard rubber, about 2.5 cm thick and 7.6 cm across, and it is frozen before games so that it slides smoothly and bounces less. Everything that happens to it can be counted. For more than a hundred years, newspapers have printed goals, assists (passes that help set up a goal) and points (goals plus assists). Goalies are judged by their save percentage, the share of shots they stop. Since the late 1960s, the NHL has also kept an official stat called plus/minus: a player earns +1 if her team scores while she is on the ice and −1 if the other team scores.

Numbers that can fool you. Statistics are powerful because they turn a fast, confusing game into facts you can compare. But a number is only as good as the question behind it. Imagine a player who scores 5 goals in his first 3 games. A reporter writes, "At this pace, he'll score 135 goals this season and smash every record!" That sounds exciting, but it isn't reliable. Three games is a tiny sample. A lucky bounce or two can make a short streak look amazing, and over a full season most streaks fade back toward a player's usual level. Statisticians have a rule of thumb: the smaller the sample, the less you should trust the pattern.

Plus/minus shows a different problem. A strong defender on a team that is having a terrible year can end up with a low plus/minus, because the goals against him often happen when his teammates make mistakes. A weaker player on a great team can look better than he really is. In other words, the number is misleading on its own: it mixes up how well one player plays with how well the whole team plays. That doesn't make plus/minus useless, but it means a careful fan never judges a player by it alone.

Counting what used to be ignored. In the 2000s, a group of fans, bloggers and team staff tried a new idea. Goals are rare, so they are hard to study. Shot attempts are common: a team may attempt 50 or 60 shots in a single game, counting the ones that miss the net or get blocked. These analysts counted every attempt and noticed a pattern: over a long season, teams that took more of the shot attempts in their games usually spent more time with the puck, and teams that controlled the puck usually won more often. One popular version of this stat was named after Jim Corsi, a goalie coach who used shot counts to measure how busy his goalies were. Hundreds of games of evidence made the pattern hard to ignore, and today almost every NHL team employs analysts.

Linked is not the same as caused. Analysts also learned to be careful about what a pattern means. Suppose you notice that a team wins more games when its players wear their new white jerseys. Do the jerseys cause the wins? Probably not. In many leagues, teams wear one colour at home and another on the road, and teams tend to win a little more at home, where they have their own fans, their own rink and no long bus ride. The jerseys and the wins are linked, but the real cause is a third factor: playing at home. Scientists call a link between two things a correlation, and one of the most important lessons in all of science is that correlation does not prove causation.

Watching for cherry-picking. Numbers can also be chosen to tell only one side of a story. A coach who wants more ice time for her son might say, "He scored in four of our last five games!" That's true, but if she leaves out that he had no goals in the fifteen games before that, she is cherry-picking: choosing only the numbers that help her argument. A good reader always asks, "What numbers are missing?"

The future of the numbers. In recent seasons, the NHL has placed small tracking sensors inside pucks and in players' equipment. Cameras and computers can now record where every player is and how fast the puck moves, many times every second. Teams use this flood of data to plan practices, find tired players and choose strategies. Yet coaches still watch games with their own eyes, because some things, like a player's courage, teamwork or a smart pass that wasn't quite completed, are hard to turn into a number.

So the next time someone tells you a statistic, at the rink, in an ad or in the news, put on your detective hat. How big was the sample? Is it a total or a fair rate? Could something else be causing the pattern? What might be missing? Numbers can tell the truth about a game, but only when the people using them ask good questions.

Part 2: Think Deeper (not scored)

A team's website says: "Since Coach Patel started bringing her lucky orange scarf to games, the team has won 3 of 4 games. The scarf works!" Use at least two of today's four detective questions to evaluate this claim. Then explain what evidence would actually convince you that something helps the team win.

Model answer: First, the sample is tiny: 4 games is far too few to prove anything, because a short lucky streak happens to every team. Second, the scarf and the wins are only linked (a correlation); something else could be the real cause, such as easier opponents, more home games, or a star player coming back from an injury at the same time. It also might be cherry-picked if the team lost lots of games the season before when the scarf was there too. To convince me, I'd want to compare many games, maybe a whole season, with and without the scarf, against similar opponents, and see a big, steady difference, along with a reason it could actually cause better play (which a scarf can't really do!).
