✍️ Writing Week 52: Play-by-Play & Colour Commentary Script — Live from the Rink! 🏒🎙️

Yesterday you wrote a calm, thoughtful artist's statement. Today, Ace, you'll write something that is meant to be heard, fast and loud: a sports commentary script. On a hockey broadcast there are usually two voices. The play-by-play announcer tells listeners exactly what is happening right now, in quick, vivid, present-tense sentences. The colour commentator, often a former player or coach, explains why it happened, adds a statistic or a story, and helps the audience understand the strategy. Together they turn a game into a story that someone listening on the radio, who can't see a thing, can follow and feel. Today's genre is a script: writing for two speakers, with names, lines and short stage directions.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong commentary script 🎙️

Think of it as a mini-story told by two people who take turns.

1. Scene-setter: where we are, who is playing, the score, the time left and what's at stake
2. Play-by-play: short, present-tense action lines with strong verbs: who has the puck and where it goes
3. Colour commentary: the expert explains why, with a fact or a statistic (a rate, not just a total!)
4. The big moment: the build-up and the climax: a goal, a huge save, a breakaway
5. Replay + analysis: slow it down and explain the key detail that made the play work
6. Sign-off: the final score, what it means, and a memorable last line

Five habits of great commentators

  • Present tense for live action: "Maya picks up the puck… she is flying down the wing!" Switch to past tense only in the replay: "Watch how she faked the shot."
  • Strong, precise verbs instead of weak ones. ❌ "He hits the puck and it goes in." ✅ "He snaps a wrist shot that rockets over the goalie's glove."
  • Paint the picture for a listener who can't see: say where the puck is (blue line, left circle, top of the crease) and who has it.
  • Make the colour commentator earn their spot: every colour line should add a reason, a strategy or a fair statistic, not just "Wow, that was great!"
  • Vary sentence length: short, punchy sentences speed up the action. "She shoots. She scores!" Longer sentences slow it down for analysis.

Broadcaster's rule: close your eyes and have someone read your script out loud. If you can picture every play, it works.

Mini-lesson: grammar for scripts and sports writing 📻

Script format: write each speaker's name in capital letters, followed by a colon, then the line: PLAY-BY-PLAY: Here comes the opening faceoff. Put short stage directions (sounds and actions, not spoken words) in square brackets: [The crowd roars.] No quotation marks are needed around the spoken lines in a script.

Collective nouns take a singular verb in Canadian English: the team is, the crowd roars, the Rapids defence has been strong. (But a team's name in the plural, like the Rapids, takes a plural verb: the Rapids are ahead.)

Possessives: team's vs. teams': one team owns it → the team's coach. Two or more teams own it → both teams' goalies. Plural names: the Rapids' power play.

Numbers: use numerals for scores, times and stats (3–2, with 45 seconds left, a 92% save percentage), but spell out a number that starts a sentence (Forty-five seconds to go!). Canadian spelling: colour, centre, defence, favourite, metre.

Model commentary script (about 250 words)

Scene-setterPlay-by-play + big momentColour commentary + replaySign-off

PLAY-BY-PLAY: Good evening, hockey fans, and welcome to a packed Riverside Arena! It's the third period of the Peewee final, the Hillcrest Thunder lead the Riverside Rapids 2–1, and just 90 seconds remain.
COLOUR: The Thunder's goalie has been a brick wall tonight, with 27 saves on 29 shots. That's a save percentage of about 93%.
PLAY-BY-PLAY: Rapids win the faceoff! Sam slides it back to the point. He wires a pass across the blue line to Maya. Maya dekes around one defender, cuts to the middle… she's in alone! [The crowd rises.] She shoots… SHE SCORES! Top corner, glove side! It's tied 2–2!
COLOUR: Let's look at the replay. Watch Maya's eyes. She looked to the right, so the goalie slid right, and then she snapped it to the left. Maya scores on about 15% of her shots, but from the slot she's even more dangerous. That's why the coach keeps telling her to drive to the middle.
PLAY-BY-PLAY: What a finish! We're headed to overtime here at Riverside Arena. Stay with us, folks; this one isn't over yet!

Notice: speaker names are in capitals with a colon; the stage direction is in square brackets; live action is in the present tense with strong verbs (slides, wires, dekes, snapped in the replay); the colour lines give a reason and a fair rate; the short sentences speed up the big moment. Look for The Thunder's goalie (possessive) and the numerals in 2–1 and 90 seconds.
