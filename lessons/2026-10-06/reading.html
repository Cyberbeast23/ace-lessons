Rome: The City That Engineered an Empire

Every time you turn on a tap, walk across a stone bridge, or read the numbers on an old clock, you are touching ideas that are more than two thousand years old. They come from Rome, which began as a cluster of farming villages on seven hills beside the Tiber River in Italy. According to legend, the city was founded in 753 BCE. Over the next eight centuries, that small settlement grew until it ruled territory stretching from the misty hills of Britain to the deserts of Egypt.

For much of its history, Rome was a republic. In 509 BCE the Romans drove out their last king and decided that no single person should hold power for long. Each year, citizens elected two leaders called consuls, and a council of experienced men called the Senate advised them. (The word senate comes from the Latin senex, meaning "old man.") Over time, however, ambitious generals fought bloody civil wars for control. In 27 BCE a leader named Augustus became Rome's first emperor. The Senate still met, but the real power now belonged to one ruler. In short, the Republic tried to spread power among many leaders, while the Empire concentrated it in the hands of one. Wise emperors kept the Senate anyway, because it made their rule look traditional and fair.

To hold such a huge empire together, Rome needed to move soldiers, messages, and goods quickly. Its engineers built more than 80 000 kilometres of paved roads. A Roman road was like a layered sandwich: packed earth at the bottom, then large stones, then gravel, and finally tightly fitted paving stones on top. Each road was slightly higher in the middle than at the edges, so rainwater ran off into ditches instead of forming puddles that would wear the road away. Stone markers called milestones told travellers how far they had come.

A city that may have reached a million people also needed enormous amounts of fresh water. The Romans built aqueducts, a word that joins the Latin aqua (water) and ducere (to lead). An aqueduct used no pumps at all, only gravity. Engineers measured the gradient so carefully that one famous channel in France drops only about 25 centimetres per kilometre on average. If the slope was too steep, rushing water would scrape away the channel. If it was too flat, the water would slow down and become stagnant, which could make people sick. Most aqueduct channels actually ran underground; the famous arched bridges were built only where a channel had to cross a valley. The water filled public fountains, bathhouses, and even toilets flushed by running water, which helped keep the crowded city cleaner and healthier.

Two inventions made these structures possible. The first was the arch. Wedge-shaped stones lean against one another, with a keystone locked in at the top, so the weight above presses the stones together and pushes it down into the supports. The second was Roman concrete, a mixture of lime and volcanic ash. It was so durable that harbour walls standing in seawater have survived for two thousand years; scientists have found that seawater actually helped new minerals grow inside the concrete. The Colosseum, which opened in 80 CE and held about 50 000 spectators, rose on rings of stacked arches. The dome of the Pantheon, built nearly 1900 years ago, is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world.

Rome's greatest legacy may be its language. Latin grew into Italian, French, Spanish, and other languages, and thousands of English words have Latin roots: aquarium from aqua, civic from civis (citizen), and legion from legio. Doctors still name bones in Latin, such as the femur and the cranium. Yet Rome was far from perfect. Only some men could vote, enslaved people did much of the hardest work, and crowds cheered as gladiators fought in deadly games. Studying Rome helps us see both the brilliant ideas worth keeping and the mistakes we should never repeat.
