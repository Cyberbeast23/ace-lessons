✍️ Writing Week 9: A Persuasive Speech to the Roman Senate

Yesterday you told a story. Today, Ace, you'll step into the Senate House in Ancient Rome and give a persuasive speech. Speeches are written to be heard, so great speakers use rhetorical devices: special tricks with words that grab listeners and make an argument hard to forget. You'll also learn the strongest move in persuasion: admitting the other side's argument and then answering it (a counterargument and rebuttal).

Part 1: The Lesson

A persuasive speech has a shape

Roman speakers such as Cicero trained for years to persuade the Senate. Their speeches followed a plan you can still use today:

1. Hook: grab attention and speak directly to your audience
2. Claim: say exactly what you want them to believe or do
3. Reasons + evidence: two or three strong reasons with facts
4. Counterargument + rebuttal: "Some say... but..."
5. Call to action: tell them what to do now

Five rhetorical devices to try

  • Direct address: speak to your listeners by name. "Senators of Rome, hear me."
  • Rhetorical question: a question asked to make people think, not to get an answer. "Who among us wants to drink dirty water?"
  • Rule of three: list three ideas in a row; it sounds complete and memorable. "Clean water means healthier children, busier markets, and a prouder Rome."
  • Repetition: repeat a key word or phrase to hammer it home. "We need water. We need it now. We need it for every citizen."
  • Counterargument + rebuttal: admit what the other side says, then prove why you are still right. "Some say an aqueduct costs too much. But sickness costs far more."

Useful persuasive linking words: first, furthermore, most importantly, admittedly, however, even so, therefore, for these reasons.

Model Speech

Hook + claimReasons + evidenceCounterargument + rebuttalCall to action

Senators of Rome, how can a city that rules the world still let its children drink from muddy wells? I stand before you today to ask for one thing: a new aqueduct for the eastern hills. First, fresh running water keeps people healthy, because stagnant water spreads disease. Furthermore, water would fill new fountains, baths, and workshops, bringing busier markets, more jobs, and higher taxes for the treasury. Most importantly, our engineers have already measured a gentle gradient of only 30 centimetres per kilometre, so the water can flow by gravity alone. Admittedly, some of you say an aqueduct costs too much. However, every winter we pay doctors, bury the sick, and lose workers to fever. Sickness costs far more than stone. Therefore, I urge you to vote yes today. Vote for health. Vote for work. Vote for Rome!

Notice: The speech starts with direct address and a rhetorical question, gives three reasons with evidence, answers the other side, and ends with repetition and a rule of three.
