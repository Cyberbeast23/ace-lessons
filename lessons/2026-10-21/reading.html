Four Forces and a Bicycle Shop: The Science of Flight

A fully loaded passenger jet can weigh more than 70 000 kilograms, about as much as fifty cars. Yet every day, thousands of them rise off runways and cruise for hours, kilometres above the clouds. It looks like magic, but it isn't. Every airplane, from a paper glider to a giant jet, is controlled by the same four forces, and engineers have learned to balance them almost perfectly.

The first force is weight, the pull of gravity on the plane and everything inside it. The second is thrust, which pushes the plane forward. A propeller or a jet engine shoves a huge amount of air backwards, and because every action has an equal and opposite reaction, the air shoves the plane forward. The third force is drag, the resistance of the air rubbing and pushing against the plane as it moves. That is why airplanes have smooth, pointed noses and sleek bodies: a streamlined shape slices through the air with less drag. The fourth force, lift, holds the plane up, and it comes mostly from the wings. Notice that the forces come in opposite pairs: lift against weight, and thrust against drag. When a plane flies straight and level at a steady speed, each pair is perfectly balanced. To climb, a pilot needs more lift than weight. To speed up, the engines must make more thrust than drag.

So how does a wing make lift? Look at a wing from the side and you will see a special shape called an airfoil: a rounded front edge, a curved top, and a thin back edge. The wing is also tilted slightly upward into the oncoming air. As the wing moves, it deflects a great mass of air downward. Push air down, and the air pushes the wing up, just as a swimmer pushes water backwards to move forward. At the same time, air flowing over the curved top speeds up. A Swiss scientist named Daniel Bernoulli discovered in the 1700s that fast-moving air exerts less pressure than slow-moving air. So the pressure on top of the wing drops, while the slower air underneath keeps pushing up harder. Many books once claimed that the air over the top must "catch up" with the air underneath, so that both reach the back edge at the same moment. That is a misconception. Wind tunnel tests show that the air over the top actually arrives at the back edge first, not at the same time.

The tilt of the wing into the oncoming air is called the angle of attack. Raise the nose a little, and the angle of attack grows, which creates more lift. But there is a limit. If the angle gets too steep, usually around fifteen degrees, the air can no longer follow the curved top of the wing. It breaks away into messy swirls, and the lift suddenly collapses. Pilots call this a stall, and it has nothing to do with the engine stopping. To recover, a pilot lowers the nose so that air flows smoothly over the wing again.

For centuries, people strapped on feathered wings and leapt from towers, and nearly all of them crashed. The first people to truly solve the problem were two brothers who ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio: Wilbur and Orville Wright. Bicycles taught them that a rider must constantly balance and steer, and they realized that a flying machine would need the same kind of control. By watching birds, they noticed that a bird twists the tips of its wings to roll and turn. So they built gliders whose wings could be twisted with cables, an idea they called wing warping. When their test gliders did not lift as much as published tables predicted, the brothers built their own small wind tunnel in 1901 and tested about two hundred tiny model wings. No company could sell them an engine light enough, so their mechanic, Charlie Taylor, helped them build one. On December 17, 1903, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville flew for 12 seconds and 36.6 metres. It was the first sustained, controlled, powered flight with a pilot on board. By the fourth flight that day, Wilbur stayed up for 59 seconds. Years of crashes, broken parts, and patient testing had paid off, a true lesson in perseverance.

In Canada, airplanes soon became more than an exciting invention. Canada is enormous, and in the 1920s many northern communities, mines, and trading posts had no roads at all. Bush pilots began delivering mail, medicine, food, and people to these remote places. Their planes landed on floats on lakes in summer and on skis on frozen lakes in winter. In 1947, the company de Havilland Canada, near Toronto, flew the first prototype of the DHC-2 Beaver. Before designing it, the engineers asked bush pilots what they really needed: a rugged plane that could take off from a short lake, carry heavy loads, and keep working in bitter cold. About 1600 Beavers were built, and many are still flying today, more than seventy-five years later, on floats along the coast of British Columbia and on skis in the Arctic.

From a 12-second hop over the sand to jets that cross oceans overnight, every flight still depends on lift, weight, thrust, and drag. The next time you fold a paper airplane and send it across the room, you are running the same experiment the Wright brothers did, one careful test at a time.
