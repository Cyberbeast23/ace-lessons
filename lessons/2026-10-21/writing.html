✍️ Writing Week 24: Procedural & Explanatory Writing: How to Build and Test a Paper Airplane ✈️📐

Yesterday you wrote a compare-and-contrast essay that made a judgment. Today, Ace, you'll write like an engineer writing a test manual: a procedural and explanatory text. Procedural writing tells a reader exactly how to do something, step by step, so clearly that a stranger could follow it without asking a single question. Explanatory writing tells why it works. Today you'll do both: teach your reader how to build a paper airplane, how to test it fairly, and the science of why it flies.

Part 1: The Lesson

A procedural + explanatory text has six parts 🧩

Think of the instructions that come with a model kit or a recipe card, plus a short "how it works" section at the end.

1. Goal (introduction): a title and 1 to 2 sentences that tell the reader what they will make and why it's worth doing
2. Materials: a complete list of everything needed, with exact sizes and amounts
3. Steps: numbered, in order, one main action per step, each starting with a command verb
4. Test: how to try it out fairly and measure the results
5. The science: an explanation of why it works, using cause-and-effect words
6. Tips + conclusion: troubleshooting ("If it… then…") and a final encouraging thought

Five technical-writer's moves

  • Start steps with command verbs (imperative mood): Fold, Crease, Line up, Measure, Launch, Record. Not "You should maybe fold…" Just: "Fold the paper in half."
  • Be precise: give exact measurements and positions in metric units. "Fold the top corners down to the centre crease" beats "fold the top bit." Write numbers with units like 2 cm and 1.5 m (a space, and no period after the unit).
  • Use sequence words to guide the reader: first, next, then, after that, once, finally. Put a comma after a sequence word or phrase that starts a sentence: "Next, flip the plane over."
  • Plan a fair test: change only one variable at a time (like the wing shape), keep everything else the same (the same paper, the same launch height, the same throw), repeat each trial at least three times, and calculate the mean.
  • Explain with cause and effect: use because, so, as a result, this causes, which means to connect each design feature to one of the four forces (lift, weight, thrust, drag).

Grown-up words: a procedure is a set of steps; a command like "Fold the paper" is an imperative sentence. The thing you change in a test is the independent variable, the thing you measure is the dependent variable, and everything you keep the same is a controlled variable.

Model Procedural + Explanatory Text (short version)

Goal + materialsStepsTest + scienceTips + conclusion

The Classic Dart: How to Build and Test a Paper Airplane. In ten minutes, you can build a paper dart that flies more than 8 m and learn the science of flight at the same time. You will need one sheet of A4 or letter-size paper, a ruler, a metre tape, a pencil, and a notebook.
1. Fold the paper in half the long way, crease it sharply, and unfold it. 2. Fold the two top corners down so that their edges meet at the centre crease. 3. Fold the new slanted edges to the centre crease again to make a sharp point. 4. Fold the plane in half along the first crease. 5. Fold each side down to make a wing, lining up the top edge of the wing with the bottom of the body.
To test your dart fairly, stand behind a tape line and launch it at shoulder height with a gentle, level push. Measure from the line to where the nose first touches the floor, then repeat for three trials and find the mean. Next, change only one thing, such as bending the back edges of the wings up by 2 mm, and run three more trials. Your throw provides the thrust, and the air pushing back causes drag. Because the wings are tilted slightly into the oncoming air, they deflect air downward, which creates lift to fight the plane's weight.
If your dart dives straight down, bend the back edges of the wings up a little. If it climbs and then stalls, bend them down. Every crease is an experiment, so keep testing, keep recording, and keep improving.

Notice: the title and goal tell exactly what you'll make; the materials are complete and specific; every step starts with a command verb and gives an exact position; the test changes only one variable and repeats three trials; the science uses cause-and-effect words (because, which creates, causes) and names the four forces; and the tips use "If… then…" troubleshooting.
