✈️ Math Week 24: Flight: How Airplanes Fly Lab

Ace, every airplane in the sky is a flying math problem. Pilots, engineers, and air traffic controllers use numbers every second to keep planes safe: How fast are we going? How far can we glide? How cold is it up here? Today you'll work like an aerospace engineer with speed, distance, and time; converting between km/h and m/s; headwinds and tailwinds; glide ratios and scale models; negative temperatures at high altitude; reading a line graph and finding rates; means (averages) and a missing value; angle of attack; and multi-step fuel planning.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your pilot's pre-flight checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Speed, distance, and time

Speed = distance ÷ time. Rearrange it when you need something else: distance = speed × time and time = distance ÷ speed.

A floatplane flies 450 km in 2.5 hours. Its average speed is 450 ÷ 2.5 = 180 km/h. At that speed, 4 hours would take it 180 × 4 = 720 km.

Worked Example 2: km/h and m/s

1 km = 1000 m and 1 h = 3600 s, so 1 km/h = 1000 ÷ 3600 m/s. That's the same as dividing by 3.6.

km/h → m/s: divide by 3.6. 72 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s.
m/s → km/h: multiply by 3.6. 15 m/s × 3.6 = 54 km/h.

Worked Example 3: Headwinds and tailwinds

A plane's airspeed is how fast it moves through the air. Its ground speed is how fast it moves over the ground. Wind changes the ground speed.

Headwind (blowing against you): subtract. Tailwind (blowing from behind): add. Airspeed 500 km/h with a 50 km/h headwind gives a ground speed of 450 km/h, so 900 km takes 900 ÷ 450 = 2 h.

Worked Example 4: Glide ratios and scale

A glide ratio compares how far forward something glides to how far it drops. A glide ratio of 20 : 1 means it travels 20 m forward for every 1 m it sinks. From 2 km up, it could glide 20 × 2 = 40 km.

Glide ratio = distance forward ÷ height dropped. A paper plane that drops 2 m while gliding 12 m has a glide ratio of 12 ÷ 2 = 6 : 1.

Scale models: on a 1 : 50 model, every real length is divided by 50. A real wing 10 m (1000 cm) long becomes 1000 ÷ 50 = 20 cm.

Worked Example 5: Negative temperatures with altitude

In the lower atmosphere, the air gets colder as you go up, by about 6.5 °C for every 1000 m (on average).

If it is 20 °C on the ground, at 4000 m it is about 20 − 6.5 × 4 = 20 − 26 = −6 °C. Going below zero is just like moving left past 0 on a number line. (Type negative answers with a minus sign, like -6.)

Worked Example 6: Rates from a line graph

On a graph of altitude against time, a steeper line means the plane is climbing faster. A flat line means the altitude is not changing.

Rate = change in altitude ÷ change in time. If a plane goes from 1000 m at 2 min to 3400 m at 8 min, its rate of climb is (3400 − 1000) ÷ (8 − 2) = 2400 ÷ 6 = 400 m per minute.

Worked Example 7: Angles, averages, and the four forces

Four forces act on every airplane: lift (up), weight (down), thrust (forward), and drag (backward). In steady, level flight, lift balances weight and thrust balances drag.

The angle of attack is the angle between the wing and the oncoming air. If the wing is tilted up 10° from horizontal but the plane is climbing along a path 6° above horizontal, the air meets the wing at 10 − 6 = 4°.

Mean (average) = total ÷ number of values. To find a missing value when you know the new mean: new total = new mean × new count, then subtract the old total.
