✍️ Writing Week 42: Museum Exhibit Labels — Longships & Legends 🏛️⛵

Yesterday you wrote a myth-vs-science essay. Today, Ace, you'll try a brand-new form: a museum exhibit label set. When you walk through a museum, the small cards beside each object are written by real writers called curators and interpretive writers. They have a tough job: explain a big idea in very few words, so that a busy visitor reads the label in about 30 seconds and walks away amazed — and accurate. Today you are the junior curator for an imaginary exhibit called Longships & Legends: The Norse in North America at a Canadian history museum.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six labels in a great exhibit set 🏛️

An exhibit label set is a group of short, connected texts. Each label has one job. Together they tell one clear story.

1. Exhibit title + intro panel: a catchy title and 2–3 sentences giving the big idea
2. Object label: name of the object, what it is made of, where and when it was found, and why it matters
3. "How do we know?" label: the evidence — digging, dating, tree rings, sagas
4. Myth-buster label: names a popular myth and corrects it with evidence
5. People & perspectives label: who else was there; respectful and fair to everyone
6. Visitor question: invites the reader to think, look closely, or imagine

Five rules real curators follow

  • Short and strong: most labels are 25–60 words. Every word must earn its place.
  • Lead with the wow: put the most interesting fact in the first sentence.
  • Be accurate: use real dates, places and materials. If experts are not sure, say so ("may have," "historians think").
  • Talk to the visitor: use concrete details they can see ("Look for the tiny hole in the centre").
  • Keep your tenses steady: tell what happened long ago in the past tense; describe the object in front of you in the present tense. Don't switch back and forth inside one sentence.

Curator's rule: a visitor who reads only your titles and first sentences should still learn the big idea.

Mini-lesson: past-tense consistency ⏳

History writing mostly uses the past tense: sailed, built, found, traded. A common mistake is slipping into the present tense by accident.

❌ The Norse sailed west and build a camp near the shore.
✅ The Norse sailed west and built a camp near the shore.

Watch the tricky irregular verbs: build → built, find → found, sink → sank, steer → steered, ride → rode, bring → brought, leave → left, keep → kept.

It is fine to use the present tense for the object that is still here today: "This rivet is made of iron. A Norse shipbuilder hammered it about 1 000 years ago."

Model Label Set (short version)

Title + introObject + evidenceMyth-busterPeople + visitor question

LONGSHIPS & LEGENDS
About 1 000 years ago, Norse sailors crossed the North Atlantic in open wooden ships. Their journey ended here, in Newfoundland.
Iron Boat Rivet · iron · found at L'Anse aux Meadows, 1960s
This small rivet once held a longship's oak planks together. Rivets like this were made by Norse smiths, so they help prove that Norse ships were repaired here.
How do we know when? In 2021, scientists counted tree rings in wood cut with metal tools. The trees were cut in 1021 CE.
Myth-buster: Horned helmets?
No! No Viking-era horned helmet has ever been found. The idea came from opera costumes in the 1800s.
Who was already here? Indigenous peoples had lived in this region for thousands of years before the Norse arrived. The sagas describe both trade and conflict.
Your turn: If you crossed the ocean with no compass, what would you watch to find your way?

Notice: the title is punchy; each label does one job; dates and materials are exact; the myth-buster gives evidence; history is told in the past tense while the object "is" here today.

Goal: 200 to 320 words in total, split into six labels. Each label gets a bold-style heading (type it in CAPITALS) and is about 25 to 60 words. Include at least three exact facts (a date, a place, a material, or a measurement), one "may have" or "historians think" for something experts are unsure about, and a visitor question at the end. Tell the history in the past tense. Bonus: use two words from today's spelling list (like settlement or archaeological) correctly!
