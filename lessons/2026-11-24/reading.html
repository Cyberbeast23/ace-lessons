📖 Reading Week 58: Masters of the Mesozoic Skies: The Puzzle of the Pterosaurs

Before you read, Ace: Yesterday you figured out an author's purpose and tone. Today's skill is one that strong readers use without even noticing: asking questions while you read, and knowing where the answer lives. Some answers are sitting right in one sentence, some have to be pieced together, some need your own thinking, and some questions can't be answered by anyone yet! Reading teachers call these four kinds of Question-Answer Relationships:
  • 1. Right There: the answer is stated in one place in the text, often using the same words as the question. (Point to the sentence!)
  • 2. Think and Search: the answer is in the text, but in more than one place. You have to gather pieces from different paragraphs, a table, or both texts, and put them together.
  • 3. Author and Me: the answer is not written down. You combine clues from the text with what you already know to make an inference.
  • 4. On My Own: you can answer from your own knowledge or experience, even without the text. These questions help you connect the topic to your life.
  • 5. Still-open questions: in science writing, watch for signal phrases such as no one knows, scientists still debate, may have, one idea is. They tell you the question isn't settled yet, so the best answer is "Here's what scientists think so far, and why."
  • Word detective: Greek pteron = "wing" + sauros = "lizard," so a pterosaur is a "wing lizard" (and a helicopter is a "spiral wing": helix + pteron!) · Greek daktylos = "finger," so pterodactyl = "wing finger" · Greek mesos = "middle" + zoion = "animal," so the Mesozoic is the "middle age of animals" · Latin quattuor = "four" + pes = "foot," so quadrupedal = "walking on four feet"
  • Words in yellow are vocabulary words. Use the sentence around them (the context) to figure out what they mean.

Text 1: Masters of the Mesozoic Skies: The Puzzle of the Pterosaurs

Picture yourself on the shore of a warm, shallow sea about 85 million years ago. Back then, a long ocean split North America in two, from the Arctic all the way to the Gulf of Mexico. A shadow sweeps across the water. You look up and see a creature with wings wider than a minivan is long, a long toothless beak and a bony crest pointing backward from its head. It barely flaps. It simply glides, rides the wind and then swoops down to snatch a fish. This is Pteranodon, and it is not a bird, not a bat and not a dinosaur. It is a pterosaur.

The first flyers with backbones. Pterosaurs were reptiles that lived from about 228 million years ago until 66 million years ago. They were close cousins of the dinosaurs, but they formed their own separate branch of the reptile family tree. Insects had already been flying for more than 100 million years, but pterosaurs were the first animals with backbones to evolve powered flight, which means flapping to climb and stay aloft instead of only gliding downward. They took to the air more than 70 million years before the first birds appeared.

A wing built on one finger. A bird's wing is made of feathers attached to its arm. A bat's wing is skin stretched between several long fingers. A pterosaur did something completely different. Its wing was a membrane of skin and muscle, stiffened by thin fibres, that stretched from the tip of one hugely elongated fourth finger back to the animal's legs. That single finger could be longer than the rest of the arm. The other three fingers stayed short, with small claws that were probably used for climbing and grabbing.

Built light, but tough. Many pterosaur bones were hollow, with walls sometimes only a few millimetres thick, and they were connected to air sacs linked to the lungs, much like a bird's. Thin-walled tubes are light, but they are also surprisingly robust, which is why bike frames are built the same way. Many pterosaurs were also covered in short, hair-like fuzz called pycnofibres, which may have helped keep them warm.

The take-off puzzle. How does an animal as tall as a giraffe get airborne? For a long time, many scientists thought giant pterosaurs must have jumped off cliffs or run into the wind like an albatross. Today, one of the most plausible ideas is the quad launch. Pterosaurs walked on all four limbs, and their arms were far stronger than their legs. According to this idea, a pterosaur crouched, then pushed off powerfully with its arms, vaulting into the air like a pole-vaulter, and began flapping. Computer models and studies of bone strength support the idea, but scientists are still testing exactly how it worked for the very largest species.

Babies of the sky. In 2017, scientists in China described more than 200 eggs of a pterosaur called Hamipterus, found together at one site. The eggs had soft, leathery shells, more like many lizard eggs than hard bird eggs. Some researchers think pterosaur hatchlings could fly within days of hatching and looked after themselves. Others think the young needed care from their parents for a while. Whether the babies could fly so early is still debated, and there is no consensus yet.

Why fossils are so rare. Pterosaur fossils are scarce compared with dinosaur bones. Those light, hollow bones were easily crushed or scattered after an animal died. The best fossils formed when a body sank quickly into soft, fine mud at the bottom of a calm sea or lake, where it was buried before it could fall apart. Then, 66 million years ago, the same disaster that wiped out the non-bird dinosaurs ended the age of the pterosaurs. Why some birds survived it and every pterosaur did not is a question scientists are still trying to answer.

Text 2: The Frozen Dragon of Alberta

In 1992, a fossil crew working in Dinosaur Provincial Park, in southern Alberta, dug up part of a skeleton of a very large pterosaur, including a big bone from its neck. Its rocks were about 77 million years old. Because the bones looked a lot like those of the giant Quetzalcoatlus from Texas, many people assumed it was the same kind of animal, or a close relative.

Almost thirty years later, a team of scientists studied the Alberta bones carefully and compared them with those of other giant pterosaurs. They found small but important differences in the shape of the neck bones. In 2019, they announced that it was a new species, and they named it Cryodrakon boreas, which means "frozen dragon of the north wind." (Alberta was actually warm when it lived there. The name is a nod to Canada's chilly weather today!)

How big was it? The scientists estimated that a large adult may have had a wingspan of about 10 m, but that estimate comes from comparing a few bones with those of its relatives, not from a complete skeleton. Like its cousins, Cryodrakon probably stalked across open land on all four limbs, picking up lizards, small mammals and even baby dinosaurs, a bit like a gigantic stork. Only a handful of its bones have been found, so palaeontologists are hoping the badlands of Alberta will give up more.
