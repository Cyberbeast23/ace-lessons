Today, Ace, you're the writer for a nature documentary, the kind where the camera follows a wild animal while a calm voice tells you what's happening and why it matters. Of course, no camera has ever filmed a living pterosaur, so documentary teams use fossils, science and computer animation to bring them back. Your job is to write the words the narrator speaks and the notes that tell the animators and sound team what to show and play. Today's genre is the nature documentary narration script.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong narration script 🎬

A documentary scene is usually 1 to 3 minutes long, so the script is short and organized into numbered scenes. Each scene mixes three kinds of lines.

1. Title and cold open: the title, then a short, gripping first line that hooks the viewer before anything is explained ("Eighty-five million years ago, the sky had teeth.")
2. Visual direction: lines starting with VISUAL: that tell the animators what the viewer sees (VISUAL: Wide shot of a calm, green sea at sunrise.)
3. Sound cue: lines starting with SOUND: for music, animal calls and effects (SOUND: Waves. Wind rushing past wings.)
4. Narration with a science fact: lines starting with NARRATOR: that add what the camera can't show: facts, numbers and reasons
5. Turning point: a moment of tension or change (a hunt, a storm, a danger) that keeps viewers watching
6. Closing line: a final narrator line that connects the scene to a big idea or to us today

Five habits of great documentary writers

  • Don't narrate the picture: if the viewer can see the pterosaur flying, don't say "The pterosaur is flying." Add something new: "It hasn't flapped once in ten minutes. It's riding a column of warm, rising air."
  • Write for the ear: the narrator reads your words out loud, so use short, clear sentences. Read each line aloud. If you run out of breath, split it.
  • Stay in the present tense: documentaries make the past feel live: it glides, it dives, it swallows, not it glided.
  • Choose vivid, exact verbs: ❌ "It goes down really fast." ✅ "It plummets." One strong verb beats a weak verb plus an adverb.
  • Be honest about what scientists know: real fossils tell us some things for sure, but other details are educated guesses. Use phrases like scientists think, may have and probably when the science isn't settled.

Narrator's rule: for every NARRATOR line, ask, "Would the viewer learn something they couldn't see?" If not, cut it or add a fact.

Mini-lesson: grammar for scripts that sound great aloud 🎙️

Consistent present tense: don't slide between tenses. ❌ The Pteranodon spots a fish and dived. ✅ The Pteranodon spots a fish and dives.

Appositives with commas: an appositive is a noun phrase that renames the noun beside it. Put commas on both sides when it's in the middle of a sentence: Quetzalcoatlus, a pterosaur as tall as a giraffe, stalks across the plain.

Its or it's? its = belonging to it (its wing); it's = it is (it's hungry). Test it: if you can say "it is," use it's.

Colons for script labels: each speaker or cue label ends with a colon: NARRATOR: VISUAL: SOUND:

Canadian spelling: metre, kilometre, centre, colour, manoeuvre. A pterosaur can manoeuvre over the centre of a sea, 10 metres from wingtip to wingtip!

Model script: "Wings over the Inland Sea" (about 230 words)

WINGS OVER THE INLAND SEA
SCENE 1
NARRATOR: Eighty-five million years ago, the sky had a hunter, and it wasn't a bird.

VISUAL: Wide shot of a calm, green sea at sunrise. A dark shape crosses the sun.
SOUND: Soft waves. Wind rushing past stretched skin.

NARRATOR: This is Pteranodon, a flying reptile with wings more than six metres across. It hasn't flapped in minutes. Warm air rising from the water holds it aloft, so it saves its energy for the hunt.
VISUAL: Close-up of the wing. Light glows through the thin membrane.
NARRATOR: Its wing hangs from a single finger, longer than your whole arm.
SCENE 2
VISUAL: The Pteranodon tilts its head. Below, a school of silver fish flashes.
SOUND: Music builds. A sharp splash.
NARRATOR: It folds its wings and plummets. Then, at the last second, it skims the surface and snatches a fish in its toothless beak. But the splash has been noticed. A huge marine reptile, a mosasaur, is rising from the deep.
SOUND: A low rumble.
NARRATOR: Scientists think Pteranodon could take off from the water by pushing with its powerful arms. Right now, it had better be quick.
VISUAL: The pterosaur bursts upward as jaws snap shut below.

NARRATOR: For 160 million years, pterosaurs ruled the skies. Today, the only flyers left with backbones are birds and bats, but the first to fly were these wing lizards.

Notice: the cold open hooks you before it explains anything. The NARRATOR lines never just describe the picture; they add facts the camera can't show (six metres, one finger, rising warm air). The script stays in the present tense and uses vivid verbs (plummets, skims, snatches, bursts). The appositive a mosasaur has commas on both sides. "Scientists think" shows honestly that the take-off idea is a hypothesis. Look for its wing (belonging to it) and the Canadian spelling metres.
