Pedal Power: How the Bicycle Was Invented, and Reinvented

Look at any bicycle and you will see two wheels, a chain, some gears, a seat and a pair of handlebars. It seems so simple that you might think it was invented all at once. In fact, the bicycle took about seventy years of clever ideas, painful crashes and many small improvements before it looked like the bike you ride today.

The story begins in Germany in 1817. An inventor named Karl Drais built a wooden "running machine" with two wheels in a line and a seat in the middle. It had no pedals at all. Riders sat on it and pushed along the ground with their feet, a bit like the balance bikes little kids use today. The running machine was heavy and cumbersome, but it proved something surprising: a person could balance on two wheels, as long as they kept moving forward.

In the 1860s, inventors in France attached pedals directly to the front wheel. Now riders could keep their feet off the ground. These machines were called velocipedes, but most people called them "boneshakers." Their wooden wheels had iron rims, and riding one over a bumpy cobblestone street rattled every bone in your body.

Because the pedals were fixed to the front wheel, one turn of the pedals meant exactly one turn of that wheel. Designers soon realized that a bigger wheel would carry the rider farther with every turn. By the 1870s, the front wheels of "high-wheelers," also called penny-farthings, could be about one and a half metres tall, with a tiny wheel at the back. They were fast, but the rider sat in a precarious spot high above the ground. If the front wheel hit a rock, the rider could fly over the handlebars and land head first. This kind of crash was so common it had its own name: a "header."

The solution came in 1885, when an English inventor named John Kemp Starley built the Rover "safety bicycle." It had two wheels of about the same size, and the pedals turned a gear that pulled a chain connected to the back wheel. The chain changed everything. By using a big gear at the front and a small gear at the back, a normal-sized wheel could spin several times for every turn of the pedals. Riders could go fast without sitting on a giant wheel. Three years later, a Scottish inventor living in Ireland, John Boyd Dunlop, made air-filled rubber tires for his son's tricycle. These pneumatic tires soaked up the bumps, and the boneshaker's rattle disappeared.

The shape of the frame was refined too. Most bikes today have a "diamond frame," which is really two triangles joined together. Engineers love triangles because a triangle is rigid: if you push on one corner, it cannot squash into a different shape the way a square can. That is why a bike frame can be light and still hold up a rider bouncing down a gravel trail.

By the 1890s, a bicycle craze had swept across Europe and North America. For the first time, ordinary working people could travel many kilometres without owning a horse. Bicycles were especially liberating for women, who could now ride to school, to work or to visit friends on their own. Many women traded long, heavy skirts for clothing that was easier to pedal in.

The bicycle is still being reinvented. Gears that let riders shift between easy and hard pedalling, light frames made of aluminium and carbon fibre, and electric motors on e-bikes have all come since. Today, many Canadian cities are building protected bike lanes because cycling is quiet, healthy and sustainable: a bike burns no fuel and gives off no exhaust. More than two hundred years after Karl Drais pushed his running machine down a German road, people are still finding new ways to improve one of the most useful machines ever made.
