🚲 Math Week 38: Pedal Power: Bicycle Engineer's Math Lab

Ace, a bicycle looks simple, but it is one of the cleverest machines ever invented. Every bike is full of math: the circumference of the wheels decides how far you roll with each turn, the gears are ratios that trade speed for power, and engineers who plan bike lanes use rates, averages and percents every day. Today you are a bicycle engineer in the imaginary town of Spokeville, using circumference with π, gear ratios, cadence and speed, distance-speed-time, discounts plus tax, a missing value for a mean, percent grade of a hill, millilitres to litres, the mean from a table, comparing rates on a graph, two-step equations, the lowest common multiple, and stopping distance.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your bicycle engineer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (cm, m, km, s, min, h, mL, L, $).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change minutes to hours or cm to m before you calculate.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A kid on a bike might go 20 km/h, but not 2000 km/h!

Worked Example 1: Circumference of a wheel

The distance around a circle is its circumference: C = π × d, where d is the diameter (the distance straight across through the centre). We'll use π ≈ 3.14.

A wheel 50 cm across: C = 3.14 × 50 = 157 cm. Each full turn of the wheel rolls the bike forward 157 cm.

Worked Example 2: Gear ratios

The chain links the front gear (the chainring) to a rear gear (the cog). Gear ratio = front teeth ÷ rear teeth tells how many times the back wheel turns for each full turn of the pedals.

Front 36 teeth, rear 12 teeth: 36 ÷ 12 = 3, so one pedal turn spins the wheel 3 times. A big ratio is fast but hard to push; a small ratio is easy for climbing hills.

Worked Example 3: Distance = speed × time

If you know two of distance, speed, time, you can find the third: time = distance ÷ speed. Watch the units: km/h needs hours.

10 km at 20 km/h: 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5 h = 30 minutes.

Worked Example 4: Discount, then tax

Take the discount off first, then add the tax to the sale price. 20% off means you pay 80% (× 0.8). 5% GST means you pay 105% (× 1.05).

A $100 helmet at 20% off: 100 × 0.8 = $80. Add 5% GST: 80 × 1.05 = $84.

Worked Example 5: The missing value for a mean

Mean × number of values = total. Find the total you need, then subtract what you already have.

To average 10 km over 3 rides after riding 8 km and 9 km: total needed = 10 × 3 = 30, so ride 3 must be 30 − 17 = 13 km.

Worked Example 6: Percent grade of a hill

Road signs show steepness as a grade: rise ÷ run × 100, where the rise is how far you climb up and the run is how far you go across.

A hill climbs 20 m over 400 m: 20 ÷ 400 × 100 = 5% grade.

Worked Example 7: Lowest common multiple (LCM)

When two things repeat on different schedules, they line up again at the LCM: the smallest number both divide into evenly. List multiples until you find a match.

Every 4 min and every 10 min: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 … and 10, 20 … so they line up after 20 minutes.

Worked Example 8: Kilometres per hour to metres per second

Yesterday you multiplied m/s by 3.6 to get km/h. To go the other way, divide km/h by 3.6.

36 km/h ÷ 3.6 = 10 m/s: you travel 10 metres every second!

Worked Example 9: Two-step equations

A bike-share costs a $2 unlock fee plus $1.50 per hour. A ride costs $8: 2 + 1.5h = 8 → 1.5h = 6 → h = 4 hours. Undo the adding first, then undo the multiplying.
