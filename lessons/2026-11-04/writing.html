✍️ Writing Week 38: Balanced Discussion: Should Maple Street Get a Protected Bike Lane? 🚲⚖️

Yesterday you wrote a brochure that tried to win readers over. Today, Ace, you'll do something harder: write like a fair-minded reporter or judge. A balanced discussion looks at a real question from both sides, gives the best reasons and evidence for each side, and only then reaches a careful conclusion. Today's question comes from the town of Spokeville: should the town turn one car lane on busy Maple Street into a protected bike lane?

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a balanced discussion ⚖️

A persuasive piece shows only one side. A balanced discussion is different: a reader should finish it understanding why reasonable people disagree.

1. Title as a question: the issue, written so that it could be answered either way ("Should Maple Street Get a Protected Bike Lane?")
2. Introduction: explain the issue, give background, and say that people disagree (don't give your opinion yet!)
3. Arguments for: the strongest reasons on the "yes" side, each with evidence
4. Arguments against: the strongest reasons on the "no" side, each with evidence, explained just as fairly
5. Weighing up: compare the two sides: which reasons matter most, and could a compromise solve some problems?
6. Conclusion: your own careful judgement, with a reason, written in a calm voice

Five moves of a fair writer

  • Use neutral words: "some residents believe" instead of "only silly people think."
  • Use contrast transitions: however, on the other hand, in contrast, although, despite this.
  • Back up every reason: a number, an example or an expert ("a bike counter recorded 375 riders a day").
  • Give each side a fair share: about the same number of reasons and the same amount of space.
  • Write in the third person in the body (people, residents, cyclists, drivers). Save "I think" for the conclusion.

Fair-writer rule: describe the other side so well that someone who agrees with it would say, "Yes, that's exactly what I mean."

Model Discussion (short version): "Should Spokeville Build a Bike Path Along the River?"

Should Spokeville Build a Bike Path Along the River?
Spokeville's town council is deciding whether to spend $2 million on a paved bike path beside the Cedar River. Residents have strong feelings on both sides.
Supporters argue that a separate path would keep young riders away from traffic. They also point out that cycling is healthy and produces no exhaust, so more riders could mean cleaner air.
On the other hand, some residents worry about the cost. They argue that $2 million could repair older roads or fund the library instead. Others are concerned that paving the riverbank could disturb nesting birds.
Both sides make reasonable points. However, a compromise might work: a narrower gravel path would cost less and protect more of the riverbank. In my judgement, the town should build the path, because the safety of young cyclists matters most, but it should be designed to protect the birds.

Notice: the title is a question; the introduction explains the issue without taking a side; each side gets two reasons; the writer uses on the other hand and however; words are neutral (supporters argue, some residents worry); and the opinion comes only at the end, with a reason and a compromise.
