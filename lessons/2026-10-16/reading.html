Frozen Giants: British Columbia in the Ice Age

Imagine standing in southern British Columbia about 20 000 years ago. Instead of green forests and rushing rivers, you would see a blinding white plain of ice stretching to the horizon, with only the sharpest mountain peaks poking through. This was the Cordilleran Ice Sheet, and it covered nearly all of BC. In places it was more than two kilometres thick. An ice sheet is like a glacier the size of a country. A glacier is a huge mass of ice that forms when snow piles up for many years, gets pressed into ice, and slowly flows downhill under its own weight. Over the past two and a half million years, a time scientists call the Pleistocene, Earth swung back and forth between long cold glacial periods and shorter warm ones.

Glaciers are powerful sculptors. As the ice crept forward, rocks frozen into its base scraped the land like giant sandpaper. It carved deep U-shaped valleys and fjords such as Howe Sound, and it ground solid rock into gravel, sand, and fine "rock flour." When the ice finally melted, it left this sediment behind in thick layers, which you can still see today in many BC river banks and cliffs. So much of the world's water was locked up in ice that the oceans were about 120 metres lower than today. A wide strip of dry land, called Beringia, joined Siberia to Alaska. Herds of animals made long migration journeys across it, and many scientists think some of the first people to reach the Americas came this way too.

North and west of the great ice sheet, in Yukon, Alaska, and Beringia, lay a cold, dry tundra: a treeless land of grasses, low shrubs, and tough little flowers. Beneath it was permafrost, ground that stays frozen all year long. This chilly land was home to Ice Age megafauna, which means "giant animals." The most famous was the woolly mammoth, about as tall as an African elephant. Its shaggy coat could be almost a metre long, and a thick layer of fat lay under its skin. It had small ears and a short tail, which lost less heat than an elephant's big ears do. Each of these features was an adaptation that helped it survive the bitter cold. Its long, curving tusk was actually an enormous front tooth, useful for sweeping snow off the grass and for battling rivals.

Mammoths had company. Mastodons were shorter, stockier relatives that lived mostly in forests and wetlands. Their teeth had cone-shaped bumps for crunching twigs and leaves, while mammoth teeth had flat ridges for grinding grass. Giant ground sloths, some weighing nearly a tonne, used long claws to pull down branches. Hunting many of these plant-eaters was the sabre-toothed cat, whose two upper fangs could grow about 18 centimetres long.

How do we know all this? The permafrost is a natural freezer. In 2022, gold miners in Yukon's Klondike uncovered a baby woolly mammoth, about 30 000 years old, so well preserved that it still had skin and hair. Elders of the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in named her Nun cho ga, meaning "big baby animal" in the Hän language. Scientists also use radiocarbon dating: after a living thing dies, the carbon-14 in its body slowly disappears, halving about every 5730 years, so measuring what is left tells its age. Ice cores, long tubes of ice drilled from glaciers, hold yearly snow layers and tiny bubbles of ancient air, which reveal what the climate was like long ago.

Then, between about 13 000 and 11 000 years ago, the ice melted and most of North America's megafauna vanished. Why? One theory blames climate change: as the world warmed, grasslands turned into forests and wetlands, and the grazers lost their food. Another theory blames people, who had arrived with stone-tipped spears; animals that raise only one baby every few years cannot easily replace those that are hunted. Many scientists now think both causes worked together. A few woolly mammoths survived on Wrangel Island, off Siberia, until about 4000 years ago, long after Egypt's Great Pyramid was built. Today BC's remaining glaciers are shrinking again, so studying the last Ice Age helps us understand how living things respond when the climate changes.
