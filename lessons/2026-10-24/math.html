🎬 Math Week 27: Animation Studio Math Lab

Ace, every cartoon you have ever watched is really thousands of still pictures shown so fast that your brain sees them move. Animators are artists, but they are also mathematicians: How many drawings does a scene need? How long will it take to make? How do you make a ball slow down smoothly, or blow up a drawing for a poster without squishing it? Today you'll run your own animation studio with frame rates, big multi-step calculations, percent, the least common multiple, aspect ratios, scale factors (and what they do to area), translations on a coordinate grid, number patterns, slow motion, fractions of a group, and an equation.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your studio checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match (seconds with seconds, minutes with minutes)?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Frame rates

Movies show pictures called frames. Most films play at 24 frames per second (24 fps). That's a rate, so multiply by the time: 3 seconds × 24 fps = 72 frames. Watch the units: change minutes to seconds first (1 min = 60 s).

Animators often work "on twos": each drawing is shown for 2 frames in a row. Then 72 frames need only 72 ÷ 2 = 36 drawings.

Worked Example 2: Least common multiple (LCM)

A flag in the background flaps every 4 frames and a character taps her foot every 6 frames. When do both actions restart together? List multiples: 4, 8, 12, 16... and 6, 12, 18... The first number on both lists is the LCM = 12, so they line up again at frame 12.

Worked Example 3: Aspect ratio

A screen's aspect ratio compares width to height. An old TV is 4 : 3. If its picture is 600 pixels tall, then 600 ÷ 3 = 200 per part, and the width is 4 × 200 = 800 pixels.

Worked Example 4: Scale factors, length and area

To enlarge a drawing by a scale factor of 2, multiply every length by 2. A 4 cm × 3 cm sketch becomes 8 cm × 6 cm.

Careful: the area does not just double! The old area was 4 × 3 = 12 cm². The new area is 8 × 6 = 48 cm², which is 2 × 2 = 4 times as big. Scale the lengths first, then find the area.

Worked Example 5: Translations on a coordinate grid

A point (x, y) tells you x across, then y up. A translation slides a shape without turning or resizing it. To slide from (1, 2) to (4, 7), the shape moves 3 right (4 − 1) and 5 up (7 − 2). Every other point of the shape slides the same amount: (6, 0) moves to (9, 5).

Worked Example 6: Patterns and slow motion

To make a ball slow down (animators call it easing), the gaps between drawings get smaller in a pattern, like 10 mm, 8 mm, 6 mm, 4 mm, 2 mm. The total distance is the sum: 10 + 8 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 30 mm. Shortcut: pair the first and last (10 + 2 = 12), and you have 5 numbers, so 12 × 5 ÷ 2 = 30.

Slow motion: a camera that films 48 fps but plays back at 24 fps makes everything last 48 ÷ 24 = 2 times as long.

Worked Example 7: Fractions of the rest, and equations

"2/5 of 30 panels are close-ups, and half of the rest are wide shots." Close-ups: 30 ÷ 5 × 2 = 12. The rest: 30 − 12 = 18. Wide shots: half of 18 = 9. Always find "the rest" first!

Solving an equation: 5n + 30 = 80. Undo each step in reverse: subtract 30 (5n = 50), then divide by 5 (n = 10).
