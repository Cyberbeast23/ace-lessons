✍️ Writing Week 27: Cartoon Script: Write Your Own One-Minute Cartoon 🎬✏️

Yesterday you wrote like a scientist. Today, Ace, you'll write like a cartoon screenwriter. Before a single drawing is made, every cartoon starts as a script: a special kind of writing that tells the animators, the voice actors, and the sound team exactly what happens, who says what, and what the audience will hear. A script is a story, but it is written in a format that people can read like a plan, so a whole team can turn your words into moving pictures.

Part 1: The Lesson

A cartoon script has six building blocks 🧩

Professional scripts look a little different from stories, because each part has its own job.

1. Title + logline: a one-sentence summary of the whole story: who wants what, and what gets in the way
2. Character list: each character's name with a few words about their look and personality
3. Scene heading: where and when the scene happens, in capitals: EXT. CITY PARK - DAY (EXT. = outside, INT. = inside)
4. Action lines: what we see on screen, in the present tense, short and vivid
5. Dialogue: the character's name in capitals, then exactly what they say (and sometimes how, in brackets)
6. Sound effects + ending: sounds in capitals (CRASH!) and a strong ending: a punchline, a twist, or a problem solved

Five screenwriter's moves

  • Show, don't tell: instead of "Pip is sad," write what the animators can draw: "Pip's ears droop. A single tear rolls down his whiskers."
  • Use the present tense in action lines: "The cat leaps... the pie splats..." The audience is watching it happen now.
  • Think in pictures: name the camera shot when it matters: CLOSE-UP on a face for feelings, WIDE SHOT to show a big place.
  • Exaggerate: cartoons love squash and stretch, so write big, bouncy actions and funny surprises.
  • Every line of dialogue should do a job: show who the character is, move the story forward, or get a laugh. Keep it short; cartoons move fast!

Grown-up words: the main character is the protagonist, whoever or whatever blocks them is the antagonist, and the moment of greatest tension is the climax. The drawings that plan each shot are called a storyboard.

Model Cartoon Script (short version)

Title, logline + charactersScene heading + actionDialogue + sound effectsEnding

THE LAST PANCAKE
Logline: A hungry squirrel named Pip must outsmart a sneaky seagull to win the last pancake at a park picnic.
Characters: PIP, a tiny squirrel with a huge, fluffy tail and bigger dreams. GULLY, a seagull who thinks every snack belongs to him.
EXT. CITY PARK - DAY
WIDE SHOT: a picnic blanket. One golden pancake sits on a plate. Pip peeks out from behind a tree, his eyes growing as big as dinner plates. He tiptoes forward, then FREEZES. A shadow sweeps across the grass.
SOUND: FLAP! FLAP! SCREECH!
GULLY: (landing with a thud) Mine, mine, MINE!
PIP: (folding his arms) Not today, bird brain.
Pip yanks the blanket like a magician. The plate flies up. Gully grabs at the air and misses. CLOSE-UP: the pancake lands, SPLAT, right on Pip's head like a hat. Pip grins and takes a bite. FADE OUT.

Notice: the logline tells us who wants what and what's in the way; the scene heading is in capitals; the action lines are in the present tense and describe things an artist can draw ("eyes growing as big as dinner plates"); the dialogue shows personality in just a few words; the sound effects are in capitals; and the ending is a funny visual surprise.

Part 4: Your Turn to Write

Step 1: Choose your cartoon

Your cartoon should be about one minute long on screen. That's short, so pick one problem, two or three characters, and one or two places.

Plan your script: jot a few words for each box. (These notes save with your writing.)

Step 2: Write your script

Goal: 250 to 350 words with all six building blocks. Write a title and a one-sentence logline, a character list, at least one scene heading in capitals, action lines in the present tense, at least six lines of dialogue with the speaker's name in capitals, at least three sound effects, at least one camera shot (CLOSE-UP or WIDE SHOT), and an ending that ties up the problem. Show, don't tell!
