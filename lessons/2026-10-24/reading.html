Making Pictures Move: The Art and Science of Animation

Grab a pad of sticky notes and a pencil. On the bottom note, draw a stick figure with its arms down. On the next note up, draw the same figure with its arms raised a little higher, and keep going until the arms are high in the air. Now flip the pad quickly with your thumb. The figure waves! Nothing on the paper actually moved, yet you saw motion. That small trick is the secret behind every cartoon ever made. Animation is the art of creating the illusion of movement from pictures that are perfectly still.

Why does it work? When pictures flash by faster than about ten or twelve per second, your brain stops seeing them as separate images. Instead, it fills in the gaps and connects them into one smooth action. For many years, people called this "persistence of vision" and believed images simply lingered in the eye. Today, scientists think the brain itself does most of the work, constantly guessing how things move from one moment to the next. Either way, the result is the same: show a sequence of slightly different pictures fast enough, and they come to life. Movies usually show 24 pictures, called frames, every second.

People were playing with this effect long before television. In the 1830s, inventors built spinning toys such as the zoetrope, a drum with slits in its sides and a strip of drawings inside. When you spun the drum and peeked through the slits, a horse galloped or an acrobat flipped. In the 1860s, the first flipbooks went on sale. By the early 1900s, artists were drawing short cartoons for movie theatres, and audiences were amazed to see drawings walk, dance, and tell jokes on a giant screen.

Making a hand-drawn cartoon took enormous patience. A lead animator drew the most important poses, called keyframes, such as a character crouching before a jump and landing after it. Assistants then drew all the in-between pictures that connected those poses. Each finished drawing was traced onto a clear plastic sheet called a cel and painted by hand. Because the cels were see-through, they could be laid on top of one painted background, so the scenery did not have to be redrawn thousands of times. Hundreds of artists had to collaborate, and the work was so meticulous that a single feature film could take several years to finish.

Good animators are not just careful copiers; they are actors with pencils. Over the years, they discovered tricks that make drawings feel alive. One technique is called squash and stretch: a bouncing ball flattens when it hits the ground and stretches long as it falls fast, which makes it seem rubbery and full of energy. Animators also exaggerate movements on purpose. A surprised character's eyes might pop out like saucers, and a running character's legs might spin into a blur. Real people don't move like that, but on screen, the bigger action looks more convincing, not less.

Not all animation is drawn. In stop-motion, artists build puppets out of clay, fabric, or wire, then move them a tiny bit, take a photo, move them again, and take another photo. Twenty seconds of film can take a whole day. Canada has a proud history here, too. At the National Film Board of Canada, an artist named Norman McLaren became famous for an innovative idea: he skipped the camera altogether and scratched and painted his pictures directly onto strips of film. His experiments inspired animators around the world.

Today, many cartoons are made on computers. Artists build characters as three-dimensional models with hidden digital skeletons, then pose them like puppets. The computer can calculate some of the in-between frames, but people still decide every important pose, expression, and joke. The tools keep changing, from paper and cels to clay and computers, but the basic idea has stayed the same for almost two hundred years: many still pictures, shown one after another, can make an audience laugh, cheer, and believe that drawings are alive.
