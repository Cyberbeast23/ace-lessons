Sky Dancers: How the Northern Lights Work

On a clear winter night in Yellowknife, the Yukon, or northern British Columbia, the sky can suddenly fill with green and purple curtains that ripple like silk. People call them the northern lights, or the aurora borealis. For thousands of years, storytellers have invented myths to explain the glow — dancing spirits, reflections from ice, or fires in the sky. Today scientists know the real story is even more amazing: it begins on the Sun.

The Sun is a giant ball of hot plasma, a gas so energetic that electrons have been stripped from atoms. From its outer atmosphere, called the corona, a stream of charged particles rushes outward in every direction. That stream is the solar wind. It usually travels at hundreds of kilometres per second and can reach Earth in only a few days. When the Sun throws off an extra burst during a solar storm, the wind becomes denser and faster — and auroras on Earth become brighter and more active.

Earth is not helpless against this shower of particles. Deep inside our planet, swirling liquid iron creates a magnetic field. That field stretches into space and wraps around Earth like an invisible bubble called the magnetosphere. Most of the solar wind is deflected around the bubble, which is lucky for living things: without that shield, high-energy particles could strip away our atmosphere over long stretches of time. Near the north and south magnetic poles, though, some particles are guided down along magnetic field lines into the upper air.

About 100 kilometres above the ground, in a layer called the ionosphere, those incoming particles slam into oxygen and nitrogen gases. The gases briefly store the energy, then release it as light — a kind of natural luminescence. Oxygen often glows green or red; nitrogen can add blue or purple edges. From the ground the lights look like curtains, arcs, or swirling ribbons, because the magnetic field lines themselves are curved, and the glowing gases follow those paths.

Canada is one of the best countries on Earth for aurora watching. The "auroral oval" — a ring of frequent lights around the magnetic pole — often sits over the Northwest Territories, Yukon, Nunavut, northern Manitoba and parts of northern British Columbia and Alberta. Towns such as Yellowknife and Whitehorse advertise clear, cold nights and open skies. Farther south, people may see a faint glow only during strong geomagnetic storms, when the oval expands.

Myths and science can live side by side. Indigenous peoples across the North have rich aurora stories that carry culture, respect and wonder. Science does not erase those stories; it adds another layer: measurements from satellites, magnetometers on the ground, and cameras that capture the fluorescence of oxygen atoms. The next time you see a photo of green light dancing over a frozen lake, remember the chain: a restless Sun, a racing solar wind, Earth's magnetic shield, and gases high above Canada turning invisible energy into a celestial show.
