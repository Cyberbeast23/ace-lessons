✍️ Writing Week 47: Technical Explanation with a Flow Chart — How Clean Water Gets to Your Tap 🚰📐

Yesterday you told a true science journey as a story. Today, Ace, you'll write like a real engineer in a brand-new genre: a technical explanation with a flow chart. Engineers, scientists and city water departments use this kind of writing to explain how a system works: what happens at each stage, in what order, and why each stage is needed. It is not a how-to guide that tells the reader what to do, and it is not a story. It is a clear, precise explanation, backed up by a diagram, that someone could use to understand (or even fix!) the system.

Part 1: The Lesson

The six parts of a strong technical explanation 📐

A technical explanation answers two big questions: How does it work? and Why is each step there? The flow chart shows the "how" at a glance; the paragraphs explain the details and the "why."

1. Title + overview: a clear title and one or two sentences that say what the whole system does
2. Flow chart: boxes connected by arrows (→) that show every stage in order, with short labels
3. Step explanation: one sentence or more for each stage, using sequence words (First, Next, Then, After that, Finally)
4. Why it's needed: the purpose of each stage, using cause-and-effect words (because, so, in order to, which prevents)
5. Precise data + units: exact numbers with units (1.8 billion L a day, 24 filters, 1 m³ = 1 000 L) instead of "lots"
6. Conclusion + significance: a summary of how the stages work together and why the system matters

Four tricks technical writers use

  • Define technical words right away: use an appositive, a phrase between commas that explains a word: "Floc, fluffy clumps of dirt, sinks to the bottom."
  • Match the flow chart and the text: every box in the chart should appear in the paragraphs, in the same order, with the same name.
  • Be precise, not chatty: ❌ "Then some stuff gets added and it's really cool." ✅ "Next, a coagulant is added, which makes tiny particles sticky."
  • Use the present tense: a system works the same way every day, so write flows, traps, kills, not flowed, trapped, killed.

Technical writing rule: a reader who has never seen a treatment plant should be able to redraw your flow chart from your words alone.

Mini-lesson: writing like an engineer 🔧

Passive voice (when the "who" doesn't matter): technical writers often focus on what happens, not who does it. Active: Operators add chlorine. Passive: Chlorine is added. Form it with is/are + past participle: is added, are trapped, is stored. Use it sometimes, not in every sentence!

Appositives need two commas (unless they end the sentence): The reservoir, a human-made lake, stores water for summer.

Subject-verb agreement with a phrase in the middle: find the real subject. The water in the pipes flows downhill. (The subject is water, not pipes.)

Commas after sequence words: Next, the water trickles through sand. Canadian spelling: litre, metre, centre, neighbourhood, colour.

Model technical explanation (about 210 words)

How Rain Becomes Tap Water
Clean tap water is produced by a system that collects, treats and delivers water. In Metro Vancouver, the process begins in protected mountain watersheds and ends at your kitchen tap.
Watershed→Reservoir→Coagulation→Flocculation→Filtration→UV disinfection→Chlorine→Water mains→Your tap
First, rain and snowmelt drain into reservoirs, human-made lakes that store water for dry summers. Next, at the treatment plant, a coagulant is added so that tiny particles become sticky. Then, during flocculation, slow paddles gather the particles into floc, fluffy clumps of dirt. After that, the water trickles through deep filters of sand and crushed coal, which trap the floc. Disinfection is the most important stage because germs that slip through could make people sick. Ultraviolet light damages the germs' genetic material, so they cannot multiply. Finally, a small amount of chlorine is added in order to protect the water during its trip through the water mains. The Seymour-Capilano Filtration Plant can treat up to 1.8 billion litres a day. Each stage removes a different problem, so the stages work together like a team of guards. Because of this system, more than three million people can drink safely from the tap.

Notice: the overview says what the whole system does; every box in the flow chart appears in the paragraphs in the same order; technical words (reservoirs, floc) are defined with appositives; because, so, in order to explain why each stage is needed; numbers have units; the writer uses the present tense, with a few passive sentences (is added); the conclusion explains why the system matters.
