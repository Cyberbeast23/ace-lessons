From Mountain to Tap: The Amazing Engineering of Clean Water

Turn on the tap, fill a glass, and take a sip. It takes about five seconds. Yet the water in that glass may have started its journey months earlier, as a snowflake landing on a mountainside. Between the mountain and your mouth stands one of the most important engineering systems ever built: a network that collects, cleans, tests and delivers safe drinking water every hour of every day. Most of the time, nobody even notices it is there.

In Metro Vancouver, where more than three million people live, the story begins high in the Coast Mountains. Rain and melting snow trickle down steep, forested slopes into three protected areas: the Capilano, Seymour and Coquitlam watersheds. A watershed is all the land that drains into the same river, lake or reservoir, so whatever happens on that land ends up in the water. That is why these watersheds are closed to the public. There are no houses, no farms, no factories and no hiking trails inside them. Keeping people out is the first line of defence, because it is far easier to keep water clean than to clean it after it becomes dirty.

Concrete dams block the rivers and hold the water back, forming reservoirs, large human-made lakes that store water until it is needed. Metro Vancouver's reservoirs fill up mostly in the fall and winter, when rain pours down for weeks. The stored water then has to last through the long, dry days of summer, when people drink more, water gardens and fill pools. This explains why cities sometimes limit lawn watering in July and August: the reservoirs are not being refilled as fast as people are emptying them.

Even sparkling mountain water is not ready to drink. It carries tiny particles of soil and rotting leaves, plus microscopic organisms that can make people sick. So the water travels to a treatment plant, where it is cleaned in a series of steps. Many treatment plants follow a similar recipe. First, during coagulation, operators add a chemical that makes the tiny particles sticky. Next, during flocculation, slow paddles stir the water gently so the sticky particles bump into each other and grow into fluffy clumps called floc. In many plants, the water then rests in huge, calm tanks for sedimentation, where the heavy floc sinks to the bottom. After that comes filtration: the water trickles through deep beds of sand and crushed coal that trap the smaller bits left behind.

The last and most important step is to disinfect the water, which means killing or switching off any germs that slipped through. Some plants shine ultraviolet (UV) light through the water, which damages the germs' genetic material so they cannot multiply. Others add chlorine, or use both. A small amount of chlorine is left in the water on purpose, so it keeps protecting the water during its long trip through the pipes. Metro Vancouver's Seymour-Capilano Filtration Plant, which opened in 2009, is the largest drinking-water filtration plant in Canada and can treat up to 1.8 billion litres a day. Interestingly, it skips the big settling tanks: after flocculation, the floc goes straight to the filters, a design called direct filtration. Then come UV lamps and chlorine. Coquitlam's water, which is usually clearer, is cleaned at a different plant using ozone, UV light and chlorine. Engineers design each plant to match the water it receives.

Clean water is useless if it cannot reach you. From the plants, it flows through hundreds of kilometres of large pipes called water mains, then through each city's smaller pipes, until it arrives at your kitchen tap. Because the reservoirs sit high in the mountains, gravity, the force that pulls everything downward, does much of the work. The weight of water sitting higher up creates pressure that pushes water through the pipes, even uphill for short distances. Pumps help where the land is hilly or the buildings are tall. All along the way, workers collect water samples and test them in laboratories for bacteria, chlorine levels and cloudiness, so problems can be caught early.

What happens after you flush the toilet or pull the plug in the bathtub? That used water, called wastewater, travels through a completely separate set of pipes, the sewers, to a wastewater treatment plant. There, screens remove large objects, solids settle out, and helpful bacteria eat much of the remaining waste before the cleaned water is released into a river or the ocean. Drinking-water pipes and sewer pipes must never mix.

Why do cities go to all this trouble? History gives a grim answer. In 1854, a terrifying outbreak of cholera struck the Soho neighbourhood of London, England, killing hundreds of people in a few weeks. At the time, most people believed diseases spread through foul-smelling air. A doctor named John Snow had a different idea. He made a map marking where the victims had lived and noticed that the deaths clustered around a public water pump on Broad Street. Snow convinced local officials to remove the pump's handle so nobody could use it, and new cases soon dwindled, although the outbreak may already have been fading. Later investigators found that a leaking cesspit (a pit for human waste) near the well had contaminated the water. Snow's detective work helped convince the world that dirty water can spread deadly disease.

Sadly, safe tap water is still not guaranteed for everyone in Canada. For years, a number of First Nations communities have lived under long-term drinking water advisories, official warnings that tell people to boil their water before using it, or not to drink it at all. In some communities, these warnings have lasted for more than a decade. The causes include treatment plants that were too old or too small, remote locations that are hard to reach, and a lack of funding and support for training local water operators. Since 2015, many long-term advisories have been lifted as First Nations and governments have built and upgraded water systems and trained more operators, but some communities are still waiting. Indigenous water protectors, such as Anishinaabe youth leader Autumn Peltier, who spoke at the United Nations when she was just 13, have reminded the world that clean water is a right that must be protected.

Next time you fill a glass, think about its journey: a snowflake on a mountain, a dam, sticky floc, beds of sand, glowing UV lamps, kilometres of pipes, and the scientists who test every step. Clean water may look simple, but it is an engineering marvel, and it is worth protecting for everyone.
