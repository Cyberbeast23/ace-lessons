🚰 Math Week 47: From Mountain to Tap: Water Engineer's Math Lab

Ace, every time you turn on the tap, you're using one of the greatest engineering systems ever built. Rain and snow fall on protected mountain watersheds, collect in reservoirs, get cleaned at a treatment plant, and then travel through hundreds of kilometres of pipes, mostly pushed along by gravity. Today you are a water engineer. You'll use unit rates, volumes of rectangular prisms and cylinders, converting cubic metres to litres, flow rates in L/s and ML/day, percentages, chlorine dosing in mg/L, integers below sea level, map scales, mean vs median, a two-step equation for a water bill, ratios, and a multi-step capacity problem.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your water engineer's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units (m, cm, km, m³, L, mL, ML, mg/L, kg, s, min, h, $, %).
  2. Plan: Do the units match? Change metres to centimetres or hours to minutes before you answer.
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? A family might use hundreds of litres a day, but not hundreds of megalitres!

Worked Example 1: Volume and litres

Rectangular prism: V = length × width × height. Cylinder: V = π × r × r × height (use π ≈ 3.14; r is the radius, half the diameter).

1 m³ = 1 000 L. A tank 5 m × 4 m × 2 m = 40 m³ = 40 × 1 000 = 40 000 L. A cylinder with r = 2 m and height 3 m: 3.14 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 37.68 m³.

Worked Example 2: Big water units

Water engineers measure huge amounts in megalitres (ML). 1 ML = 1 000 000 L (one million litres, about the water in a 1 000 m³ tank).

There are 60 × 60 × 24 = 86 400 seconds in a day. A pipe flowing at 10 L/s carries 10 × 86 400 = 864 000 L = 0.864 ML per day.

Worked Example 3: Dosing in mg/L

A dose of 1 mg/L means 1 milligram of chemical in every litre. Multiply the dose by the number of litres to get the total.

0.5 mg/L in 400 000 L: 0.5 × 400 000 = 200 000 mg. Since 1 000 mg = 1 g, that's 200 g, and 1 000 g = 1 kg, so 0.2 kg.

Worked Example 4: Integers above and below sea level

Heights above sea level are positive; depths below sea level are negative. The distance between two heights = higher − lower.

From +90 m down to −20 m: 90 − (−20) = 90 + 20 = 110 m. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding!

Worked Example 5: Map scales

A scale of 1 : 50 000 means 1 cm on the map stands for 50 000 cm in real life. To grow a map length into a real length, multiply.

6 cm × 50 000 = 300 000 cm. Divide by 100 to get metres (3 000 m), then by 1 000 to get kilometres: 3 km.

Worked Example 6: Percent of a total, and percent increase

Percent = part ÷ whole × 100. 30 out of 200 = 0.15 = 15%.

Increase by a percent: to make 500 bigger by 20%, multiply by 1.20: 500 × 1.20 = 600.

Worked Example 7: Mean vs median

The mean is the total ÷ how many. The median is the middle value once you put the numbers in order.

100, 120, 300: mean = 520 ÷ 3 ≈ 173, but the median is 120. One unusually big day (like filling a paddling pool) pulls the mean up but barely moves the median.

Worked Example 8: Two-step equations

Undo the steps in reverse order: first undo adding (subtract), then undo multiplying (divide).

2x + 15 = 51 → 2x = 36 → x = 18. Check: 2 × 18 + 15 = 51 ✔
