Rivers of Gold: The Rush That Made British Columbia

In the spring of 1858, a ship arrived in San Francisco carrying a few hundred ounces of gold from a faraway river in the North. Within weeks, the news had spread like wildfire. Thousands of people sold their belongings, crowded onto steamships, and sailed for a place most of them had never heard of: the Fraser River. That summer, the sleepy fur-trading post of Victoria, which had only a few hundred residents, was suddenly swamped by around 30 000 newcomers. Tents covered every field. Shops sold out of picks, pans, and flour. The British Columbia gold rush had begun, and it was a true frenzy.

The gold itself was not a new discovery to everyone. The Nlaka'pamux and Secwépemc peoples, whose lands surrounded the Fraser and Thompson Rivers, had known about the shining flakes for years and had traded gold to the Hudson's Bay Company. The miners who poured in did not ask permission to dig on these lands. They cut down trees, muddied the salmon streams that families depended on, and sometimes treated the people who lived there with violence. In the Fraser Canyon in 1858, the tension exploded into armed conflict before leaders on both sides agreed to make peace. For First Nations, the gold rush was not an adventure. It was an invasion that changed their lives forever.

The rush also worried the British government. James Douglas, the governor of the small colony on Vancouver Island, feared that so many American miners might lead the United States to claim the mainland. To make sure the territory stayed British, Britain quickly created a brand-new colony. On November 19, 1858, at Fort Langley, Douglas was sworn in as the first governor of the Colony of British Columbia. In a very real way, the gold rush gave British Columbia its name and its first government.

As the easy gold along the lower Fraser ran out, miners pushed hundreds of kilometres north into the rugged Cariboo Mountains. Their work was exhausting toil. They swirled gravel in pans, shovelled it through long wooden sluice boxes, and dug deep shafts by hand, often standing in icy water. In August 1862, a sailor from England named Billy Barker and his partners had dug a shaft about 16 metres deep on Williams Creek, when they hit gravel full of gold. A busy town of saloons, stores, and boardwalks sprang up beside the claim, and it was named Barkerville. Yet most miners never struck it rich. Prices in the gold fields were sky-high, because every sack of flour had to travel hundreds of kilometres on mule trains, and many miners went home poorer than when they arrived. Often the shopkeepers who sold supplies ran the most lucrative businesses of all.

Among the newcomers were thousands of Chinese miners, most of them Cantonese speakers from the province of Guangdong in southern China. They called North America Gam Saan, which means "Gold Mountain." Many patiently reworked claims that others had abandoned, finding gold that impatient miners had missed. Others opened stores, restaurants, and laundries, and Barkerville grew one of the largest Chinatowns in the colony. Chinese miners faced unfair laws, special taxes, and discrimination, yet they built strong communities that helped one another through hard times.

To carry people and supplies north, the colony built the Cariboo Wagon Road, which stretched about 640 kilometres from Yale to Barkerville. Workers blasted parts of it out of steep canyon walls. One businessman even brought in about two dozen camels to haul freight, but their soft feet were hurt by the sharp rocks, and they frightened the horses and mules, so the experiment failed. Then, on September 16, 1868, a fire roared through Barkerville and burned almost the entire town to the ground in about an hour. The townspeople proved resilient. Within weeks, they had begun rebuilding, this time with wider streets.

The gold rush lasted only a few years, but its legacy is everywhere. It brought roads, towns, and a government to the mainland, and in 1871 British Columbia joined Canada. Barkerville flourished for a while, then slowly emptied as the gold ran out. Today it is a heritage town where visitors can pan for gold, walk the boardwalks, and learn the stories of the miners, the First Nations, and the Chinese pioneers who all shaped the history of this province.
