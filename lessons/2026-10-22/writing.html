✍️ Writing Week 25: Historical Journal: My Gold Rush Diary ⛏️📓

Yesterday you wrote step-by-step instructions like an engineer. Today, Ace, you'll travel back in time and write like someone who was really there: a historical journal (a diary). A historical journal is written in the first person ("I", "we") by an imaginary person living through real events. The facts must be true to history, but the thoughts, feelings, and small daily moments come from your imagination. Your diarist will live through the Cariboo Gold Rush of the 1860s.

Part 1: The Lesson

A historical journal entry has six parts 🧩

Think of a real diary found in an old trunk: each entry starts with a date, tells what happened that day, and lets you hear the writer's private thoughts.

1. Heading: the date and the place, like August 24, 1862, Williams Creek
2. Opening: a greeting to the diary ("Dear Diary") or a first sentence that drops the reader right into the day's big event
3. Sensory details: what the writer sees, hears, smells, tastes, and feels, so the reader is there
4. Historical facts: true details (places, dates, tools, prices, events) woven naturally into the story
5. Thoughts + feelings: the writer's hopes, worries, and opinions (the inner voice)
6. Closing: a reflection or a look ahead to tomorrow, and a sign-off

Five diarist's moves

  • Stay in character: write in the first person (I, me, we) and mostly in the past tense for what happened today ("We dug until dark"). Your diarist doesn't know the future, so no "Barkerville will burn in 1868" in an 1862 entry!
  • Show, don't tell: instead of "I was cold," write "My fingers turned so numb in the creek water that I dropped my pan twice."
  • Weave in true facts: use real details from today's reading: the Fraser River, sluice boxes, a 16 m shaft, $40 flour, Gold Mountain (Gam Saan), the Cariboo Wagon Road. Facts make your diary believable.
  • Use time words to move through the day: at dawn, by noon, later that afternoon, after supper, tonight.
  • Give your diarist a voice: a personality, a goal (send money home? find a nugget?), and at least one strong feeling, like hope, homesickness, fear, or pride.

Grown-up words: the imaginary writer is the persona or narrator; writing from inside their head is first-person point of view. A diary based on real history is a kind of historical fiction, and a real diary from the past is a primary source.

Model Historical Journal Entry (short version)

Heading + openingSensory details + factsThoughts + feelingsClosing

August 24, 1862, Williams Creek, Colony of British Columbia. Dear Diary, today the whole creek went wild, because news spread that Billy Barker's crew has struck gold!
At dawn, the mist hung over the creek and smelled of wet pine and wood smoke. I was shovelling gravel into our sluice box when a shout echoed down the valley. Men splashed through the icy water toward Barker's shaft, which they say is nearly 16 m deep. By noon, I had held a nugget the size of my thumbnail, heavier than any stone I have ever touched. Flour now costs $40 a sack here, so a little gold does not go far.
My hands are blistered and my boots leak, but for the first time in weeks I feel hope. If our claim holds even a little of what Barker found, I can send money home to Mother before winter.
Tomorrow we dig deeper. Goodnight, Diary. — Thomas

Notice: the heading gives an exact date and place; the opening drops you into the big event; the senses (mist, pine smell, icy water, a heavy nugget) put you there; true facts (sluice box, 16 m shaft, $40 flour) are woven in naturally; the writer shares real feelings (hope, worry about Mother); and the closing looks ahead to tomorrow.
