⛏️ Math Week 25: The Cariboo Gold Rush Lab

Ace, in the 1850s and 1860s, tens of thousands of people rushed to British Columbia hoping to strike it rich. Every miner had to be a mathematician: How much is this nugget worth? How much gravel do I need to wash? Will I earn more than I spend? Today you'll work like a gold-rush assayer and shopkeeper with density (mass ÷ volume), unit conversions, money, percent increase, proportions and map scales, mean and median, perimeter, volume of a rectangular prism, sharing in a ratio, and solving equations.

Part 1: Learn the Moves

The 4-Step Problem Solver (your prospector's checklist):
  1. Read: What is the question actually asking? Underline the numbers and units.
  2. Plan: Which operations do I need, and in what order? Do the units match?
  3. Solve: Do one step at a time and write each step down.
  4. Check: Is my answer reasonable? Did I answer the question that was asked, in the right units?

Worked Example 1: Density

Density = mass ÷ volume. It tells you how much mass is packed into each cubic centimetre (cm³). Gold is one of the densest metals on Earth, which is why it sinks to the bottom of a miner's pan while sand and gravel wash away.

A lump of iron has a mass of 39 g and a volume of 5 cm³. Its density is 39 ÷ 5 = 7.8 g per cm³. Turn it around: mass = density × volume, so 2 cm³ of iron has a mass of 7.8 × 2 = 15.6 g.

Worked Example 2: Troy ounces and money

Gold is still weighed in troy ounces. One troy ounce is about 31.1 g. To change grams into troy ounces, divide by 31.1: 93.3 g ÷ 31.1 = 3 troy ounces.

To find the value, multiply: if gold sells for $17 per ounce, 3 ounces are worth 3 × 17 = $51.

Worked Example 3: Percent increase

Percent increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

A shovel costs $2 in Victoria but $5 in a gold-rush town. The increase is 5 − 2 = $3, and 3 ÷ 2 × 100 = 150%. (Notice: you always divide by the old price.)

Worked Example 4: Proportions and map scales

A proportion says two ratios are equal. If 2 pans of gravel give 0.5 g of gold, then 10 pans (5 times as many) give 0.5 × 5 = 2.5 g. Find the multiplier, then use it on both parts.

Map scale: a scale of 1 cm : 25 km means each centimetre on the map stands for 25 km in real life. A trail that measures 6 cm on the map is 6 × 25 = 150 km long.

Worked Example 5: Mean and median

Mean = total ÷ number of values. Median = the middle value once the numbers are put in order.

For 4, 10, 3, 7, 21: the total is 45, so the mean is 45 ÷ 5 = 9. In order: 3, 4, 7, 10, 21, so the median is 7. One very big value (21) pulls the mean up, but the median stays put.

Worked Example 6: Sharing in a ratio

Three partners share 30 g of gold in the ratio 3 : 2 : 1. Add the parts: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 parts. One part = 30 ÷ 6 = 5 g.

So the shares are 3 × 5 = 15 g, 2 × 5 = 10 g, and 1 × 5 = 5 g. (Check: 15 + 10 + 5 = 30 ✔)

Worked Example 7: Volume, perimeter, and equations

Volume of a rectangular prism = length × width × height. A box 3 m by 2 m by 4 m holds 3 × 2 × 4 = 24 m³.

Perimeter of a rectangle = 2 × (length + width). If the perimeter is 60 m and the length is 18 m, then 2 × (18 + w) = 60, so 18 + w = 30 and w = 12 m.

Solving an equation: "A number times 3, plus 5, is 26" becomes 3n + 5 = 26. Undo each step in reverse: subtract 5 (3n = 21), then divide by 3 (n = 7).
