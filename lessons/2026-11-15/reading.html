Star Stories: How Stars Are Born, Shine and Die

On a clear night far from city lights, you can see about 3 000 stars with your eyes alone. They look like tiny, calm sparks, as if someone had poked holes in a dark blanket. But every one of them is a gigantic, roaring ball of hot gas, and many are larger and brighter than our Sun. Stars are not eternal, either. Like living things, they have a life story: they are born, they spend a long "adulthood" shining, and then they die, sometimes quietly and sometimes in the biggest explosions in the universe.

A star's story begins inside a nebula, an enormous cloud of gas and dust drifting between the stars. Most of that gas is hydrogen, the simplest and most common element in the universe. A nebula can float peacefully for millions of years. Then something disturbs it, perhaps the shock wave from a nearby exploding star. Some regions of the cloud become slightly thicker than others, and gravity begins to pull. Gravity works a bit like a snowball rolling downhill: the more material a clump gathers, the stronger its pull becomes, so it gathers material even faster. Slowly, the clump begins to collapse, falling inward on itself and spinning faster and faster, the way a figure skater spins faster when she pulls in her arms.

As the gas squeezes together, it heats up. At the centre, or core, of the clump the temperature eventually climbs to about 10 million degrees Celsius. At that point something remarkable happens. Hydrogen atoms smash into one another so hard that they stick together, or fuse, forming a new element: helium. This process, called nuclear fusion, releases a tremendous amount of energy. A star has been born. You can think of fusion as the star's engine, but it is a strange engine: it burns no oxygen and produces no smoke. It turns a little bit of mass directly into light and heat. Every second, our Sun converts about 4 million tonnes of its matter into energy.

For most of its life, a star is locked in a tug-of-war. Gravity tries to crush the star inward. The heat and pressure from fusion push outward. When the two forces are balanced, the star stays steady, neither shrinking nor swelling. Astronomers call this long, stable stage the main sequence. Our Sun is about 4.6 billion years old and roughly halfway through its main sequence. Here is a surprise: the biggest stars have the shortest lives. A giant star has far more fuel, but it burns that fuel so fiercely that it may run out in only a few million years. A small red dwarf sips its hydrogen so slowly that it could keep shining for trillions of years, longer than the universe has existed so far.

You can even read a star's temperature from its colour. On a stove, a metal element glows red when it is warm and orange-yellow when it is hotter. Stars follow a similar pattern, except that the hottest ones glow blue-white. Cool red stars have surfaces near 3 000 °C, yellow stars like the Sun are about 5 500 °C, and blue stars can be hotter than 25 000 °C. Next time you look at the constellation Orion, compare reddish Betelgeuse at the hunter's shoulder with blue-white Rigel at his foot.

Eventually, every star runs low on hydrogen in its core, and the tug-of-war tips out of balance. What happens next depends on the star's mass. When a star like our Sun reaches old age, in about 5 billion years, its core will shrink and heat up while its outer layers expand enormously. It will become a red giant, perhaps wide enough to swallow Mercury and Venus. Later, it will gently puff its outer layers into space, forming a glowing shell of gas called a planetary nebula (a confusing name, since it has nothing to do with planets). Left behind will be a white dwarf: a hot core about the size of Earth but so dense that a single teaspoon of it would weigh about as much as an elephant. Over billions of years it will slowly cool and fade, like the embers of a campfire.

Giant stars, at least eight times the mass of the Sun, die far more dramatically. In their cores, fusion keeps building heavier and heavier elements: carbon, oxygen, silicon and finally iron. Iron is a dead end, because fusing it uses up energy instead of releasing it. Within seconds, the core collapses, and the star blows itself apart in a supernova. For a few weeks, a single supernova can outshine an entire galaxy of billions of stars. In the year 1054, astronomers in China recorded a "guest star" bright enough to see in daylight for weeks. Today, telescopes pointed at that spot show the Crab Nebula, a tangled, glowing remnant of the explosion that is still spreading outward.

The core left behind by a supernova is even stranger than a white dwarf. It may become a neutron star, a ball only about 20 km across, roughly the size of a city, yet holding more mass than the Sun. If the leftover core is heavy enough, nothing can stop its collapse, and it becomes a black hole: a region where gravity is so strong that not even light can escape. A black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner roaming space to suck things up, though. If the Sun were magically replaced by a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting exactly as it does now; it would just become very cold and dark.

The most amazing part of the story is what it means for us. The first stars contained only hydrogen and helium. Almost every other element, including the carbon in your muscles, the oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood, was made inside stars and blasted into space when they died. Those scattered elements mixed into new nebulas, which formed new stars, planets and eventually people. The astronomer Carl Sagan put it this way: "We are made of star-stuff." When you look up at the night sky, you are looking at the cosmic factories that built you.

Part 2: Think Deeper (not scored)

The author compares a star to a tug-of-war (paragraph 4). In 3 to 5 sentences, explain what the two "teams" are, how the analogy helps you understand a star's life, and one way the analogy does not quite fit. Use at least two pieces of evidence from the passage.

Model answer: In the tug-of-war, one team is gravity, which tries to crush the star inward, and the other team is the heat and pressure from fusion, which push outward. The analogy helps because it shows why a star stays the same size for billions of years: when the two sides pull equally, nobody moves, just like the main sequence. It also explains what happens at the end, because when hydrogen runs low, the balance tips and the star changes, swelling into a red giant or collapsing in a supernova. The analogy does not fit perfectly, though, because in a real tug-of-war one team usually just wins and the game ends, but in a star the "losing" can cause a giant explosion and leave behind a white dwarf, neutron star or black hole.
