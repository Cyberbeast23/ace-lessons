Who Decides? How Canada's Government Works

Today, Saturday, October 17, 2026, is a big day in British Columbia. From 8 a.m. to 8 p.m., voters in cities, towns, and districts across the province are choosing the mayors, councillors, and school trustees who will serve for the next four years. Somewhere near you, a grown-up is stepping behind a cardboard screen, marking a paper ballot in secret, and sliding it into a sealed box. This simple act is the heart of a democracy: a system in which the people hold the power and choose leaders to make decisions for them. The word comes from two ancient Greek words, demos, "the people," and kratos, "power."

Canada is an enormous country, so its government is divided into three main levels, each responsible for different jobs. The federal government, which meets in Ottawa, handles matters that affect the whole country, such as national defence, citizenship and passports, the postal service, and our money. Each of the ten provinces and three territories also has its own government. BC's provincial government meets in Victoria and is responsible for schools, hospitals and health care, and most highways. Finally, every city and town has a municipal government, usually a council led by a mayor. Councils look after the things you see every day: local roads and sidewalks, parks and playgrounds, libraries, garbage and recycling pickup, clean drinking water, and fire halls.

Canada's federal law-making body is called Parliament. It has three parts: the King, who is represented in Canada by the Governor General; the Senate, whose 105 members are appointed rather than elected; and the House of Commons, whose 343 Members of Parliament (MPs) are elected by the people. The country is divided into 343 ridings, and the voters in each riding elect one MP to speak for them. In each riding, the candidate with the most votes wins, even if that person gets less than half of the votes. After an election, the party that wins the most seats usually forms the government, and its leader becomes the Prime Minister. If that party wins a majority, more than half of the seats, it can usually pass its plans on its own. If it wins only a minority, it must persuade MPs from other parties to support it. BC's Legislative Assembly works in a similar way: its 93 elected members are called MLAs, and the leader of the provincial government is called the Premier.

How does an idea become a federal law? It starts as a bill, a written proposal for a new law or a change to an old one. In the House of Commons, the bill is introduced at first reading. At second reading, MPs debate its main idea. Next, a smaller group of MPs called a committee studies the bill line by line, listens to experts and ordinary citizens, and may suggest amendments, or changes. After the committee reports back, MPs debate once more and hold a final vote at third reading. Then the Senate goes through the same steps, checking the bill a second time; people often call the Senate's job "sober second thought." Only when both houses have passed the same version does the Governor General give it Royal Assent, the final approval that turns a bill into a law. This slow process frustrates some people, but it is designed so that mistakes are caught before a law affects millions of Canadians.

Living in a democracy comes with both rights and responsibilities. The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, part of Canada's Constitution since 1982, protects freedoms such as expressing your opinions, practising your religion, and gathering peacefully, as well as the right of every citizen aged 18 or older to vote. In return, citizens have responsibilities: obeying the law, respecting the rights of others, serving on a jury if asked, helping their communities, and taking part in elections. A right is like a key that opens a door; a responsibility is the promise to use that key wisely.

You might think government is only for grown-ups, but that is not quite true. Council meetings are open to the public, and anyone, including a young person, can write a letter to a councillor or ask to speak at a public hearing. When a council plans a new park, crosswalk, or bike lane, it often asks residents for their opinions first. Students on a student council practise the same skills: listening to classmates, debating ideas, and voting fairly. Tonight's voters will decide who fills the council seats, but the leaders they choose will still need to hear from the whole community, including people who are too young to vote. The habits you build now, such as staying informed, asking good questions, and speaking up respectfully, are the habits of a good citizen.
