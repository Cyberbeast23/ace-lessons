Yesterday you wrote an argument essay for any reader. Today, Ace, you'll aim your argument at one real person who has the power to say yes: a city councillor. Today is general voting day for local elections across BC, so in a few weeks newly elected councillors will start making decisions about parks, roads, crosswalks, and libraries. A persuasive letter uses everything you know about claims, reasons, evidence, and rebuttals, and adds two new skills: writing for a specific audience and using the polite, clear format of a formal letter. Your goal is to convince your councillor to take one specific action.

Part 1: The Lesson

A persuasive letter has six parts 🧩

Think of it like making your case to a busy, important person who reads many letters every week:

1. Greeting + introduction: "Dear Councillor ___," then who you are and why you care
2. My request (claim): one clear sentence saying exactly what you want done
3. Reason 1 + evidence: your strongest reason, backed by a fact, count, or example
4. Reason 2 + evidence: a second, different reason with its own evidence
5. Answering a concern: "Some people may worry that… however…"
6. Call to action + closing: what you want them to do and when, a thank-you, then "Sincerely," and your name

Five persuader's moves

  • Know your audience: a councillor cares about the whole community, the budget, and safety. Explain how your idea helps many people, not just you.
  • Be specific: "Make the park better" is vague. "Replace the two broken swings at Riverside Park before summer" is a request someone can vote on.
  • Bring evidence: counts, measurements, a class survey, or a short true story. Numbers are hard to argue with!
  • Answer the "but…": think about what might stop the councillor from saying yes (cost, time, other needs) and answer it respectfully.
  • Stay polite and formal: no slang, no exclamation-mark storms, no insults. Respect makes readers want to help you.

Grown-up words: persuasive writers use three kinds of appeals, named by the ancient Greek thinker Aristotle: logos (logic: facts and numbers), pathos (emotion: a story that makes the reader care), and ethos (trust: showing you are informed, fair, and respectful). The best letters use all three.

Model Persuasive Letter (short version)

Greeting + introduction + requestReasons + evidenceAnswering a concernCall to action + closing

October 17, 2026
Dear Councillor Patel,
My name is Ace, and I am a Grade 5 student who walks to school along Maple Street every morning. I am writing to ask council to install a crosswalk with flashing lights at the corner of Maple Street and Fourth Avenue.
First, the corner is dangerous. Last week my class counted 214 cars passing in just 30 minutes before the morning bell, and many drivers did not slow down. Second, a safe crossing would help the whole neighbourhood. In our class survey, 18 of 26 students said they would walk or bike to school if the corner were safer, which would mean less traffic and cleaner air around the school.
Some people may worry that flashing lights cost too much. However, the cost is small compared with keeping children safe, and our parent group has offered to help raise money.
Please vote for a Maple Street crosswalk when council plans next year's budget. Thank you for taking the time to listen to young people in our community.
Sincerely,
Ace

Notice: the model names the exact place and action, uses logos (a car count and a survey), a touch of pathos (children's safety), and ethos (a polite tone and a fair answer to the cost concern), and ends with a clear call to action tied to a real decision: the budget.
